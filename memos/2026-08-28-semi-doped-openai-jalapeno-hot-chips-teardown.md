- ## Semi Doped: OpenAI's Jalapeño inference chip, unpacked from the Hot Chips talk
	- **Source**: *Semi Doped* podcast, recorded under 12 hours after OpenAI presented the chip at **Hot Chips**, discussing the talk slide-by-slide plus conversations with attendees and a companion SemiAnalysis article. OpenAI presenters: **Richard Ho** (ex-Google TPU, now head of hardware), **Ravi** (chip architect), **Chris Leary** (software co-design). Machine transcript garbles the codename throughout (Halepenio/Holopenia/HaLapeño); it is **Jalapeño**, and the hosts note the conference volunteers happened to be wearing chili-pepper shirts.
	- **Relationship to prior coverage**: [[2026-07-06-openai-broadcom-jalapeno-hn-cerebras-hbm-readthrough]] covered the **June announcement** via a Hacker News thread — pre-silicon, mostly speculation. **This is the first real technical disclosure**, and it both confirms and contradicts parts of that thread; the contradictions are flagged below.
	- **Thesis**: The chip is competitive on its **first generation**, which is the surprise. But the two things worth carrying forward are not benchmark numbers: (1) OpenAI is optimizing for **end-user experience and energy per request rather than TCO**, because unlike a merchant vendor its customer is the end user; and (2) it **rejects prefill/decode disaggregation as a GPU-specific workaround**, betting instead on one balanced chip with power-gated regions — *"dark silicon is cheaper than idle accelerators."* If that bet is right, it undercuts both the GPU+LPU pairing and the dedicated-decode-ASIC thesis at once.
- ## What the Hot Chips disclosure actually adds
	- | Item | Disclosed |
	  |----------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
	  | Process | **TSMC N3** (N3P/N3E variants discussed) |
	  | Memory | **HBM4**; Samsung supply **speculated** by the hosts, on better speed-per-pin than SK Hynix — explicitly "entirely speculative" |
	  | TDP | **700W**, versus **>1,200W** for GB200 |
	  | Host CPU | **x86, believed Turin-class** (AMD); Venice would improve it |
	  | Scale-up domain, tier 1 | **128 chips**, Broadcom **Tomahawk 6**, **600 Gb/s per chip** |
	  | Scale-up domain, tier 2 | **2,048 chips** (~16 racks) at **200 Gb/s**, over **ESUN** (not UALink) |
	  | Topology | described as "half-flat, two-level Clos" |
	  | Silicon composition | mostly **fresh RTL**; some interface IP on the compute die, plus a separate **IO chiplet** |
	  | Named partners | **Broadcom** and **Celestica** |
	  | Roadmap | **Gen 2 already approaching tapeout**; Gen 3 in planning |
	- **The most useful correction to the earlier memo is on the "nine months" claim, and it cuts *against* the podcast.** The Hot Chips figure is **~9 months from first RTL to tapeout**, which the hosts call "ridiculously short" against a 2–3 year norm. But the engineers in the HN thread recorded in [[2026-07-06-openai-broadcom-jalapeno-hn-cerebras-hbm-readthrough]] argued that **RTL-freeze-to-tapeout in nine months is typical-to-unimpressive** for a large 3nm chip; the impressive claim would be *concept* to tapeout. **The newly disclosed metric is the less impressive of the two readings**, and Richard Ho has taped out with Broadcom before, so this is not a cold start. Hold the nine-month story loosely — it is the single most load-bearing claim in the bull case and it is measured on the more flattering boundary.
	- The AI-assistance detail is new and concrete: **the initial RTL was developed with a GPT-3-class model**, improving as newer models landed. The hosts' own caveat is the important one — *"it's not like any of these companies don't already have a GPT-3-class model available."* Consistent with the earlier thread's consensus that LLMs genuinely help with **verification and testbenches** (verifiable feedback loops) and are weak on physical/backend design.
- ## Designing for the user, not the buyer — the structural point
	- OpenAI stated **two** design metrics: **user experience defined as time to *last* token** (end-to-end latency, not TTFT), and **energy per request**. Because the two trade off — you can always go faster by spending more energy — they committed to publishing **Pareto frontier curves rather than standalone numbers**.
	- **The observation worth keeping**: a merchant silicon vendor sells to hyperscalers and labs, so it designs for **TCO**. OpenAI is vertically integrated and its customer is the end user, so it designs for **experience**. Different customer, different chip.
	- **The honest pushback the hosts raise**: this may be a distinction without much difference. Jensen has argued that designing to make a chip cheaper to *manufacture* is a bad decision and that what matters is **tokens per joule** — and energy per request *is* tokens per joule, i.e. the real cost of ownership. Tokens/s/MW has been the y-axis on interactivity curves for years. **Treat "we optimize for experience, not cost" as a framing choice more than a mechanical one** — though the choice of *last*-token latency over first-token latency is a genuine and unusual commitment.
	- Design-process insight from Ravi that generalizes beyond this chip: at design time you weigh **marginal cost** (transistors and floor-plan area for one more feature) against **opportunity cost** (demand you cannot serve because the feature is absent). **Opportunity cost is generally much larger** — there is "a regret factor." Asked the hardest decision on the chip, his answer was **what to cut**.
- ## Benchmarks, and where they are thin
	- OpenAI benchmarked on SemiAnalysis's **InferenceX**, praising it as fair, public, and **power-normalized**, and for spanning multiple open-source models rather than one convenient one. See [[2026-08-09-semianalysis-tilert-inferencex-gpu-vs-dataflow-asic]] for what that platform measures.
	- | Result | Detail |
	  |------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|
	  | DeepSeek R1 671B | Sets a **new Pareto frontier** — higher throughput at iso-interactivity, and reaches further out in interactivity than GPUs |
	  | GPT-OSS 120B | **>1,000 tok/s/user** — what the hosts call **"SRAM territory," where GPUs cannot reach** |
	  | Model range demonstrated | GPT-OSS 120B through **Kimi K2.5** — deliberately different model shapes |
	  | Power | **700W** versus GB200's 1,200W+, so the advantage concentrates in **tokens per watt** |
	- **Three caveats, and they matter more than the headline**:
		- **Context length is small** — the benchmark is **8k input / 1k output**. Nobody has shown behaviour at ~1M input context, and the hosts explicitly say they were hunting for the cherry-pick and could not tell.
		- **No agentic workloads have been run.** SemiAnalysis's **AgentX** is still to come — the same gap flagged for TileRT. Given that Chris Leary's own architecture argument rests on KV-cache growth *in agentic inference*, the workload that motivates the design is the one not yet benchmarked.
		- **The HBM generation is not matched.** Jalapeño has **HBM4**; Blackwell and MI355X comparisons are **HBM3E**. The hosts concede the fair fight is against **Vera Rubin** and **Helios**, and that this is "much more a Rubin-class chip than a Blackwell-class chip." **Any "beats Blackwell" framing is comparing across a memory generation.**
- ## The architecture: why the HBM roofline is not being reached
	- Chris Leary's framing inverts the usual memory-wall story: bandwidth is a limiter, **but the ceiling is not being hit**. The arithmetic — 128 chips, roughly **1 PB/s** aggregate HBM4 bandwidth, a 1T-parameter model at FP4 weighing ~0.5 TB — implies **1,000–2,000 tok/s/user without speculative decoding and 5,000–10,000 with it**. Actual performance is nowhere near.
	- **The diagnosis is "the operands arrive late."** Data is not in the registers when needed, because unified memory subsystems create contention: HBM is shared with neighbours across the scale-up network, copies proliferate, and transit stalls both compute and memory. Hence: **"the show flops don't matter, it's how many flops you actually deliver."**
	- **The fix is a NUMA-style memory architecture**: each accelerator gets its own **local HBM slice** on a dedicated low-latency, high-efficiency bus, eliminating contention. Above that sits a fast collective network, and above that a general NoC for flexibility. The cost is a harder mapping problem — deciding what data belongs where — which they judged worth solving.
	- **The read-through for memory suppliers is genuinely two-sided and mostly unremarked.** If the industry is not using the bandwidth it already buys, then the answer may be *architecture* rather than another HBM generation. The hosts put it directly: do we need to make it faster every single year, or take advantage of what we have? That is a demand-side question for HBM roadmaps (compare [[2026-08-21-sk-hynix-hbm-history-mr-muf-moat]]), and it rhymes with the observation in [[2026-08-23-beren-architecture-research-as-scaling-bottlenecks]] that **bandwidth rises 2–3× per generation while latency stays flat** — this design attacks latency and locality, not bandwidth.
- ## The contrarian bet: don't move the KV cache, and don't disaggregate
	- Leary's position, which contradicts current consensus: in agentic inference the **largest and fastest-growing data structure is the KV cache**, and **"we do not believe that the long-term solution involves moving large KV states."**
	- He characterizes **disaggregated prefill as a GPU-specific workaround** — GPUs need large batches to make sampling efficient, which forces many prefill replicas to feed a sampling replica, and the price of losing KV locality is **replicating weights in every HBM**.
	- **This is a direct collision with the architecture in [[2026-08-09-semianalysis-tilert-inferencex-gpu-vs-dataflow-asic]]**, where PD disaggregation is treated as the enabling design — one vLLM prefill pool feeding separate decode pools, KV cache shipped over RDMA. **Both cannot be the long-run answer.** The distinction that may reconcile them: disaggregation is a *scheduling* solution appropriate to fungible GPU fleets, while OpenAI is designing the *hardware* so the problem does not arise. Worth tracking as an explicit open question — the two views are testable against each other on long-context agentic workloads.
	- **"Dark silicon is cheaper than idle accelerators."** The workload mix — prefill versus drafting versus verification — shifts constantly by model and request, and speculative decoding has knobs (a smarter draft model emits fewer tokens to verify; a weaker one sprays more). GPU fleets handle this by dedicating some racks to prefill and others to decode, which leaves capacity idle when the ratio moves. OpenAI's answer is **one balanced chip** with enough compute, bandwidth, and IO for any phase, **power-gating whatever is unused**.
		- **This is the fungibility argument from the TileRT memo, solved in hardware rather than in the scheduler** — and it is aimed squarely at the pairing of a Rubin-class GPU with a Groq LPU for decode. The hosts raise the sharpest version: what is the **useful life** of a decode-only LPU if workload shape changes? A balanced chip keeps earning after Gen 2 ships; a fixed-function decode part may not.
- ## Supply chain and who benefits
	- **Broadcom** is the clearest beneficiary and appears at three layers: ASIC design-house work, likely **interface IP and the IO chiplet**, and the **Tomahawk 6** switches carrying both scale-up tiers. Note the protocol choice is **ESUN**, which Broadcom spearheaded — **not UALink**.
	- **Celestica** as the system/ODM partner for the rack, analogous to the rack-design capability AMD acquired for Helios.
	- **TSMC N3** for the die; an **x86 Turin-class** host CPU, which is an AMD data point; **HBM4**, with Samsung supply speculated but unconfirmed.
	- **The framing to hold**: if one of the two largest compute buyers shifts a growing share of its gigawatts onto its own silicon, the beneficiaries are the ASIC design house, the switch vendor, the ODM, the foundry, and the memory maker — **and the party that loses share is the merchant GPU vendor**, not the supply chain beneath it.
	- Also worth noting: **scale-up is not "within the rack."** This design's scale-up domain spans **16 racks**; scale-up is defined by accelerators sharing memory, not by physical enclosure.
- ## The nine-month question is the real investment content
	- The hosts' framing, and the reason this episode matters beyond one chip: *"if somebody can come in and generate a chip that's better than Blackwell in under a year, the question is where does acceleration take us now? How much better of a chip will a company with the right AI tools and the right people be able to design given two years? And that will dictate how money should flow — like who should be funded."*
	- **The comparison that sharpens it**: AI-ASIC startups staffed with ex-Google TPU people had the know-how but not the AI tooling. The new cohort has both. If design cycles genuinely compress, **the barrier to entry for competent inference silicon falls**, which is bullish for **EDA consumption** (more designs, more tool-seats) even as it is bearish for any single incumbent's architectural lead.
	- **The counterarguments the hosts concede, and they are the strong ones**: taping out and ramping still costs on the order of **\$100M+**; and NVIDIA can fairly ask *let's see you manufacture at scale, ship at scale, and prove reliability at scale.* First silicon that benchmarks well is not a supply chain. The earlier HN thread's skepticism about the nine-month boundary applies here too.
	- A speculation worth watching: whether NVIDIA runs a **blank-sheet skunkworks XPU** unburdened by CUDA and legacy compatibility. Richard Ho credited the **blank sheet** itself — no legacy software to accommodate — as a major reason for the speed, which is precisely the freedom an incumbent lacks.
- ## Will OpenAI sell it — and the tension in showing generality
	- The chip is a **generalized LLM inference** part — not training, not CNNs, but broad across model shapes, which OpenAI demonstrated deliberately with GPT-OSS through Kimi K2.5.
	- **The unresolved tension the hosts surface**: if the advantage of vertical integration is co-designing for your *own* models — and OpenAI uniquely knows how its tokens are actually consumed — then extreme co-design could push toward Cerebras-class performance. **So why demonstrate generality at all, unless you intend to sell it?** The counter they land on is that generality also lets the chip host **future** OpenAI models whose shapes are not yet known.
	- **The commercial paths floated**, in rising order of competitive risk: keep it captive to compete on margin and speed against Anthropic and Google; sell or rent to **enterprises** for on-prem; place capacity with **neoclouds** who resell it; or offer **"OpenAI inference as a service, cheap version"** — explicitly modelled on AWS running its own models ~30% cheaper on Trainium by removing GPU middlemen margins.
	- The analogy offered is **Intel Foundry**: captive is preferable until the fixed cost of each generation forces amortization across external customers. Gen 2 is already near tapeout, so this decision arrives sooner than it looks.
	- **The open competitive question the episode ends on**: Anthropic has a hardware team too, and no chip has surfaced.
