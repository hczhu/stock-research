- ## Beren Millidge: architecture research is bottleneck removal, not loss chasing
	- **Source**: [Architecture Research as Addressing Constraints to Scaling](https://www.beren.io/2026-08-23-Architecture-Research-as-Addressing-Constraints-to-Scaling/), Beren Millidge, August 23, 2026 — a short note extending [Scaling is Subtler than it Seems](https://www.beren.io/2026-08-15-Scaling-is-Subtler-than-it-Seems/), covered in [[2026-08-15-beren-scaling-is-subtler-than-it-seems]] — that post enumerates the five constraints on scaling, this one treats architecture research as the work of relaxing them. Conceptual essay by a researcher, not sell-side work: no market claims, no company analysis. The value is a **framework for reading model-architecture news**, plus one footnote with a large hardware read-through.
	- **Thesis**: *"The point of architecture research is not principally to improve pretraining loss under some fixed iso-param and iso-flop conditions but rather to remove, or at least push back, the bottlenecks to further scaling."* Scaling is **a regime, not a primitive** — clean scaling laws appear only once every bottleneck preventing further scaling has been removed, after which network generality converts compute into *"amortized compressions of increasingly rare features of the training dataset."* The investable consequence: **architecture progress should be scored on which constraint it relaxes and which hardware resource it spends, not on benchmark deltas** — and the resource it spends is what shows up in the bill of materials.
- ## Why the standard evaluation frame misleads
	- The common view is that architecture research is empirical dart-throwing — add a layernorm, swap an activation, shave some perplexity. Millidge's counter is that the historically decisive innovations were **not** loss improvements at fixed scale; they were removals of hard walls.
	- **The canonical case**: RNNs/LSTMs had to run sequentially forward for outputs and backward for gradients — bad for GPUs, but more fundamentally subject to **vanishing gradients across time**, which capped modellable sequence length and therefore how much of natural language was expressible at all. Attention removed **two** bottlenecks at once: the fixed-size recurrent state, and signal propagation forward and backward through time. The transformer then coupled attention to a residual stream and MLPs. **Attention enabled scaling in sequence length; residuals enabled scaling in depth.** Before that combination *"it was extremely difficult to meaningfully scale at all"* — which is why scaling laws only became widely understood some years *after* the transformer.
	- **The best single argument for the whole thesis is QK norm**, which *"does not really have much impact on the pretraining loss at small scales but is vital for stable training at scale."* An iso-flop small-scale benchmark would score it as worthless. **Interventions with no measurable small-scale benefit can be the highest-value ones** — a directly usable filter when reading lab publications and open-weight model cards.
	- **Bottlenecks are not walls** (footnote): they are *"continuous inefficiencies which start out small and noticeable, but manageable, but then compound with scale into increasingly large hits such that at large enough scale you essentially cannot progress at all."* This is why bottleneck removal often also improves small-scale loss, and why the effect is non-linear in scale.
- ## The bottleneck ledger — what each innovation buys and what it costs
	- | Innovation | Bottleneck removed | Cost / remaining limit |
	  |----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
	  | [Residual stream](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385) | Vanishing/exploding activations and gradients from multiplicative composition of Jacobians | Only **dampens**; buys "a good number of OOMs" of depth before it bites again |
	  | [GQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13245), [MLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04434), [others](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04476) | **KV-cache memory bandwidth** during decode — fewer KV heads than query heads | Constant-factor only; took context from thousands to **hundreds of thousands** |
	  | [SSMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752) | Attention's quadratic compute in sequence length, via RNN over a fixed-size state, parallelized by associative scans in prefill | **Fixed state cannot model a linearly growing one**; complement to attention, not a replacement |
	  | [Sparse attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02556) / [DSA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13392) | Reading the **whole** KV cache per decoded token — keep full cache, read a selected sparse subset | Quadratic cost is **moved into the indexer**, not removed; constant-factor speedup |
	  | [QK norm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04245) | Attention-logit explosion and softmax saturation → overflow/underflow and loss spikes at scale | Negligible small-scale loss benefit; essential for large-scale stability |
	  | **Mixture of Experts** | Couples inference cost to total parameters | Extends total params ~**2 OOMs**, *"but crucially not active parameters"* |
	  | [Hyperconnections / mHC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24880), [extensions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14530) | **Capacity of the residual stream itself** — one N-dim vector per token carrying all inter-layer information | Multiple parallel streams with learnt combining and splitting; added machinery |
	  | [Attention residuals](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15031) | [Exponential residual-stream norm growth](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/8mizBCm3dyc432nK8/residual-stream-norms-grow-exponentially-over-the-forward) that makes later layers ineffective | Attention over **L** layer activations — reads L vectors from HBM per layer |
	  | [Norm-agnostic (NAG) streams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16112) | Same norm-growth problem, by controlling addition geometry directly | Slightly reduced expressivity plus a complex norm-control apparatus |
	- **The KV-cache entry is the one with the clearest hardware read-through**: self-attention compares each new token's query against **all** prior keys and values, *"a massive amount of memory bandwidth that must be utilized for every single decoded token"* — and *"modern GPUs have vastly greater compute available than the memory bandwidth needed to saturate the compute."* Without GQA-class reductions, decode would be bandwidth-saturated even at moderate context. Compare the same conclusion reached empirically in [[2026-08-09-semianalysis-tilert-inferencex-gpu-vs-dataflow-asic]] and [[2026-06-03-ai-memory-wall-agentic-ai-dram-demand]].
	- **The residual-stream norm mechanism is worth understanding because it caps depth**: the stream is an additive sum of layer contributions; layer updates are correlated across depth in practice, so summing correlated vectors systematically grows the norm. For a late layer to have equal impact its output norm must grow exponentially too — but its inputs are normalized, so its *weights* must grow, and weight-norm growth is penalized by weight decay. Result: later layers under-contribute, giving *"an asymptoting effectiveness of depth"* and a soft ceiling on depth scaling.
- ## The hardware frame — and the footnote that matters most
	- Three substrate resources: **compute, memory, memory bandwidth**. On current GPUs the cost hierarchy is **memory bandwidth > compute > memory**. Architecture design is therefore either circumventing the newest bottleneck, or **finding favourable conversion ratios between these resources**.
	- **Sparsity is the exemplar trade**: it *"decoupl[es] storage from compute — i.e. we can store a massive system but only need to directly compute over some small fraction of it."* And it is grounded in physics, not convention: *"Information can mostly just be stored passively but actively reading or writing from it and computing things requires energy."* (He notes the brain does this more aggressively still — trillions of synapses, a tiny fraction firing at any moment, for metabolic reasons.)
	- **Scale context for MoE**: largest dense models today are in the **hundreds of billions** of parameters; largest sparse models are **~10T**. That is the two-OOM extension — in *total* parameters only.
	- **The standout claim for investing purposes is footnote 4**: the memory-bandwidth > compute > memory hierarchy *"is specific to current Nvidia-like GPUs. **SRAM chips like Groq and Cerebras have essentially an inverted cost hierarchy of memory > compute > memory bandwidth** and thus architectures designed for these face very different trade-offs."*
		- **Read-through, which the author does not draw**: the dominant direction of frontier architecture — sparsity everywhere, MoE total-parameter counts toward 10T — is an optimization **against the GPU cost curve**, spending the resource GPUs have cheaply (capacity) to save the resource they have expensively (bandwidth). On SRAM dataflow silicon that trade runs **backwards**: capacity is the scarce resource (Cerebras WSE-3 at 44 GB on-wafer SRAM, Groq LPU at 230 MB — see [[2026-05-21-hbm-perf-ratio-cerebras-vs-gpus]]), so a 10T-parameter sparse model is close to the worst possible workload for them.
		- **The models are drifting away from the challengers' hardware regardless of what the challengers do.** That is a structural headwind independent of, and additive to, the fleet-fungibility argument in [[2026-08-09-semianalysis-tilert-inferencex-gpu-vs-dataflow-asic]]. It also means dataflow vendors' best case is a **dense** model of moderate size — precisely the segment the frontier is leaving.
		- Conversely it is a quiet bull point for **capacity-heavy memory demand**: MoE scaling drives total-parameter storage faster than it drives active compute, which is a different demand vector from HBM bandwidth (relevant to [[2026-08-21-sk-hynix-hbm-history-mr-muf-moat]]).
- ## Predictions and forward markers
	- **Context length**: sparse attention variants *"will allow us to push out the sequence length frontier another OOM or two to **over 10M tokens** without truly bottlenecking decode."* If that lands, long-context agentic workloads stop being gated by decode economics — the same frontier the AgentX benchmark is built to test.
	- **The quadratic bottleneck is not solved and may not be**: it *"seems fundamental and we have not yet figured out a way to just completely remove this with no downsides."* Instead the field is *"sketching out a pareto frontier between state-size and expressivity."* Treat any claim of a clean linear-attention replacement for attention with suspicion; the honest framing is a new point on a frontier, not an escape from it.
	- **The named next trade to watch is diffusion**: *"if we can swap memory for memory bandwidth and compute, as sparsity does, or if we can trade memory bandwidth for compute, like diffusion, then these are good trades to make."* **Diffusion language models are framed here as a hardware arbitrage** — converting the expensive resource (bandwidth) into the cheaper one (compute). Given that compute-to-bandwidth ratios keep widening on each GPU generation, that trade gets *more* attractive over time, which is a concrete reason to track diffusion LLM progress as an infrastructure-demand variable rather than a modelling curiosity.
	- **The general method he leaves the reader with**: scaling means scaling depth, width, sequence length, flops, and memory bandwidth *simultaneously*, so the work is *"figuring out which part of the system is likely to become the most important bottleneck as we continue to scale and then figuring out how to push that bottleneck out further until another one becomes more pressing."* The progression he sketches — signal propagation → sequence length and memory bandwidth → residual-stream capacity → depth-effectiveness — is a usable checklist for anticipating which hardware resource the next architecture generation will bid up.
