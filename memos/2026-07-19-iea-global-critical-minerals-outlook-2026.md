- **Source**: International Energy Agency (IEA), *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*, Executive Summary, pp. 7–13 (provided text excerpt).
- **Thesis**: The critical-minerals trade is shifting from a simple commodity-shortage thesis to a government-backed supply-chain-security cycle. The most durable value may accrue not merely to miners, but to scarce ex-China refining, magnet, smelting, recycling, conversion and processing assets that can secure policy support and long-term offtake.

- ## Executive Investment Takeaways
	- **Copper has the clearest large-market structural deficit**: the IEA still projects a 25% supply shortfall in 2035 despite improving the estimate from roughly 30% last year. Copper-focused capex rose 8% in 2025 while broader critical-mineral investment fell 9%, showing that capital already distinguishes copper from weaker battery-metal markets.
	- **The bottleneck is migrating downstream**: geographically diversified mining projects are advancing faster than refining, cathode, magnet and other manufacturing capacity. By 2035, diversified rare-earth refining capacity equals roughly two-thirds of expected mined supply, but planned magnet capacity equals only one-third; planned cathode capacity is likewise only about one-third of projected lithium mining capacity.
	- **Strategic minor minerals offer asymmetric economics**: gallium, germanium, tungsten, yttrium, tellurium, antimony and magnet rare earths support enormous downstream markets but have small primary markets. A few projects can materially improve resilience, making them natural candidates for targeted subsidies, price floors and guaranteed offtake.
	- **Ex-China supply requires a policy-created security premium**: refining projects outside the dominant supplier face 20% to more than 150% higher capital costs and about 50% higher operating costs on average. Market prices alone are unlikely to sustain diversification through a commodity downcycle.
	- **Base-metal smelters are hidden strategic assets—but poor standalone economics matter**: copper, zinc and lead smelters recover many minor minerals as by-products and provide recycling capacity. Yet copper treatment charges settled at USD 0/tonne in 2026, spot charges have been negative since 2024, and non-China utilisation fell below 70% in 2025.
	- **Supply disruption is now an observed earnings risk, not a tail scenario**: China's April 2025 heavy-rare-earth controls forced some automakers to cut utilisation or temporarily halt production. The proposed broader controls were suspended only until November 2026, leaving a visible policy catalyst and deadline.
	- **Nuclear's nearer-term constraint is fuel processing, not just uranium ore**: conversion capacity is already tight, enrichment must expand over the medium term, and higher-assay low-enriched uranium adds another source of demand. This favors scarce Western fuel-cycle capacity in addition to mine supply.

- ## IEA Outlook and Predictions
	- **Copper and lithium deficits persist through 2035**: project advancement narrows the gaps but does not eliminate them. Copper's projected 25% deficit is the report's strongest quantified long-duration scarcity signal.
	- **Cobalt has shifted from potential surplus to projected deficit**: the DRC export quota demonstrates that sovereign policy can change the balance faster than mine-development models can adapt.
	- **Recycling's contribution could double by 2040**: average rates approach 20%, led by established copper and cobalt streams and later by lithium, nickel and end-of-life rare-earth magnets from EVs and wind turbines.
	- **Latin American lithium output rises nearly 50% by the end of the decade**: the larger economic opportunity is to pair mine growth with local refining and processing.
	- **Uranium conversion is the immediate fuel-cycle bottleneck; enrichment follows**: new reactors and HALEU demand increase the urgency of capacity additions.
	- **Full rare-earth control implementation remains a live November 2026 risk**: suspension delays rather than resolves the threat to ex-China manufacturing.

- ## Derived Predictions and Variant View
	- **Policy support will move from mine financing toward midstream economics**: grants and loans can build capacity, but 50% average operating-cost disadvantages mean governments will increasingly need price floors, contracts for difference, offtake backstops, sourcing mandates and strategic reserves.
	- **The highest-quality assets will combine strategic scarcity with contractual protection**: investors should prefer projects with government participation, committed offtake, floor pricing, low-cost power, secure feedstock and proven process technology over high-cost commodity exposure relying on spot prices.
	- **Non-China smelting capacity is likely to consolidate or become quasi-regulated infrastructure**: zero/negative treatment charges and sub-70% utilisation are incompatible with durable standalone returns. Closures would worsen concentration, increasing the probability of subsidies or strategic intervention.
	- **Minor-mineral by-product credits become more important in base-metal asset valuation**: as treatment-fee income disappears, the ability to recover gallium, germanium, indium, tellurium, rhenium and other by-products can determine smelter competitiveness.
	- **Large regional price spreads can persist**: export controls, specialised equipment shortages, patents and qualification timelines prevent rapid arbitrage between Chinese domestic and ex-China markets.
	- **Equipment and process know-how may be the scarcest layer**: rare-earth grain-boundary-diffusion equipment outside China reportedly costs more than 10x and has only one supplier; ultra-high-purity gallium and graphite processing have similarly narrow equipment bases. Capacity announcements without technical capability deserve a valuation discount.
	- **Strategic stockpiling creates price-insensitive incremental demand**: below USD 900M per year across 11 high-risk materials is fiscally small, making procurement politically easier after visible industrial disruptions.
	- **The security premium should be highest where minerals are a tiny share of end-product value**: rare-earth users can pay substantially more for resilient supply while adding little to vehicle cost, supporting premium contracts more readily than in battery materials or copper-intensive equipment.

- ## Stock-Selection Framework
	- **Most attractive exposure types**
		- Tier-one copper resources with credible permits, infrastructure and 2030s production profiles.
		- Proven ex-China processors in magnet rare earths, graphite anodes, gallium/germanium and nuclear conversion/enrichment.
		- Integrated mine-to-refined-product platforms that avoid reliance on Chinese toll processing.
		- Base-metal smelters with unusually valuable by-product recovery, recycling capability and policy support.
		- Recycling platforms with secure collection feedstock, high recovery yields and contracted buyers outside China.
		- Equipment, engineering and process-technology vendors that solve purification, separation, magnet or battery-material bottlenecks.
	- **Avoid or discount**
		- Early-stage projects that advertise strategic geology but lack processing know-how, infrastructure, permits or bankable offtake.
		- Diversified projects whose economics require permanent spot-price premiums but have no policy or contract protection.
		- Custom smelters dependent on treatment charges and volatile by-product revenue without secure concentrate supply.
		- Recycling capacity built ahead of local scrap availability or dependent on Chinese recovery stages.
		- Mining projects whose concentrate must still be refined in the dominant supplier, leaving the core security risk unresolved.

- ## Catalysts
	- Actual disbursement of the roughly USD 65B in advanced-economy public-finance commitments.
	- New strategic stockpile purchases, sourcing mandates, price floors, contracts for difference or government-backed offtake agreements.
	- November 2026 decisions on China's suspended extraterritorial rare-earth restrictions.
	- Additional DRC cobalt quota changes or new lithium/graphite trade restrictions from producing countries.
	- Smelter closures, consolidation or rescue packages outside China as treatment charges remain depressed.
	- Binding construction and customer-qualification milestones for non-China magnet, cathode, anode, conversion and enrichment capacity.
	- M&A for high-grade copper projects and strategic processing assets.

- ## Risks and Counterarguments
	- **Policy promises may not become cash**: the report explicitly flags a gap between government commitments and disbursements.
	- **High prices can cure scarcity**: substitution, efficiency, demand destruction, recycling and new projects can reduce deficits over time; the copper estimate already improved from 30% to 25%.
	- **Incumbents can defend share through price**: dominant suppliers with lower costs may compress prices long enough to strand subsidised entrants after construction.
	- **Processing scale-up is technically difficult**: announced capacity is not equivalent to qualified, high-yield commercial output.
	- **Resource nationalism can impair project economics**: export quotas, local-processing mandates, taxes and trade restrictions can benefit prices while hurting specific operators.
	- **Smelters may remain value traps**: strategic importance does not guarantee attractive shareholder returns if governments preserve capacity without allowing adequate economics.
	- **Commodity equities can front-run fundamentals**: copper M&A and capex strength suggest part of the long-term shortage thesis may already be reflected in asset prices.

- ## What to Monitor
	- Copper mine approvals, construction slippage, grades and annual changes to the projected 2035 deficit.
	- Public-finance **disbursements** versus commitments, including the form of support and conditions attached.
	- Ex-China/China regional price spreads for gallium, germanium, dysprosium, terbium and tungsten.
	- Rare-earth magnet and battery-cathode capacity relative to upstream mine capacity—not mine announcements alone.
	- Copper, zinc and lead treatment charges; non-China smelter utilisation; by-product recovery revenue.
	- DRC cobalt quota policy and producer-country restrictions on lithium and graphite.
	- Customer qualification, yield, equipment availability and commissioning timelines at ex-China processors.
	- Recycling collection volumes and actual recovery output by region.
	- Uranium conversion and enrichment contract prices, capacity additions and HALEU demand.
	- Evidence that downstream customers accept a security premium through long-term contracts rather than reverting to the lowest-cost supplier.
