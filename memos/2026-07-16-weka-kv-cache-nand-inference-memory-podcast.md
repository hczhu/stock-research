- ## Weka on KV Cache, NAND, and the Inference Memory Stack
	- **Source**: Semi-Doped podcast, Vick Shaker interviewing Val Bercovici, Chief AI Officer of Weka. Transcript supplied July 16, 2026; episode date and title were not included.
	- **Core thesis**: Agentic inference is becoming a memory- and cache-management problem rather than merely a compute problem. Compression reduces KV-cache use per token, but longer contexts, more concurrent agents, longer task horizons, and multimodality expand aggregate token and memory demand faster. This creates a new memory hierarchy in which network-attached NAND can act as a high-capacity memory tier—provided the system preserves production latency and throughput.
	- **Investment takeaway**: The strongest read-through is not simply “more storage.” Value shifts toward the parts of the stack that can deliver high effective cache-hit rates at acceptable service levels: high-speed networking, inference scheduling and routing, cache-aware software, endurance-optimized flash, and tightly integrated memory systems. HBM/DRAM remain critical, but NAND becomes more strategically valuable as an active inference tier.
	- **Attribution caveat**: Unless otherwise noted, all figures and forecasts below are statements by Bercovici or the host, not independently verified facts. Bercovici represents Weka, so claims about Weka performance and competing architectures should be treated as vendor positioning.

- ## Insight 1 — Inference Has a Different Bottleneck Than Training
	- Training remains compute-heavy and is well suited to large homogeneous GPU clusters; inference bifurcates into compute-bound prefill and memory-bound decode.
	- Bercovici points to the proliferation of inference architectures—GPU, SRAM-heavy LPU/ASIC systems, Cerebras-style wafer-scale systems, and mixtures of SRAM, HBM, DRAM, and NAND—as evidence that there will be no single optimal inference architecture.
	- Model routing is the coarse-grained first optimization: agent swarms should reserve the largest, most memory-intensive models for hard turns and route routine work to medium or small models.
	- The economic objective becomes tokens per second and concurrent users per dollar, subject to latency and quality constraints—not peak accelerator FLOPS.
	- **Implication**: Inference diversification is favorable to memory, networking, and orchestration vendors whose products can work across accelerator types; it is less favorable to any thesis that assumes training's GPU-centric architecture maps unchanged onto inference.

- ## Insight 2 — The Memory Hierarchy Is Becoming a Systems Problem
	- Bercovici describes Nvidia Dynamo's four-tier hierarchy:
		- **G1**: GPU HBM.
		- **G2**: CPU-attached DRAM, LPDDR, or SOCAMM.
		- **G3**: local or rack SSD.
		- **G4**: remote storage such as NFS or S3.
	- These are discontinuous tiers, not a smooth ladder: latency and bandwidth can change by orders of magnitude. A nominally cheap offload tier can destroy time-to-first-token and throughput.
	- Weka's counterintuitive claim is that an NVLink- or high-speed-fabric-attached NAND pool can exceed CPU DRAM's raw aggregate bandwidth because the network path is wider than the motherboard path.
	- This does **not** mean NAND has lower device latency than DRAM. The claim depends on parallelism, network line rate, software architecture, transfer size, and bypassing bottlenecked CPU paths.
	- **Implication**: The winner is unlikely to be the cheapest media in isolation. It will be the system that coordinates placement, prefetch, routing, and transfers across HBM, DRAM, and flash without violating service-level objectives.

- ## Insight 3 — Cache Economics Are the Real Agent-Economics Benchmark
	- Agent turns reuse large prefixes: system prompts, repository context, tool definitions, conversation state, and outputs from sibling agents. This creates very high logical cacheability.
	- Effective physical hit rate can be much lower because providers have finite HBM and DRAM. Evicted cache entries only remain useful if a lower tier can restore them quickly enough.
	- Bercovici argues that provider price sheets and profit-and-loss outcomes are better benchmarks than lab demos: a provider cannot sustainably offer very low cached-read prices without a correspondingly efficient infrastructure stack or subsidy.
	- DeepSeek's low cached-read pricing sets a global reference point, even if the lowest price is limited to China-hosted inference. Open-weight providers outside China have room to differentiate through caching, compression, and energy efficiency.
	- **Implication**: Watch cached-read price, cache-write price, effective hit rate, and production latency together. Headline input/output token prices increasingly obscure the cost structure of agent workloads.

- ## Insight 4 — KV-Cache Compression Is More Likely an Enabler Than a Demand Killer
	- Techniques mentioned include quantization such as TurboQuant, sliding-window attention, hybrid compressed attention, and other short- and long-horizon attention mechanisms.
	- DeepSeek was described as combining about five attention mechanisms to cut KV-cache consumption per request by roughly 90%.
	- The offsetting demand vectors are powerful:
		- Context windows moving from 100K toward 1M tokens and potentially beyond.
		- Agents operating for hours or days, eventually weeks for persistent tasks such as cybersecurity.
		- Parallel subagents multiplying concurrent sessions.
		- Multimodal context adding image, audio, and video tokens.
		- Lower cost per task stimulating more usage.
	- **Variant perception**: Compression headlines can trigger bearish reactions in HBM/DRAM/NAND, but the more important variable is aggregate useful token volume. A 10× efficiency improvement that makes 100× more agent work economical is net positive for memory consumption.

- ## Insight 5 — NAND Is Splitting Into Inference-Specific Tiers
	- Training storage favored capacity-oriented QLC because data was written infrequently and read repeatedly. KV-cache workloads are bursty and write-intensive, making endurance and write amplification central.
	- **SLC**: Bercovici expects renewed demand as a high-endurance write buffer for context-memory and HBF systems.
	- **TLC**: Weka claims it can sustain KV-cache workloads by spreading writes across a shared NVMe fabric with global queue visibility, avoiding a dedicated SLC tier.
	- **QLC**: Attractive on cost and density, but generally needs buffering, intelligent scheduling, or heavy overprovisioning; de-staging from SLC can add latency and complexity.
	- Weka's “shared everything” architecture allegedly distributes writes across many drives and queues, treating the NVMe fabric more like a memory system than independent storage devices.
	- **Implication**: AI flash demand may favor endurance, controllers, firmware, and system-level software—not just raw NAND bit growth. Effective capacity and drive replacement rates matter as much as shipped capacity.

- ## HBF, CXL, and Predictive Tiering
	- **High Bandwidth Flash (HBF)**
		- Bercovici expects SLC in common HBF configurations because HBF must absorb memory-like write intensity.
		- SK Hynix's vision of packaging HBF near or on the accelerator could emerge around 2028, but accelerator-vendor commitment, packaging form factor, and endurance remain unresolved.
		- Packaging NAND directly with an accelerator raises a lifetime mismatch: worn flash cannot be replaced independently. Mitigations include overprovisioning, write distribution, inference-aware scheduling, model compilation, and token routing.
		- He predicts compilers could eventually target HBM and HBF directly and bypass CPU DRAM for some paths.
	- **CXL**
		- Bercovici is bearish despite CXL's ability to pool underutilized DRAM. His view is that NVLink, RoCE/Ethernet, and InfiniBand already provide deployable high-bandwidth alternatives without adding another bus.
		- His key evidence is absence: CXL has not become a standard component of Nvidia Blackwell/Vera Rubin, AMD Helios, or mainstream Dell/HPE/Lenovo/Supermicro AI systems.
		- The transcript cites a report that Nvidia cut Vera Rubin DRAM capacity roughly in half, which Bercovici interprets as confidence that CMX-style tiers can reduce CPU DRAM requirements. This claim requires verification.
	- **Predictive tiering / AMD-Mext**
		- Mext applies small neural networks to predict which DRAM pages should move to or return from NAND, replacing simple recency heuristics with semantic or workload-aware caching.
		- Bercovici says the proven applications are scientific computing rather than KV cache today, but the architecture could migrate into inference.
		- Broader implication: cheap, domain-specific models can optimize infrastructure locally; not every useful AI model needs to be a large language model.

- ## Risks and Counterarguments
	- **Bandwidth is not latency**: A network-attached flash pool can have higher aggregate bandwidth than a constrained CPU-DRAM path while still having much worse access latency. Application performance depends on request granularity and cache policy.
	- **Vendor incentive**: Weka benefits from redefining storage as memory infrastructure and from arguing that its fabric can displace DRAM or SLC. Independent production benchmarks are needed.
	- **Compression could win**: Architectural changes—latent attention, state-space models, retrieval, recurrent memory, or better context pruning—could reduce KV-cache growth faster than usage expands.
	- **Maximum context is not utilized context**: Million-token model limits do not prove that customers will economically use million-token prompts at high concurrency.
	- **Cacheability is not monetizable hit rate**: A 95% logically reusable prefix may still miss because of eviction, tenant isolation, routing across providers, model updates, or privacy requirements.
	- **Vertical integration may destroy value**: SaaS companies buying neoclouds would inherit capex, power, hardware-obsolescence, and utilization risk. Reserved capacity, custom inference contracts, or open-weight self-hosting may be better than M&A.
	- **CXL dismissal may be premature**: Open standards, CPU-vendor support, and stranded DRAM economics could sustain CXL even if Nvidia-centric GPU clusters prefer NVLink.
	- **HBF is early**: Packaging, endurance, software support, qualification, repairability, and accelerator-vendor commitment remain open questions.

- ## What to Monitor
	- Production benchmarks using 200K–1M-token contexts, multi-turn agents, and high concurrency—not small-model, single-chat demonstrations.
	- Provider disclosures for cache-read share, effective hit rate, cache-write price, time-to-first-token, and output tokens/sec.
	- Nvidia CMX/STX ecosystem announcements and whether CMX reduces CPU DRAM content or expands total addressable memory per rack.
	- HBF packaging decisions from SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron, Nvidia, AMD, and custom-ASIC vendors.
	- Enterprise SSD endurance specifications, overprovisioning ratios, write amplification, and replacement cycles under KV-cache workloads.
	- Whether CXL becomes standard in merchant AI servers or remains confined to specialized memory-pooling deployments.
	- SaaS gross margins and cost-of-revenue trends as agent features scale; evidence of neocloud M&A versus long-term capacity contracts.
	- Independent validation that Weka's augmented memory grid improves tokens/sec and concurrent users at lower total cost while meeting production service levels.
