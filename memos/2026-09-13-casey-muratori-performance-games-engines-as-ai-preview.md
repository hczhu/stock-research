- ## Casey Muratori — The Games Industry Already Ran the AI Experiment, and Distribution Won
	- **Source**: The Pragmatic Engineer podcast, Gergely Orosz interviewing **Casey Muratori** (Molly Rocket, *Computer Enhance*; previously RAD Game Tools, Microsoft). Career biography, assembly-language pedagogy and the clean-code argument are craft content and are compressed to what carries investment weight. Sponsor reads are advertising and are segregated.
	- **Source quality**: Muratori is a performance advocate arguing a position he has held for a decade, and he flags his own limits — he has not worked inside a modern live-service studio and calls himself "not a game historian." The games-industry analysis is the most valuable part *because* it is retrospective and falsifiable; the performance advocacy is the part most colored by his priors.
	- **Thesis**: The investable content is not the performance argument. It is Muratori's concession, drawn out by the host, that **the games industry already ran the experiment everyone is now running with AI** — a technical capability that was the primary barrier to entry got commoditized, supply exploded, and **value migrated from production to distribution.** His verdict on that trade is deliberately grim: "the licensable engine thing kind of was our AI transition already, unfortunately. And I regret to inform you that the news is not probably that positive."
- ## The completed experiment
	- **Before licensable engines**, a studio faced two existential risks. **Engine risk** — could you build something technically capable of the game at all, and fast enough to build the game *on*? And **fun risk** — is the game any good, which you could barely test because you had no engine and no level tools until the end. Muratori notes the codebase was itself the asset: rolling Warcraft 1's engine, pathfinding and tooling into Warcraft 2 was "a huge advantage," because a competitor "has to do all of that from scratch."
	- There was no way to buy your way out. Hardware you could test on was roughly what consumers had, so "you were just gritting it through."
	- **Unreal, Unity and Godot removed engine risk and the capital barrier.** Muratori's honest accounting of what followed:
		- **Early: net positive.** People who "could not have marshaled the technical staff" made games; some were good; artistic expression that would not otherwise have existed.
		- **Then it inverted.** "It rapidly kind of accelerates into this kind of a nasty scenario where you just have massive numbers of releases" — Steam at tens of thousands to ~100,000 titles a year. **"There is no way that your game will be organically noticed any more, pretty much period."**
		- **The mechanism that died is the one that mattered.** "It used to be that if you made a quality game, if it was fun, people would find it." Now the odds of being the organic breakout "are like zero."
	- **The conclusion, which generalizes**: the product being good becomes **table stakes**, and distribution and marketing become the differentiator. "Was that a good trade? I don't know. But that's what happened."
	- **Read-through if AI does to software what engines did to games** — and the host's framing that games "might give us a hint" is the right one: technical capability commoditizes, software supply floods, and **value accrues to whoever owns distribution rather than whoever builds well.** That is bullish for platform owners, app stores, existing user bases and brand, and structurally bearish for the thesis that better-built software wins on merit. It also argues the returns to AI-enabled production capacity get competed away faster than the returns to owning demand.
- ## Two compounding headwinds the games analogy exposes
	- **Old content stopped depreciating.** Games used to age out visibly — "you come out with something that takes advantage of the hardware of that day, and boy, did it look cooler." Now "a large portion of the gaming market by revenue doesn't really care what the game looked like," and "I don't have to be an appreciator of retro gaming to go play something from 2017. It just looks fine." New releases therefore compete against the entire back catalogue, permanently.
	- **Attention is zero-sum and live service has already claimed it.** Hours spent in Fortnite, Minecraft, League and Dota are hours not spent buying anything new — "they can only spend their hours in certain places, just like Netflix... they're competing with everyone else for entertainment hours."
	- **Together these are a structural headwind to new-release unit economics** that content-supply growth models tend to miss: supply rises, the substitute set widens to include every prior year's output, and the demand denominator is fixed. Relevant to [[2026-06-25-roblox-retention-first-discovery-algorithm-change]] and the discovery problem it documents.
- ## GTA 6 is a franchise replacement, not a launch
	- Muratori reframes the long development cycle as a business problem rather than an execution problem, and it is a usable modeling frame: **"Grand Theft Auto 6 is not a game that they are selling to players who are going to play the game. It is a replacement of Grand Theft Auto 5."**
	- GTA 5 was, on his recollection, "by far the most revenue generating entertainment product in existence," with GTA Online generating billions — **"Fortnite before Fortnite."**
	- **The actual risk is cannibalization, not demand**: "the last thing you want to do is ship a new product that cannibalizes something from your old product and then is less revenue generating."
	- Two supporting observations. GTA 5's online success was likely **a surprise** to Rockstar rather than a plan, and the attempt to repeat it with Red Dead Redemption 2 "didn't hit nearly as big." So the live-service economics are **not demonstrated as transferable** even within the same publisher.
	- His summary is the line worth keeping: **"It's equivalent to a relaunch of Google search. If I was in charge of that project, I would be sweating bullets."**
	- **The investable question for [[$TTWO]] is therefore not units sold at launch.** It is whether the live-service revenue base migrates, and at what rate — a question whose only prior evidence point, RDR2, is negative.
- ## Performance as a go-to-market wedge, and why it usually fails
	- Muratori's standing claim is that most software runs **10 to 100 times slower than it needs to**, and he thinks the tide is finally turning — pointing to products attacking incumbents on speed alone and getting traction, naming File Pilot and a video editor he calls Blick. The host adds the two examples with real commercial weight: **Bun against npm** on 10–50× claims, and **Linear against Jira**, where Linear holds a **300-millisecond budget for any action**.
	- Muratori's own gloss on that budget is the sharper point: **300ms "is like an eternity in computing,"** and needing to advertise it "shows you how the bar was so far past where it probably should have been."
	- **But he gives three structural reasons performance usually cannot win, and they matter more than the examples**:
		- **The buyer is not the user.** Enterprise software is purchased on cost, compliance and legal liability by someone who is "not in there looking to see whether there's a 30-second pause every time you want to access somebody's record." Users may hate it and "might not be in any position to change it."
		- **Monopoly and network effects dominate.** Bluesky and Threads tried to assail X. Performance "can't sell a product on its own into a monopoly space" without a distribution plan — which is the same conclusion as the games section, reached independently.
		- **The winnable slice is shrinking.** "If you're just talking about apps that someone can choose to download, maybe you've got a shot there, but those are forming a smaller and smaller subset of what software is."
	- **Net read**: performance is a viable wedge in a narrowing set of categories — developer tools and prosumer utilities, where the buyer *is* the user and switching is individual. It is not a wedge in enterprise procurement or against platform incumbents. That sharpens rather than supports the Linear-versus-Jira case tracked in [[2026-07-04-atlassian-news-roundup-pricing-rovo-competition]]: Linear wins where an individual team chooses, not where procurement does.
- ## Architecture debt is the part you cannot defer, and rewrites are the proof
	- The "premature optimization" defense works **only for hotspot-shaped problems** — swap a naive sort for a good one, localized, deferrable. Muratori accepts that case explicitly.
	- **It fails for architecture.** His example is a **serial dependency chain**: a codebase where every operation is ask-the-server, wait, compute, ask again. That chain "cannot be shortened without just rewriting it" — it cannot be parallelized, multithreaded or amortized, and it is not a hotspot, so profiling will not find it. You call in performance experts "and they go, there's nothing we could do for you, sorry."
	- **The generalized claim**: "your code base will not end up [hotspot-shaped] by accident in most cases anymore. **You have to engineer up front for a hotspot code base** that people can then optimize."
	- **The falsification argument is elegant and is the memo-worthy line**: companies — "it's Facebook, it's Uber, it's everybody" — publish posts about rewriting whole systems for performance. **"If it was always hotspots that made your performance bad, you'd never have to rewrite the whole thing."** The existence of full rewrites is itself evidence that deferred optimization does not work at the architectural level.
	- **He also flags why the public record is unreliable**: rewrite postmortems give the safe reason (we changed languages) rather than the real one (an architectural oversight), because "a lot of those blog posts are going to help someone get promoted," and large companies have content teams — "not quite PR, but somewhere midway in between." **Discount engineering blog posts as evidence of causation.**
	- **Investable consequence**: periodic full rewrites are a recurring, largely unmodeled cost line in software companies, and they are a lagging indicator of architectural decisions made years earlier — which means AI-accelerated code production raises the stakes on the architectural decisions it does not make.
- ## The Python-to-Rust migration at the frontier labs
	- The host supplies the live datapoint: the pattern that took Uber from Python and Node.js to Go and Java **"right now it's happening with AI companies... OpenAI and Anthropic are both going through this right now."**
	- The mechanism is the same one every time. Python was chosen because data scientists and ML engineers knew it, web servers and APIs were built on it, and it scaled horizontally — until horizontal scaling stopped being the cheap answer. The move is to **Rust** (the host hedges Go, "but I think it's Rust") so that "the same machine can actually handle more connections" multithreaded.
	- **Why this matters beyond engineering trivia**: at the frontier labs this is a **serving-efficiency and therefore COGS** decision, not a preference. Requests-per-machine is a direct input to gross margin on inference-adjacent traffic. Relevant to [[2026-07-18-napkin-llm-inference-cost-serving-economics]] and the margin work in [[2026-07-13-semianalysis-anthropic-ipo-financials-tokenomics]] — and it suggests some portion of reported margin improvement is ordinary software rearchitecture rather than model or hardware efficiency.
- ## Why AI productivity gains may be simultaneously real and invisible
	- Muratori declines to assess: the people whose judgment he trusts did not find the tools usable until recently, and he wants "another six months, if not another year" before anyone can say what the right workflows are.
	- **The calibration point is the useful one, and it cuts against a lot of commentary in both directions**: "If people got 10% more productive, that would still be pretty impressive, because it's hard to get a 10% across-the-board uplift... but almost no one would know. **It's not really externally observable.**"
	- His falsifiable threshold for the strong claim: "It should be like **five people are now shipping Fortnite instead of 5,000**." Nothing like that has appeared.
	- **He discounts the lab self-reports explicitly**, and the host agrees: the only widespread software written close to 100% by agents comes from OpenAI and Anthropic, "but that's a product they're selling," so there is a sell bias. Worth applying to the Codex disclosures in [[2026-09-13-pragmatic-engineer-codex-harness-shrinks-cloud-execution]].
	- **The usable conclusion**: absence of visible productivity gains is **not** evidence of absence, because the commercially meaningful effect size sits below the detection threshold of outside observation. Any memo claiming AI productivity has or has not shown up in the numbers should carry this caveat.
- ## AI fatigue tracks autonomy, not capability
	- The host reports a pattern from many engineers and managers: **"I am good at coding... but since this AI thing happened, I'm starting to lose my drive. Why am I here? Anyone could do this."** He calls it AI fatigue or burnout and says he has not seen a technology produce this breadth of effect before — reported across AI-native firms, infrastructure vendors and traditional companies alike.
	- **The correlation both he and Muratori land on is autonomy.** High-autonomy engineers reach for AI when there is something they *did not want to do*, so the experience is positive by construction. Engineers handed tickets and usage mandates experience the same tool as a threat, particularly alongside layoffs or forced reassignment.
	- Muratori's formulation is the one to keep: **"Are you using an AI to do your job, or is an AI using you to do your job?"** — with the observation that mandated labeling and evaluation work reads to the worker as "you're just there to train the AI to do it so that we don't need you anymore."
	- **Investable read**: companies that measure and mandate individual AI usage are converting a productivity tool into a retention liability, and **autonomy becomes a compensating differential in engineering compensation.** That partially re-rates the attractiveness of large-cap tech employment relative to smaller firms, which is a slow-moving input to talent quality.
- ## Benchmarks that decide vendor selection are often measuring the wrong thing
	- The host relays a Simon Eskildsen anecdote from Shopify: teams chose between database vendors by writing their own benchmarks and picking the faster result — two seconds versus ten. Checked against the theoretical floor for the operation, roughly 100 milliseconds, **"the benchmark was just wrong. They were benchmarking the wrong thing and they were making decisions."**
	- Muratori's methodology is the corrective, and it is the through-line of his teaching. The common approach — profile, change the big parts, see if the statistics improve — **"is not optimization, that's improvement,"** and it only finds a local minimum. The correct approach is to establish what the hardware can do at theoretical peak, **measure the delta**, and explain the residual.
	- **Why this is investable rather than merely methodological**: enterprise infrastructure purchasing decisions are being made on self-authored benchmarks that can be wrong by two orders of magnitude. That is a real source of vendor-selection noise, it argues that benchmark-led competitive claims deserve heavy discounting — **including AI inference benchmarks** — and it means observed vendor win rates are a weaker signal of product quality than they appear.
	- A related note with a similar flavor: Muratori's anti-clean-code position is not about virtual call cost. **"It's the cost of the compiler not being able to do any optimisations. That's the actual cost."** He argues well-architected code and fast code usually coincide, diverging only "way out on the curve."
