- ## Stratechery: AI was on the ballot, and data centers lost. What if the political limit on building is permanent?
	- **Source**: Ben Thompson, Stratechery Update "AI on the Ballot," Nov 4, 2026, the morning after the US midterms. Election results are as reported by Bloomberg and Yahoo Finance at the time of writing. The ten takes are Thompson's own scenarios, and he says plainly that he isn't calling the constraint permanent. Related: [[2026-09-15-semianalysis-datacenter-moratoriums-300-mapped]], [[2026-09-11-nvidia-backstop-universe-credit-support]], [[2026-10-26-anthropic-s1-financials-compute-economics]], [[2026-06-19-semianalysis-space-datacenters-tco-orbital-compute]], [[2026-07-20-stratechery-chinese-models-intelligence-commodity-economics]], [[2026-11-03-stratechery-apple-memory-purchase-commitments-consumer-memory-market]].
	- **Thesis**: The binding constraint on AI has moved again. First it was compute, which ramped. Now it is memory, which will ramp. Power was close to binding, but that will be fixed. **The last constraint is political**, and it may be the hardest, because it is harder to solve and also gets in the way of solving the others. Many AI theories assume intelligence becomes a commodity. Thompson thinks it is just as likely that **AI stays scarce much longer than expected**, and that the scarcity changes who wins.
- ## What happened on Nov 3
	- **Midterms**: Democrats won both chambers in a landslide, flipping at least seven Senate seats. Thompson accepts that Iran and the economy mattered most nationally.
	- **Data centers were the top ad topic in Wisconsin**, and both parties ran *against* them, each accusing the other of supporting them. The issue wasn't partisan.
	- **Texas**: Gina Hinojosa became the first Democratic governor since 1990 on a promise of a **data-center construction moratorium**. Texas holds **over 25% of planned US capacity by 2030**. Her Republican opponent, Gov. Abbott, had already declared a moratorium on new data-center permits in September.
	- **Local ballot measures**: nearly every local measure opposing data centers won or is winning. The one exception so far is in North Dakota. Some local officials have been recalled for approving data centers.
- ## Why opposition is about AI, not data centers
	- Data centers built for the internet faced little opposition; towns competed for them for construction jobs and tax revenue. The current opposition is about AI itself, a technology pitched as taking jobs at best and ending humanity at worst.
	- **The contrast with globalization**: when factories moved to China, affected towns had no say. Building a data center needs permission, so for the first time the people worried about losing their jobs to a technology can vote against it. Thompson isn't saying they're well informed, only that they finally have a veto and are using it.
	- **The investment case makes it worse**: the scale of AI spending is hard to justify without admitting that a big part of the thesis is replacing workers.
- ## Ten takes, if the constraint persists
	- | # | Who | Thompson's view | Mechanism |
	  |---|---|---|---|
	  | 1 | **The AI trade** | Bad in the short term, but much of the pain may already have happened | Markets were already shaken by tighter credit, and AI funding has shifted to equity |
	  | 2 | **Neoclouds** | Mixed, leaning negative | Already-permitted sites become scarcer and more valuable. But neoclouds need new sites and have less political influence than hyperscalers, on top of costlier capital |
	  | 3 | **Hyperscalers** | Winners | They gain share of AI compute, can reuse or rebuild existing sites, and get a politically acceptable reason to moderate capex that every competitor shares |
	  | 4 | **Nvidia** | Short-term loser, long-term mixed | Fewer data centers means fewer chips, and weak neoclouds hurt most because they buy Nvidia racks and Nvidia backs many of them. But when sites are fixed, **tokens per megawatt** decides revenue, and Nvidia leads on it. Rebuilding an existing site with new Nvidia chips becomes the main way to add capacity |
	  | 5 | **China** | Advantaged | China has power and can build. It lacks leading-edge chips, but can make up for it with electricity (Huawei CloudMatrix 384: more chips, more power, worse efficiency). Once it gets better chips, that could be decisive |
	  | 6 | **Space data centers (SpaceX)** | Big upside | If nowhere on Earth is left to build, space becomes the only option. The more permanent the opposition, the lower the investment risk, because demand for orbital compute looks more durable |
	  | 7 | **Frontier labs** | Mostly winners | Anthropic's S-1 showed high inference margins, but R&D compute driven by the race was on track to exceed revenue. The public is now effectively capping that race. Scarce compute also favors efficient models over costlier-to-serve Chinese open models, and Democrats are more likely to pass rules that help the labs |
	  | 8 | **Apple** | Winner | On-device inference matters more. A slower AI rollout helps the company that dominates the current platform. Most important, **slower AI building eases the memory shortage**, and no company benefits more from that than Apple |
	  | 9 | **Startups** | Losers | Less cheap compute, slower capability gains to build on, and more time for frontier labs to lock in end users with their own products |
	  | 10 | **Users and society** | Mixed | No overbuild means no glut of cheap compute, and no Web 2.0-style wave of free AI services. AI keeps a meaningful marginal cost for a long time. But slower commoditization of intelligence gives people more time to adapt |
	- **Counterpoints Thompson gives for the labs**: costly compute pushes customers to right-size models, which favors open models at the low end, and the labs' cost advantage will never reach that end of the market. Labs also find it harder to build their own compute, leaving them dependent on hyperscalers. "Big tech wins again."
- ## Tokens per megawatt: the Nvidia argument in detail
	- Thompson returns to Huang's 2025 "iso power" keynote: if power is fixed, the question is how many tokens a fixed power envelope produces, and Blackwell produces far more than Hopper. Huang was effectively warning every cloud provider building an ASIC: can you keep up with Nvidia's cadence, or will you run out of power because your chips are less efficient?
	- **Why constraints help Nvidia's margins**: with unlimited power and sites, Nvidia's margins are hard to defend in the long run, because customers can buy cheaper, less efficient chips and simply build more. When sites are capped, efficiency is worth a lot, and existing sites get refreshed with the latest chips sooner.
	- **Read**: this reverses the usual case for ASICs. Their main selling point is cost per chip, which matters less when the binding cost is the site. Trainium and TPU would need to match Nvidia's tokens per megawatt, not just undercut its price.
- ## How this fits with other evidence
	- **SemiAnalysis's count points the other way, for now**: its moratorium map ([[2026-09-15-semianalysis-datacenter-moratoriums-300-mapped]]) found 20.1 GW inside moratorium boundaries but only ~2.3 GW actually delayed, with restrictions mostly changing *where* projects go. That was before the election. A statewide Texas moratorium would be a different order of magnitude, since Texas holds over a quarter of planned capacity. Whether the governor can impose one, and what it covers, isn't clear from the piece.
	- **Neoclouds were already weak on credit**: Nvidia's backstops ([[2026-09-11-nvidia-backstop-universe-credit-support]]) turn the neocloud permitting problem into contingent risk on Nvidia's balance sheet. Take 4 and take 2 are the same exposure.
	- **The memory link**: the Apple memo ([[2026-11-03-stratechery-apple-memory-purchase-commitments-consumer-memory-market]]) shows Apple locked into large purchase commitments at shortage prices. A slower buildout would help Apple only with a lag, and it would undercut the memory makers' long-shortage case.
- ## Investing read-across
	- **Already-permitted, powered sites are the scarce asset**: their value rises for whoever owns them (hyperscalers, permitted neoclouds, data-center REITs, bitcoin miners with grid connections). Owners of unpermitted land pipelines lose.
	- **Equipment shifts from new builds to refreshes**: less spending on new shells, more on replacing chips, power gear and cooling in existing facilities. That favors the most efficient accelerators and suppliers tied to retrofits.
	- **Memory and Nvidia diverge**: a slower buildout hurts memory demand directly, while Nvidia's share of a smaller market may rise. The two have traded together on AI capex; this scenario separates them.
	- **AI pricing stays high**: scarce compute supports the frontier labs' pricing and inference margins, and argues against the view that AI prices collapse toward cost.
	- **What would show the constraint is binding**: hyperscaler capex guides cut because of permitting rather than demand, neocloud project delays or defaults, rising prices for permitted sites, and whether Texas actually enacts a statewide moratorium.
	- **Thompson's closing caution**: citizens often want the result while opposing the means, and demand usually wins in the end. The difference now is that the industry has to persuade people and can no longer assume it gets the benefit of the doubt.
