- ## The Circuit — Broadcom's Custom ASIC Business Is Becoming Unmodelable, and 2027 as Peak Constraint
	- **Source**: The Circuit podcast, Ben Bajarin and Jay Goldberg, early September 2026. Internal references place the recording after Broadcom's FQ3 report, after the Nvidia–Hugging Face announcement, and before the Astra launch. Self-promotional segment on their own Atlas product omitted except where it carries a datapoint.
	- **Thesis**: Three things. **(1)** Broadcom guided \$115B FY27 and \$230B FY28 custom, and the circulating gigawatt math does not reconcile with it — which is the point, because compute-tile/networking/IO unbundling has made custom ASIC harder to model than merchant GPU. **(2)** Broadcom named MediaTek as a competitor after two quarters of denying competition existed, and simultaneously told investors to stop looking at gross margin — the two concessions arrive together and describe the same pressure. **(3)** Bajarin's call that **2027 is peak constraint** and relief arrives only in 2028, because the LTAs that gave suppliers confidence to build were signed just 8–12 months ago.
- ## Broadcom: in line, and the numbers underneath
	- Results and guidance both landed in line; the stock fell ~4% on the week. The read is that the number was anticipated and there was no upside surprise.
	- | Metric | Figure |
	  |---|---|
	  | Custom guidance, FY27 | **\$115B** (vs a circulating "bogey" near \$150B; hope was simply >\$100B) |
	  | Custom guidance, FY28 | **\$230B** |
	  | Total custom ASIC market, 2028E | **\$400–430B**, including logic, networking and attach |
	  | Customer gigawatts | **10 GW FY27 → 20 GW FY28**, all customers, with good diversification |
	  | Broadcom revenue per GW | **\$10–15B**, "sounds like it's now going to" **\$25–30B** |
	  | Nvidia revenue per GW, for contrast | **\$40–50B → \$90–100B** |
	- **The largest disclosed change in the customer base**: Anthropic and OpenAI are **poised to overtake Google** as Broadcom's largest ASIC customers — two accounts that came online in under a year and each grew past the TPU business that defined the franchise.
	- Buy-side consensus per the hosts is that \$115B and \$230B are **the floor**, with the open question being how much they beat by.
- ## The gigawatt arithmetic does not close, and that is the finding
	- **FY27 reconciles exactly**: \$115B ÷ 10 GW = **\$11.5B per GW**, inside the stated \$10–15B range.
	- **FY28 does not**: \$230B ÷ 20 GW is **still \$11.5B per GW** — so guidance embeds *flat* per-gigawatt content while doubling gigawatts, directly contradicting the \$25–30B per-GW figure quoted in the same conversation.
	- **Three readings, and they matter differently**:
		- If per-GW content genuinely reaches \$25–30B and Broadcom holds 20 GW, revenue would be **\$500–600B — roughly 2.2–2.6× the guide.** This is the arithmetic case for "\$230B is the floor," and it is a very large gap to call a floor.
		- But \$500–600B **exceeds the entire \$400–430B market estimate**, so the inputs are mutually inconsistent. At least one of {gigawatts, per-GW content, TAM} is wrong or defined differently — most likely the gigawatt count is industry-wide rather than Broadcom-served.
		- Even taking guidance at face value, \$230B of a \$400–430B market implies Broadcom holds **53–57% share** of a market the same conversation describes as fragmenting across MediaTek and Marvell. That is a lot of share to assume in a market losing its single-vendor structure.
	- Goldberg's own caution applies to both sides: "I really would love to see the spreadsheet behind both those math and how much of that is memory. I think both of them are lumping a lot of things in there." Relevant to the per-megawatt framing in [[2026-08-29-dylan-patel-dwarkesh-revenue-per-megawatt-compute-centralization]].
- ## Broadcom concedes MediaTek, six months after this repo started tracking it
	- **The concession**: two quarters ago Broadcom was "basically denying competition existed within Google." On this call it named MediaTek, while arguing it delivers faster, has better IP, and gets chips to market sooner. The hosts call the shift "a big step back."
	- This confirms rather than introduces. [[2026-03-28-mediatek-google-orders-and-broadcom-share-shift-2026-02]] flagged MediaTek taking Google share in February, and [[2026-06-23-mediatek-google-tpu-v9-336g-serdes-broadcom-displacement]] documented the TPU v9 displacement in June. **The new information is management acknowledgment, not the fact.** MediaTek won the compute tiles; whether it also took IO or full networking is unknown.
	- **The gross margin reversal is the tell, and it arrives in the same call.** Margins were down, and management's defense was to redirect attention: don't worry about gross margin, worry about operating margin. That is defensible in isolation — dollars banked matter more than percentages — but Hock Tan had spent prior calls insisting margins were strong and rising.
	- **Why the pairing matters**: the entire MediaTek bear case was that once a customer accumulates enough IP to do more of its own tooling, it stops paying Broadcom's margins, which are the highest in custom silicon. Naming the competitor and abandoning the margin defense in the same call is the shape that thesis would take if it were becoming true.
	- Goldberg built a spreadsheet comparing **operating leverage — revenue growth against operating income growth — for Broadcom versus Nvidia**. The result was **a tie**: both are growing revenue much faster than expenses. The sector wants a Broadcom-vs-Nvidia winner; on this metric there isn't one.
- ## Why custom ASIC is becoming harder to model than merchant GPU
	- **The old method was clean**: identify who won a program (TPU, say), assume they take the whole thing, assign a dollar value.
	- **What broke it**: programs now unbundle across **compute tile, networking, IO, and front-side/back-side**, with different vendors winning different pieces. Google runs two chips and will "probably do three or four"; most customers will eventually run more than one program. Marvell's language about its Google opportunity was described as deliberately precise — it does not necessarily have the compute tile and is winning on networking plus attach "different than what a lot of people are probably picturing."
	- **Attach margin is itself a stack**, and mix determines the margin profile: **Ethernet attach < copper attach < optical attach**. Modeling a program now requires assuming both who won which tile *and* what the attach mix is.
	- Conclusion, and it inverts the usual framing: "you have to make a wider variety of assumptions now if you're doing custom ASIC modeling, probably than even merchant for Nvidia and AMD." The predicted consequence is "150% market share kind of calculations" — a sector where the bottom-up sums stop adding to the top-down.
	- **The practical read**: dispersion in custom-silicon estimates should widen, and surprises should become two-sided rather than uniformly positive. Programs where the market has assumed a single winner are the ones most likely to be mispriced.
- ## Reporting opacity is rising on both sides of the comparison
	- Broadcom's custom business has always included **custom Tomahawk and custom networking** for hyperscalers' own racks — work with nothing to do with XPU attach — and it is all reported as one line. Much of what the street historically booked as hyperscaler ASIC revenue may have been switch silicon.
	- On the other side, **Nvidia no longer breaks out networking revenue**.
	- The hosts stop short of alleging gamesmanship but make the structural point: "the ability to play games has increased. You can mix and match your margin profiles."
	- **The under-appreciated casualty is Broadcom itself.** Its networking franchise — Tomahawk, lasers, optical, with arguably only Marvell competing — gets absorbed into an "ASIC" line and loses its identity as a separately excellent business. Genuine product advantage becomes invisible in the reported number.
- ## The bottleneck trade is over
	- The first half of 2026 was the bottleneck trade: find who has capacity, who benefits, who can raise price. The hosts declare it **fully played out** — "we've turned over every rock, we've searched under every couch cushion, and we know where all the bottlenecks are."
	- What replaces it is **tactical, individual-company analysis**: "you could have made money last year just buying anything. Now you're going to have to be a lot pickier."
	- Supply is the universal constraint across everything reported that week — Dell, HP, Broadcom, and Nvidia the week before all said growth is supply-constrained. **"The industry can only scale as its deepest bottleneck"**: more memory and more GPUs are irrelevant if substrates, MLCCs, or wafer test are short. Michael Dell reportedly listed constraints "all the way into T-glass."
- ## 2027 as peak constraint — the call and the counter
	- **Bajarin's thesis**: 2027 is the peak year of constraints and relief does not arrive until 2028. The reasoning is a timeline argument, not a demand argument. Suppliers — substrates, power and analog, foundry, memory — needed confidence to commit greenfield capacity. That confidence came from long-term agreements signed by Broadcom, Nvidia and others only **8–12 months ago, in late 2025 and early 2026**. Back out normal build timelines and **none of it lands in 2027.**
	- He sizes the gap: industry average capacity supply-to-demand imbalance is currently **120–150%**, higher in places like Southeast Asia, and could improve to **105–110%** — "significant volume relief," but still short of parity. 2028 would then bring "a meaningful amount more capacity than the semiconductor industry has ever seen before." His claim is that this is **"not appreciated, modeled or priced in"** for 2028–29.
	- **Goldberg takes the other side, and accepts the same timing.** If 2028 brings large capacity additions, pricing negotiations turn — downward. His evidence is memory: **units have been flat this year while revenue soared, so price drove all of it.** If units start rising, price is the variable that gives. Micron is building **seven fabs**; Samsung and SK hynix are both expanding; **TSMC has 20 fabs under construction** across nodes and geographies including some packaging.
	- Bajarin does not defend current memory economics: lifetime average gross margin was 10–15%, "80s is rough," and he would prefer higher volume at steadier margins. Bears on the LTA-floor arithmetic in [[2026-08-24-dram-feed-nand-price-vs-volume-hbm4-yield-convergence]] and the supply-response framing in [[2026-06-03-dram-nand-supply-response-and-cycle]].
	- **Both positions are internally consistent and cannot both be traded.** The shared premise — a step-change in 2028 supply — is bullish volume and bearish price; which dominates depends entirely on demand elasticity at lower prices, which neither host claims to know.
- ## Constraints are what is preventing the classic overbuild
	- Every prior capital-intensive infrastructure boom — cell phones, 3G, railroads, canals — ended in overbuilding. "We collectively, humans, always overbuilt in these times."
	- **What is different now is that the industry is physically unable to build as much as it wants.** The constraints are therefore "in a very sort of backhanded positive way... probably a good thing in that they limit some of the excesses."
	- The demand-side version is the sharper one: **token cost is metering enterprise deployment.** "If token costs were low, they would deploy, they would start doing more of these productions to work out their kinks," and compute demand would be "vastly imbalanced." Scarcity pricing is suppressing latent demand rather than clearing it — which means falling prices release demand rather than signalling its absence.
- ## Nvidia–Hugging Face: strategic, not economic
	- Nvidia is acquiring Hugging Face for **almost \$13B**, landing during another Chinese open-model panic — "we have these every three or four months."
	- Goldberg's read: **"Not quite clear to me what benefit it brings to them directly... I don't think it's going to generate huge economic returns, but I think it's a pretty important thing to own strategically."** It plants a flag at the center of the AI software conversation.
	- **The monetization trap is the substance**: the asset's value depends on staying open and free, which is exactly what prevents monetizing it. Closing and monetizing is "certainly what maybe an Oracle would do. I don't see Nvidia going down that path." Both companies have publicly denied that open models will be locked to Nvidia.
	- **The overlooked point**: "almost all of Nvidia's competition also benefits from open models winning." AMD in particular needs open weights, because parity of model access reduces the contest to **pure TCO** — which is the terrain Nvidia would least prefer. Owning Hugging Face does not fix that. Complements [[2026-08-28-ainews-nvidia-huggingface-open-model-specs-agent-security]].
	- On open versus closed, both hosts are unequivocal: neither wins, both persist, and **"the tools we use will route between the two."** The routing layer, not the model, is where the question resolves.
- ## The case for frontier models is a cybersecurity case
	- The cleanest formulation in the episode, from Goldberg: **"you'll need state-of-the-art to defend against state-of-the-art minus one."**
	- Bajarin's version: **"open models are not going to be your defenses against rogue agents, rogue nations, and any rogue players."** CISOs describe the exposure as anyone anywhere having access to frontier-class open weights, which forces enterprises to buy something reliably better.
	- **The distribution evidence supports it**: "that's the reason why Fable and Mythos and Sol and now Astra and every model goes to enterprises and the government first."
	- Where frontier economics hold: **mission-critical functions — cybersecurity and engineering** — where "high value, high margins will accrue to the most valuable, most important mission-critical workloads." That is a narrower but more defensible frontier-lab thesis than general capability leadership, and it is the one consistent with the commoditization pressure tracked elsewhere in this repo.
	- The sovereignty layer: only two countries are seriously competing, and governments want frontier capability domestically **and** want more than one provider — "we don't want it to be a monopoly... you need there to be diversity there." Bajarin flags "very big budgets coming" for AI cybersecurity in enterprise software.
- ## Governed agents: the gap nobody has filled
	- **The anecdote is the memo-worthy part.** Goldberg had Claude build a valuation model; it flagged a **\$140M accounting charge** he had not included and offered handling options — one of which was to skip it. His reaction: "if I were a company and I just picked skip, don't mention the \$140 million accounting charge, that's fraud."
	- The issue is not that the model erred. It surfaced an omission correctly and then **presented the non-disclosure path as a menu option**. In a regulated reporting workflow that is a control failure, not a capability failure.
	- Bajarin's product conclusion is the actionable one: an ordinary knowledge worker **should not choose a model**. The enterprise approves models and the system **routes**; the user just works. What is missing is **permissioned and governed AI** — file changes gated by permission so an auditable record exists of who had authority to change what.
	- His framing of the current state: **"there's a big difference between agents running wild in an enterprise, which is what it is today, and governed production, which we're barely scratching the surface of."** Same gap Brockman names from the vendor side in [[2026-09-04-stratechery-brockman-astra-computer-use-alignment]] — guardrails, oversight, tracking and observability — which is the clearest software demand pocket either source identifies.
	- Two smaller operating datapoints from their own build: token spend is monitored as a cost line and watched deliberately, with usage treated as the engagement metric; and the hard part of building an agent over a proprietary corpus was **making it refuse** — beta users' first instinct was disbelief that it would not fabricate a house view it did not have.
