- ## Big Tech's \$3T of off-balance-sheet AI commitments — the number, and how to read it
	- **Sources**: (1) *WSJ*, "Why Big Tech's AI Spending Is \$3 Trillion Higher Than It Seems," Peter Rudegeair and Peter Santilli, August 16, 2026 — an analysis of footnotes in the most recent filings of **nine companies**: Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, Nvidia, Broadcom, SpaceX, AMD. (2) **Olga Usvyatsky**, "Behind Big Tech's AI Commitments," August 24, 2026, a forensic-accounting response (partially paywalled in the copy read). **The pairing is the value**: WSJ supplies the number, Usvyatsky supplies the framework for interpreting it, and they disagree about what it means.
	- **Thesis**: The \$3T is real, disclosed, and GAAP-compliant — **it is not Enron, and the framing that invites that comparison is the weakest part of the coverage.** The genuinely investable problems are narrower and sharper: the off-balance-sheet figures are **undiscounted and lack maturity schedules** while the on-balance-sheet ones are present values, so the two are not comparable; **growth rates are extraordinary and unexplained**; and cancelability is real but expensive and almost entirely undisclosed.
- ## The numbers, which reconcile exactly
	- **Across the nine companies**: **~\$3 trillion** of off-balance-sheet commitments, against **~\$600B** of trailing capex — **5×** — and "about triple" what they owe under outstanding leases and long-term borrowings. Uncommenced leases alone total **\$1.2T, roughly four times the prior year's disclosure**; purchase obligations total **\$1.9T**.
	- **For the four companies WSJ breaks out** (all \$B, most recent quarterly filing):
	  | Company | On-BS lease liabilities | On-BS long-term debt | **Leases not started** | **Purchase commitments** |
	  |--------------|------------------------|----------------------|------------------------|--------------------------|
	  | **Alphabet** | — | **100.2** | **91.0** | **811.0** |
	  | **Meta** | — | **83.7** | **347.0** | **349.3** |
	  | **Microsoft** | **88.5** | **40.3** | **329.1** | **228.6** |
	  | **Amazon** | **109.8** | **132.2** | **137.2** | **130.1** |
	  | **Total** | **\$248B** | **\$356B** | **\$904B** | **\$1.52T** |
	  - Both off-balance-sheet columns sum exactly to WSJ's stated totals. **For these four the off-balance-sheet stack is \$2.42T against \$604B on-balance-sheet — 4.0×.**
	- **Meta's figures confirm what is already on file.** [[2026-08-03-stratechery-meta-timing-problems-financial-tail]] recorded \$349.3B of non-cancelable contractual commitments and \$347B of leases not yet commenced from the 10-Q — **identical to WSJ's line items**, which is a clean cross-source validation of both.
- ## Growth is the story, not the level
	- Year-over-year change in off-balance-sheet obligations versus capex growth, read from the WSJ chart:
	  | Company | Off-BS obligations | Capex |
	  |--------------|--------------------|--------|
	  | **Alphabet** | **~+840%** | ~+90% |
	  | **Meta** | **~+760%** | ~+60% |
	  | **Oracle** | **~+530%** | ~+155% |
	  | **Nvidia** | **~+265%** | ~+55% |
	  | Microsoft | ~+135% | ~+75% |
	  | Amazon | ~+90% | ~+60% |
	- **The single most striking disclosure item is Alphabet's**: purchase commitments went from **\$332B to \$811B in one quarter — +\$479B, or +144%** — and the company **did not explain why**. It describes them as relating to "technical infrastructure and inventory" and "agreements to secure energy for data center usage," with energy obligations running **as far out as 2054**.
	- **Nvidia's entry is a different animal and worth separating**: it committed to **\$27B of equity investments** between late April and the end of its January 2027 fiscal year. That is not capacity procurement — it is the vendor-financing instrument analysed as an off-margin price cut in [[2026-08-21-ben-thompson-ilb-risk-relocation-commodity-logic]], now showing up in the commitments footnote.
- ## Why the Enron framing is wrong — and what the real problem is
	- **Usvyatsky's correction is the most useful thing in either piece.** Off-balance-sheet here **does not mean the accounting is wrong or violates GAAP**. These obligations sit off the balance sheet because the rules do not require recognition until conditions are met — a lease commences, or goods are delivered. **Nor are they hidden**: WSJ compiled the entire analysis from the companies' own SEC filings, where the commitments are disclosed in footnotes and MD&A.
	- **The distinction that matters**: Enron used off-balance-sheet entities **to hide debt and losses and mislead investors**. These commitments are disclosed and remain off balance sheet because GAAP does not yet require them as liabilities. Her view is that **"loosely-defined metrics in AI-related monetization narratives are more likely to lead to inflated valuations"** than footnoted commitments that may not fully materialize — i.e. **watch the revenue claims, not the liability footnotes.**
	- That said, she is explicit that **disclosure should be improved** and that if the AI boom ends badly these commitments will make losses larger. The problem is **inadequate specificity, not concealment.**
- ## The four questions — the reusable framework
	- Usvyatsky's test for any headline commitment figure, which is the part worth carrying into every future filing read:
		- **Comparability** — do "purchase commitments" mean the same thing at Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, and Meta, or are they different definitions capturing different contract types?
		- **Cancelability** — how binding in practice? What terms reduce or eliminate the obligation before payment, and **have those rights ever been used?**
		- **Timing** — how much is due in the next year or two versus stretching decades out?
		- **Time value of money** — how different would these look **discounted**, particularly against obligations already measured on a present-value basis?
- ## The discounting asymmetry — the flaw in the headline comparison
	- **This is the technical crux and it is quantifiable.** Under **ASC 842**, a lease moves onto the balance sheet when the property becomes available for use, recorded at the **present value** of payments — and commenced leases require a **maturity schedule** showing undiscounted payments for each of the next five years plus a total thereafter. **Before commencement there is no asset or liability**, and the disclosure requirement is far looser: information about leases creating significant rights and obligations, **with no equivalent five-year maturity schedule**.
	- **So the \$904B of uncommenced leases is undiscounted and undated, while the \$248B of lease liabilities is a discounted present value.** Stacking them in one chart overstates the off-balance-sheet side by construction.
	- **Illustrative magnitude** (my calculation, using a flat payment profile purely to size the effect):
	  | Assumed term | At 5% | At 7% |
	  |--------------|--------------|--------------|
	  | 15 years | **\$626B (69%)** | **\$549B (61%)** |
	  | 20 years | **\$563B (62%)** | **\$479B (53%)** |
	  - **On plausible assumptions, present-value framing removes 30–47% of the uncommenced lease figure.** Applied loosely to the \$1.2T nine-company total, the economically comparable number is closer to **\$650–850B**. The same logic bites harder on purchase commitments running to **2054**.
	- **The practical rule**: never compare an undiscounted commitments footnote to a discounted balance-sheet liability. **If you want one number, discount the footnote — or compare undiscounted to undiscounted using the maturity schedule that only commenced leases provide.**
- ## Cancelability — real, expensive, and almost entirely undisclosed
	- **The one public contract example is instructive.** Cipher Digital signed a **15-year lease with Amazon Data Services** (October 2025) for the **Black Pearl** data center in Texas — **300 MW gross / 216 MW IT load**, roughly **\$5.5B of lease payments** over the initial term, triple-net, with **Amazon.com guaranteeing** payments and Cipher raising **\$2B** to build it.
		- **Amazon has no right to terminate for convenience** after final rent commencement — but **can** terminate on specified conditions: a **120-day delivery delay** plus 30-day cure, casualty, condemnation, material interference, certain property sales, anti-bribery violations, or **a grid-power outage exceeding 180 consecutive days**.
		- **A useful derived benchmark**: \$5.5B over 216 IT MW is **~\$25.5M per IT MW across 15 years, or ~\$1.7M per MW per year** for the shell and power. Set against the **\$10–15M/MW/year** all-in compute cost in [[2026-08-29-dylan-patel-dwarkesh-revenue-per-megawatt-compute-centralization]], **the real estate is roughly a tenth of the total** — which is why Hood's argument that land and shells are the flexible, smaller part of the cost structure holds up.
	- **Termination is not hypothetical.** In February 2025, TD Cowen channel checks found **Microsoft had terminated data center leases with at least two private operators**, totalling "a couple of hundred megawatts," **using facility and power delays as justification** — a tactic Meta had previously used when curbing metaverse-era spending. **Important qualification** (Mizuho, via Bloomberg): some of those cancelled leases **may never have been signed**, and unsigned leases are neither disclosed under ASC 842 nor counted in the WSJ total.
- ## Two company-specific disclosure findings
	- **Microsoft is the outlier, in a way that favours it.** For the first time it discloses that some of its **\$329.1B** of non-commenced datacenter leases are **"subject to certain contractual conditions being met."** It does not say what the conditions are, what portion they affect, or **what happens if they are not met**. Usvyatsky could not find comparable language at Alphabet, Meta, or Amazon.
		- **How to read it**: this is *more* disclosure than peers, not less — it flags that some commitments are contingent. It does not prove peers lack similar conditions, only that they do not say. **The asymmetry is in disclosure, not necessarily in the contracts.**
		- Worth pairing with the useful-life change in [[MSFT-2026-Q2]]: extending data-center life from 15 to 25 years shifts **future** leases from finance to operating classification, which removes them from reported capex — while they would still surface in the uncommenced-lease footnote. **Two different accounting seams, both reducing the visibility of the same spending.**
	- **Meta's Hyperion illustrates the guarantee problem.** The Louisiana project (~1,700 football fields) carries an initial **\$12.3B lease commitment** with rent starting **2029**, an initial **four-year term** and renewal options to **20 years**. Meta **guaranteed to make bondholders whole** if it does not stay the full two decades, and **records no liability** because it does not consider payment probable — the GAAP test. **Meta can terminate or decline to renew, but that flexibility is priced through a residual value guarantee protecting investors if the data centers lose value.**
		- **The asymmetry to hold**: once leases commence, obligations move onto the balance sheet — **but the termination rights, and the substantial cost of exercising them, do not disappear.** A residual-value guarantee is precisely the instrument that converts apparent flexibility into a contingent liability that is invisible until it is probable.
- ## What to actually do with the \$3T
	- **Do not use it as a leverage figure.** It mixes discounted and undiscounted, cancellable and non-cancellable, near-dated and 2054-dated obligations across nine companies with non-uniform definitions. Morgan Stanley's April framing is the fair one: **"it is becoming increasingly difficult for investors to assess companies' total potential leverage."**
	- **Do use the growth rates.** An 840% increase in Alphabet's obligations against 90% capex growth is a real signal about forward commitment regardless of definitional issues, and **the \$332B → \$811B single-quarter jump with no explanation is a legitimate disclosure complaint.**
	- **The cash-flow context is what makes it bite.** WSJ notes **Alphabet and Amazon have posted negative free cash flow**, with estimates showing Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta negative into 2027 — consistent with Meta's \$784M FCF in [[META-2026-Q2]] and with the observation in the Dylan Patel memo that **hyperscalers no longer generate free cash and are funding capex with debt**. Commitments that cannot be cancelled, against businesses no longer self-funding, is the actual risk — not the accounting treatment.
	- **Extends rather than duplicates** [[2026-06-10-morgan-stanley-hyperscaler-capex-accounting-risk]], [[2026-07-21-bloomberg-big-tech-ai-off-balance-sheet-financing]], and [[2026-07-20-nikkei-hyperscaler-ai-shadow-borrowing]] — this pair adds the **company-level breakdown**, the **ASC 842 discounting asymmetry**, and the **cancelability evidence**, which those did not have.
