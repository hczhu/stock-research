- **Source**: Two slides screenshotted from a recorded talk titled "AI for BC IC 2026-09" (15:09 long, ~25K views), at 0:45 and 0:57. The presenter and the organization behind "BC IC" are not identified in the screenshots. **These are the opening framing slides of a 15-minute talk, not its argument** — treat them as the premise the rest of the talk presumably defends.

- **Thesis**: The talk's opening claim is that AI's addressable market is **services, not software** — a **\$10–20T** pool against a **\$650B** software TAM, i.e. **15–31× larger**. The question marks on the slide are the honest part: the presenter is unsure of the size to within 2×. The investable question is not the TAM but the capture rate, because AI that replaces labour sells the outcome at a fraction of the labour's price.

- ## Slide 1: "Waves of Technology"

	- | Decade | Wave | Winners shown |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | 1960s | Semiconductors | Fairchild, National Semiconductor, Intel |
	  | 1970s | Systems | Apple, Microsoft, Oracle |
	  | 1980s | Networks | Cisco, Compaq, Dell, Autodesk, Adobe |
	  | 1990s | Internet | Amazon, Yahoo, eBay, Google |
	  | 2000s | Apps | AWS, Facebook, PayPal, YouTube, Salesforce, ServiceNow |
	  | 2010s | Mobile | Airbnb, Uber, DoorDash, WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok |
	  | 2020s | AI | OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Gemini |

	- The visual argument is one wave per decade, each **stacked on the previous**, each with its own new winners. The chart's own AI row undercuts the "new wave, new winners" reading: **Gemini is Google's**, a 1990s-wave incumbent, and three of the 2000s "Apps" winners (AWS, YouTube, and Facebook's successor Meta) are the largest AI capex spenders. The prior-wave winners are financing this one.

	- It is also a survivorship chart. Compaq, Yahoo, Fairchild and National Semiconductor were all absorbed or faded; each row shows who won the wave, not whether winning it compounded.

- ## Slide 2: "1: Biggest"

	- | Market | Size on slide | Multiple of software |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | Software TAM | **\$650B** | 1× |
	  | Services TAM | **\$10T?** or **\$20T?** | **15×–31×** |

	- The headline "1" implies this is the first of several reasons AI is the biggest wave — sized against labour spend rather than software spend.

	- **Capture rate is the missing variable.** If AI delivers services work at, say, a tenth of the labour cost, a \$10–20T pool becomes a \$1–2T revenue opportunity — still **1.5–3× all of software today**, but an order of magnitude below the slide's headline, and the difference accrues to customers as deflation rather than to vendors as revenue.

	- **Scale anchor from the repo**: India's entire software and ITES exports were **US\$221B** in FY26 per [[2026-09-18-rbi-india-software-ites-exports-fy26]] — about **2% of the low-end \$10T figure**. The offshore IT-services industry most often cited as "AI's first victim" is a rounding error inside the TAM the slide claims; most of that \$10–20T is in-house and domestic labour that has never been outsourced at all.

	- For software incumbents the framing cuts both ways: [[$CRM]] and [[$NOW]] sit in the \$650B pool the slide calls small, and their upside depends on converting seat revenue into outcome revenue before a labs-first or services-first entrant does.
