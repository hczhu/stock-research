- ## Stratechery: Apple settles with the E.U. and proposes U.S. fees, and Germany unwinds ATT
	- **Source**: Stratechery Update, Ben Thompson, August 19, 2026, aggregating Bloomberg (E.U. settlement), TechCrunch (U.S. court filing), the *Financial Times* (Services impact), Reuters and the **Bundeskartellamt** press release (ATT commitments).
	- **Thesis**: Three separate proceedings converge on one number — **App Store presence is worth 15% of app revenue** — and on one structural conclusion: **Apple is losing the payments rake globally** while retaining a small, newly legitimised claim on IP. The second-order item is arguably larger for the ad complex than for Apple: Germany has forced ATT consent prompts to become **neutral**, partially unwinding what Thompson calls one of the most egregious anti-competitive acts in tech history.
- ## The E.U. fee schedule, and what it implies component by component
	- Apple drops per-install fees and **replaces the Core Technology Fee with a 5% commission on digital transactions in apps distributed outside the platform**. The resulting schedule:
	  | Distribution and payment path | Commission |
	  |---------------------------------------------|--------------|
	  | App Store, Apple in-app payments | **26%** (15% for subscriptions after year one) — down from 30% |
	  | App Store, developer's own payments | **20%** |
	  | App Store, linking out to the web | **15%** |
	  | Distributed outside the App Store | **5%** |
	- **Thompson's decomposition is the reusable asset here** — the schedule is a set of simultaneous equations, and solving it prices each component of the platform separately:
	  | Component | Implied value |
	  |-------------------------------------------------|----------------|
	  | Apple's APIs (the "Core Technology Commission") | **5%** |
	  | App Store distribution | **10%** |
	  | In-app payment APIs for third-party processors | **5%** |
	  | Apple's own payment processing | **6%** |
	  - The arithmetic closes exactly: 5 (outside) → 15 (APIs + distribution) → 20 (+ payment APIs) → 26 (+ processing). **Caveat Thompson attaches**: because the European Commission blessed these figures, they were almost certainly negotiated rather than derived — treat as directionally right, not as a measurement.
	- **The 6% is the number that matters.** A fully loaded cost of card acceptance — swipe plus percentage fees, refunds, disputes — lands near 6%. So the Commission has effectively decided **Apple should earn nothing for processing payments**. The reasoning is legible (anyone can charge money; only Apple built the APIs and the store), but Thompson's counter is the substantive one: **Apple's payment rails may be the most valuable part of the App Store** — no payments to handle, cards on file, high consumer trust — and those benefits were minimised to roughly zero **because they are hard to quantify**. That is a systematic bias worth naming: regulatory value allocation rewards what is measurable.
	- **Where the Commission gave ground, and why it may matter more than the money**: Apple still collects **5% on apps distributed entirely outside the App Store**. The DMA can be read as barring any commission on third-party distribution; Apple argued its IP should not be expropriated by fiat and won the principle. Thompson judges **the recognition worth more to Apple than the revenue**, which will be minuscule — while noting it sounds like an administrative nightmare for everyone. **Read-through: this is the precedent that caps the downside case globally** — it establishes that Apple's platform IP is compensable even where Apple provides neither distribution nor payments.
- ## The U.S. proposal — the same 15% anchor
	- Filed in the U.S. District Court for Northern California: **15% standard**, **5% for small-business developers**, **10%** for Video, News, and Mini Apps Partner Program members, and **10% on subscription renewals**.
	- Procedural context matters for the timing: Apple tried to defer this filing pending a Supreme Court ruling on whether it was **in contempt** for imposing a 27% commission on external-link purchases and restricting how developers could present those links. **The Supreme Court refused to pause the lower-court case**, forcing disclosure. This is a proposal, not a negotiated outcome.
	- **The convergence is the signal.** The small-business discount appears in the E.U. agreement too, and the headline rate matches the E.U.'s link-out tier. Thompson: it is no accident — *"being in the App Store is worth 15% of your revenue."* **Apple is laying the framework to bring App Store fees down globally**, and 15% is the number to anchor any take-rate model on.
- ## The measured financial damage, and the causality question
	- | Metric | Reading |
	  |------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
	  | U.S. consumer spending through the App Store, Q2 | **−6%**, versus **+9% growth** a year earlier (Sensor Tower) — a ~15-point swing |
	  | Apple's U.S. commission revenue, year to date | **−18%** (Appfigures) |
	  | Services revenue and margin | **Below Wall Street expectations** |
	  | Regulatory filings | First acknowledgment that Apple **"may not earn a commission at all"** on purchases via alternative payment systems |
	  - The FT frames this as the first time Apple has conceded that regulation is denting its **>\$100bn Services business** — the clearest sign yet that antitrust action is eroding a core high-margin division.
	- **The most valuable original thought in the piece is the challenge to causality.** Thompson believes the trend but wants the mechanism established: are customers shifting to web payments, **or are they simply spending less time in paid apps** — more on short-form video, free games, and AI? He points at the tell: *"Meta's revenue is still rising significantly; the time fueling the simultaneous rise in inventory and price-per-ad is coming from something, and are we sure it's not Apple?"*
		- **Why this distinction is worth money**: if the decline is **payment leakage**, it is a one-time re-basing of take rate onto a still-growing spend base. If it is **attention reallocation**, the App Store's underlying volume is shrinking and the problem is secular and larger.
		- It is also directly testable against work already on file: the simultaneous rise in impressions **and** price-per-ad is the "extraordinary state" in Thompson's own Meta diagnostic recorded in [[2026-08-03-stratechery-meta-timing-problems-financial-tail]] and [[META-2026-Q2]]. **If Meta's incremental inventory is coming out of App Store engagement, the two datasets are describing one migration**, and Apple's Services problem is partly a Meta win rather than purely a regulatory one.
	- Thompson's normative read, worth recording because it is unusually unsympathetic to Apple: he does not feel bad if the Services narrative takes a hit, because *"for too long the payoff for great products of the past has been an ongoing tax on developers"* — and more pressure to ship new products in the AI age is a good thing. Compare the margin pressure from memory costs in [[AAPL-2026-Q2]]: **Services take-rate compression and hardware input-cost inflation are now hitting simultaneously.**
- ## Germany forces ATT prompts to become neutral — the item with the ad-complex read-through
	- The Bundeskartellamt closed a **years-long** investigation, finding that Apple's App Tracking Transparency framework gave **Apple's own apps more favourable consent prompts** than third-party developers'. Its preliminary assessment was that competition law permits even powerful firms to protect user privacy, but that the differences **exceeded what could be justified** by differences in the types of data processing.
	- **The specific findings**, which are the substance: the wording, design, and selection options in Apple's own prompts **had the potential to encourage consent**, while the prompts Apple prescribed for third parties **had the potential to discourage it** — and third-party apps sometimes had to request consent **repeatedly even after users had already given data-protection-compliant consent**.
	- **The binding commitments**:
		- Consent prompts for Apple's own offerings and for third parties to be **aligned much more closely**, with possibly discouraging symbols and wording removed; design to be **neutral in content, wording, and layout**.
		- App publishers and content providers given **more scope to explain what personalised advertising means for their offering and business model** — i.e. developers may now argue the value exchange.
		- **Reduced complexity** in the consent architecture, with more freedom to **combine or connect Apple's required prompt with the consent requests required under data-protection law**.
		- The regulator explicitly notes the improved conditions **may also benefit advertisers and technical service providers to the advertising industry**.
	- **This is the most underpriced item in the Update.** ATT's damage ran through opt-in rates, and every commitment above targets opt-in rate directly: neutral framing, no discouraging design, no repeated prompting, and the right to explain why consent matters. **Any durable lift in iOS opt-in rates flows to Meta and the ad-tech complex**, and re-opens the share-shift question tracked in [[2026-03-28-meta-ios-att-and-applovin-share-shift]].
	- **Two limits to hold against it**: the commitments are **German**, not EU-wide, though they are precedent-setting and hard to implement locally in a global OS; and **no opt-in rate improvement has been measured** — the mechanism is plausible, the magnitude is unknown. Thompson's own view is that ATT as implemented was *"one of the most egregious anti-competitive acts in tech history,"* and he notes the article making that case opened with the announcement of this very investigation — **four years ago**.
- ## The regulatory-timing argument, which is the memo's transferable idea
	- Thompson's closing point generalises beyond Apple: four years to resolve ATT reflects the E.U.'s current eagerness to regulate AI **early**. He is sympathetic to the impulse but draws the opposite lesson from the App Store — which was *"incredibly innovative and fruitful for everyone involved for many years"* before the abuses accumulated.
	- **The formulation worth keeping**: he is fine with crackdowns on abuse taking time **if that delay is what allowed the innovation to happen first**; the concern with the AI Act is that eagerness to set rules **prevents anything interesting enough to regulate from being built at all**.
	- **How to use it**: platform regulation reliably arrives late and then re-prices the incumbent's take rate — as it is doing here, from 30% toward 15%. That lag is the window in which platform economics are actually earned. For AI, the same lag is being deliberately compressed, which shifts where in the stack durable rents can form — and argues for weighting jurisdictions and layers where the rule-setting is slowest.
