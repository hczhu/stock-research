- ## China’s Mega Data Centers: Inner Mongolia’s Integrated Buildout
	- **Source**: Everlyn Tan, Jeremie Eliahou Ontiveros, Xiaopeng Laoban, and Dylan Patel, SemiAnalysis, “We Visited China’s Stargate in Inner Mongolia,” November 7, 2026; user-provided text.
	- **Thesis**: The report’s contrast is a different delivery system, not simply larger buildings: Inner Mongolia combines low-cost power, abundant construction labor, domestic equipment, dedicated renewable generation, storage, DC power, and climate-specific cooling. U.S. campuses more commonly rely on grid-plus-onsite generation, AC/UPS infrastructure, powered-shell delivery, and chiller capacity retained for tenant flexibility. These advantages are local and execution-dependent; the report does not establish that every Chinese campus is cheaper or more reliable than a U.S. peer.

- ## Galaxy Scale and Build Economics
	- Galaxy, Envision’s campus in Ulanqab, is planned for **800MW of IT capacity** across four phases; the first **120MW** phase was accepted in August 2026. The report says this phase was delivered in under a year and at **one-fifth of U.S. costs**, but the supplied article gives no like-for-like cost methodology for that comparison.
	- The scale is comparable to the core of Crusoe’s Abilene campus: the report cites **about 820MW of critical IT capacity** within an 1.2GW power campus. Abilene draws on the grid and onsite gas turbines; Galaxy is designed around wind and storage. The article also notes possible later expansion at both sites, so these are not necessarily final campus capacities.
	- | Operating datapoint | China / Galaxy or Inner Mongolia | U.S. comparison in the report |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | Construction timeline | About 4 months from land-process start to groundbreaking, then 10–11 months to customer acceptance; top-out can take 8–10 weeks | The report also calls the first 120MW phase a sub-12-month delivery, but the supplied text does not reconcile that claim with the longer land-to-acceptance sequence |
	  | Labor and construction style | A 100–120MW site uses roughly 3,000–4,000 peak workers; conventional concrete construction is common because labor is plentiful and inexpensive | Similar-sized U.S. sites use 3–5x fewer workers; modular construction is more attractive where labor is scarcer. This comparison is the report’s generalization, not a site-by-site survey |
	  | Domestic sourcing | New Chinese facilities source roughly **80–90%** of equipment domestically, up from about **50% in 2021** | China still imports some HVAC compressors/fans, main breakers, and many diesel generators; domestic sourcing is not complete self-sufficiency |
	  | Local market economics | Inner Mongolia power is about **\$0.055/kWh all-in**; rents are about **\$40/kW/month excluding power**, roughly half their level five years earlier; capex is down about **25%** to under **\$3M/MW** | The report presents low-cost power, equipment localization, and construction as structural pressures on global colocation pricing, but does not supply a matched U.S. rent/capex series |
	- Plenty of labor also changes the modularization trade-off. Some Chinese tenants now request T+4 delivery; highly prefabricated builds can save up to **3 months** but cost **5–10%** more. Galaxy used prefabrication selectively, including low-voltage skids. U.S. hyperscalers more often lease a powered shell and fit out interior power and cooling themselves; Chinese developers typically hand over a turnkey hall to the tenant’s standards.
	- Better electrical and cooling design increases saleable IT capacity from the same grid allocation: the report estimates a 100MW grid connection historically supported **67–68MW** of critical IT load, versus roughly **70–72MW** now. This matters especially where China’s power quota, rather than land, limits project size.

- ## Power: China’s Earlier DC Adoption and the 800V Pilot
	- China’s power path differs from the U.S. baseline: the report estimates roughly **one-quarter of Chinese data centers built in the past decade** use DC power, while about **97% of U.S. data centers** still use AC UPS systems. Telecom operators piloted 240V DC in 2007; Alibaba’s Panama system later combined 10kV distribution and rectification into a prefabricated 240V DC unit. Alibaba reported **366 units and over 800MW installed by March 2023**; Delta reported more than 500 units across operators by August 2024, implying over **1GW**.
	- Galaxy advances that installed base with a **5MW** solid-state-transformer pilot taking 10kV AC directly to **800V DC at the rack**. Envision says the system exceeds **98.5% efficiency** and cuts footprint by more than half versus conventional equipment. It is live at a hyperscaler campus but remains a small pilot; it costs more than a conventional system today, with wider deployment planned for the next phase.
	- Galaxy’s first two phases target more than **60% wind-supplied power**. Envision’s wind farms total **420MW** against a **240MW** campus load and pair geographically separated sites with a four-hour battery. By contrast, the U.S. example Abilene combines grid power with onsite gas turbines. The difference reflects resource siting and infrastructure history as much as technology choice.
	- The U.S. industry is also moving toward 800V DC for higher-density racks: Nvidia’s future Kyber racks are cited at around **600kW**. Chinese AI racks are not yet at that density; the report argues China is advancing because it already has DC distribution experience, not because current rack density forces the transition.

- ## Cooling and Climate Fit
	- Galaxy runs a shared water loop for liquid- and air-cooled equipment without a central chiller plant. Its first phase delivered **30MW of liquid-cooled capacity**; the liquid side can carry up to **80%** of heat and the air side up to **50%**, allowing the mix of rack types to change while retaining flexibility.
	- Liquid cooling water runs at up to **35°C supply / 43°C return**. Ulanqab’s average temperature is about **4°C**, enabling dry coolers to provide free cooling for nearly **10 months a year**. Air-cooled equipment gets local compressor coils inside fan walls, rather than requiring the whole loop to be chilled centrally; the report estimates this costs **10–20% less** than rooftop magnetic-bearing chillers.
	- U.S. designs more often keep central chillers to preserve flexibility across tenants and air-cooled loads, even when liquid-cooled racks could use warmer water. Galaxy’s approach appears most attractive in cold climates; the report says it can work in Texas but compressors would run more often and savings would shrink. Its economics should not be generalized to warmer U.S. markets without climate and tenant-mix adjustments.

- ## Public-Market Read-Through
	- Falling Chinese colocation rents and capex raise the bar for operators: access to power, higher IT yield, and integrated energy/power systems may differentiate capacity more than the shell alone. The article describes CATL, VNET, and Zhongheng Electric as assembling complementary positions in batteries/green power, data-center development, and power systems. It reports CATL became VNET’s largest shareholder and announced a transaction for a **49% stake in the controlling shareholder of Zhongheng Electric**.
	- For VNET, the opportunity is participation in gigawatt-scale campuses and access to CATL’s storage and Zhongheng’s electrical systems; the counterweight is a market where rent has roughly halved and capex is falling, compressing returns unless utilization and power economics compensate.
	- For CATL, stationary storage and power integration extend its battery ecosystem beyond EVs. The report’s thesis is strategic adjacency, not evidence yet of material data-center earnings or attractive project returns; it says project IRRs are thin and some industrial groups pursue infrastructure partly to create demand for existing equipment.
	- Envision is the most integrated case in the article, spanning wind generation, batteries, power electronics, data-center electrical systems, and operations. Its economics show how an energy supplier can monetize a data-center buildout across multiple product lines, but the report says integration has not yet guaranteed hyperscaler orders.
	- **Evidence limits**: The site visit provides detailed project-level observations, but several metrics are Envision, vendor, or local-report figures repeated by SemiAnalysis. Inner Mongolia’s **25GW** of announced projects is a pipeline figure, not commissioned capacity or proof of secured power. The “one-fifth of U.S. cost” claim is not independently comparable from the data supplied; Galaxy’s 5MW 800V system is a pilot, not a scaled fleet result.
