- ## Tibo Sottiaux on Codex — The Harness Shrinks, and Execution Leaves the Laptop
	- **Source**: The Pragmatic Engineer podcast, Gergely Orosz interviewing **Thibault "Tibo" Sottiaux**, who leads the Codex team at OpenAI. Career biography omitted as non-investable. Sponsor reads are advertising, not research, and are segregated below.
	- **Note on provenance**: Sottiaux is the same executive quoted on record in [[2026-06-02-information-inside-openai-codex-chatgpt-combination]]. This is his first-party account of a reorganization the repo previously held only second-hand.
	- **Thesis**: Three structural claims, each from the team that would know. **The harness is deliberately temporary** — it exists to paper over model deficiencies and is designed to shrink as models improve, with an explicit build-versus-wait decision rule. **Code review is already automated at OpenAI**, with security review mandatory and merge-blocking. And **agent execution is migrating off the laptop into cloud VMs**, which converts a free client-side resource into metered inference-adjacent compute.
- ## The harness is scaffolding with a planned demolition date
	- Sottiaux states the relationship plainly: **"the harness, in a sense, is always a little bit ahead of the model."** It supplies "crutches" — guardrails, safety, efficiency, steerability — plus the *developer message* injected into context at the start of each turn.
	- **The direction of travel is the finding**: "over time, what we see is the system — the developer message — shrinks, and then the harness also shrinks." His worked example: early on you had to remind Codex to run tests; they trained a model that reflects on what the user actually wants, and the instruction became unnecessary.
	- **There is an explicit decision rule**, which is the most transferable thing in the interview. For any gap: "should this be a harness change or should this be a model change? If it's a model change, how soon would we have it? Can we have it in a month? Three months? Six months?" If the model will fix it soon enough, **they build nothing and wait.**
	- The internal heuristic that follows: **"if you're building this 10,000 lines of code crutch to work around model flaws, you're probably doing the wrong thing."**
	- **A concrete casualty**: the `/goal` command existed to keep a model on a single objective for long horizons. "With the new generation of models, you don't need slash goal anymore, you don't need a harness around it, you can just tell the model 'go and work for a week' and it'll actually do it."
	- **This independently confirms the scaffolding-depreciation argument in [[2026-09-04-stratechery-brockman-astra-computer-use-alignment]]**, but from the builders rather than the president, and with mechanism attached. Two OpenAI principals describing the same write-down in a week is stronger evidence than either alone.
	- **The host supplies the necessary counterweight**, and it matters for sizing the damage: not everything in a harness is a crutch. Protocols and interfaces — MCP, skills, plugins — are *tools the model uses*, and "it's not like the next version of the model will re-invent MCP." **The depreciating asset is prompt-scaffolding and workaround logic; the durable asset is the protocol layer and the permissions, data and execution substrate around it.** Agent-tooling businesses should be sorted on which side of that line they sit.

- ## Code review is already automated, and security review is merge-blocking
	- OpenAI trained a dedicated **code review model** targeted at logic and reasoning errors — the class requiring "three or four levels deep into the dependencies" to find, where documentation is wrong and a third-party implementation differs from expectation so an invariant silently fails. Sottiaux says catching these would take a human "potentially multiple hours."
	- Those capabilities are **no longer a separate model**; they are "just part of the mainline models." His benchmark claim: **"they're superhuman in code review."**
	- **The operational fact is the strongest datapoint in the interview**: automated security review is **"now mandatory across all of OpenAI pull requests — we block pull requests from merging if we flag them with a security issue, and this is all automatic."** A frontier lab gating its own merge queue on model judgment is a materially higher commitment than recommending the practice.
	- **What survives as human work is narrower than expected and not the code**: the discussion of *intent* — "what are you even trying to do? And is that a right thing to attempt to do?" — which he notes "doesn't have to be around the pull request" at all.
	- The endpoint he describes is contract-based: agree on **"the box"** and its invariants — resource utilization, data access, security — after which "what happens inside the box could be literally anything, you don't really need to care."
	- **Read-through**: the correctness-and-security tier of code review is being absorbed into the model layer and given away, while the surviving value is specification and invariant design. That compresses standalone AI-code-review products toward feature status and argues the durable layer is policy, audit and merge-gate enforcement rather than finding the bug.
- ## Execution is migrating off the laptop
	- Default today is **fully local and sandboxed**, with any command needing elevated permissions prompting the user. Cloud is now selectable, running in a managed VM — the same VM used by ChatGPT Work.
	- **The stated direction is unambiguous and is the demand argument**: "as models get better and more capable, they can leverage so much more compute and many more resources than are available on your local machine. And so it would be a constraint, at some point, to just limit execution on your local machine."
	- **Cloud dev environments are being revived by the thing that killed them.** Sottiaux's diagnosis of why they failed pre-AI outside big tech: a large up-front setup cost plus ongoing maintenance, with little payoff for solo developers or small teams. His claim now: **"the setup is almost free"** because the agent configures and syncs the environment itself. He expects "a resurgence of fully cloud-orchestrated machines."
	- **This is the same migration argued in [[2026-06-30-openai-anthropic-cursor-cloud-agents-token-economics]]**, by the same interviewer three months earlier, now confirmed by the vendor. The investable consequence: work that ran free on a developer's own hardware becomes **metered cloud compute**, which is incremental demand for CPU, storage and networking alongside inference — and it undercuts any thesis that local execution relieves cloud capacity.
- ## A general-purpose cloud computer inside a \$20 plan
	- The merge required reconciling two incompatible stacks: ChatGPT is "fully managed cloud-based... built for scale, built for efficiency"; Codex was "fully local." The engineering problem was running the full Codex harness in the cloud "together with a cloud computer."
	- **The binding constraint was unit economics, stated directly**: it had to serve "tens and hundreds of millions of users... in a way that is still efficient so that we can include it all the way into the Plus plan" — \$20 per month, which as the host notes "is not all that much in terms of compute purchase."
	- **What that \$20 actually buys is more than it sounds.** The cloud machine is "very permissive," with internet access, and users have already pushed it well past coding: getting ChatGPT to **train another model** inside it, and installing Blender to do 3D rendering.
	- **Two readings, both worth holding.** It is an aggressive customer-acquisition subsidy whose cost scales with usage rather than seats — the classic consumption-subscription mismatch. And it is an abuse surface: a general-purpose, internet-connected compute environment at consumer price points is attractive for workloads that have nothing to do with the product.
	- The "work toggle" is explicitly **a temporary state** — the direction is full unification of Codex and ChatGPT.
- ## Maintenance and re-architecture are getting cheap, and that changes what gets built
	- Dependency upgrades: given decent changelogs and documented code, a model can "blast through your codebase in a couple of hours" on work teams "previously punted on" despite its security importance. He expects this **fully automated**.
	- Re-architecture, historically "a really, really costly endeavor, sometimes multiple years," should be "super, super accelerated."
	- **The ship criteria changed as a result.** What OpenAI asks before merging is evidence a change will be well received, is a worthy addition, and is **"worth maintaining over time" — "but also the cost of maintenance has gone down significantly, so we think about these things slightly differently than two or three years ago."** A falling maintenance cost lowers the bar for what is worth building, which mechanically increases surface area and code volume.
	- **The counter-pressure he names is architecture, not code quality**: "the good old rules of software engineering, like having good abstractions, really help." Models are "starting to become capable of thinking about" whether the architecture reduces maintenance burden over time, not just whether a file is clean.
	- **Lifecycle compression, stated vividly**: the old path added engineers slowly enough to see scale coming and to onboard humans and write documentation. Now "suddenly you have 100 agents contributing to this thing — that can happen in a weekend."
- ## Open source and model-agnosticism as deliberate anti-lock-in
	- The Codex CLI, SDK and app server are **open source under a permissive licence** — the host notes this is unique among the major labs, several of which ship closed harnesses.
	- **Benefits claimed**: onboarding is effectively pre-completed, since new hires have already read the repo and its PRs; genuine outside contributions; and being inside the community whose behavior they are trying to change.
	- **Costs conceded, and they are real**: artificial repo boundaries against their closed code; "a tsunami of random stuff" in contributions; and competitors copying work **"before we have the time to release it"** — "it does sting a little bit... but it's part of the game."
	- **Codex is not tied to OpenAI models.** The reasoning is pragmatic rather than idealistic: the repo is open, so adding another provider is a trivial fork, and forcing that "encourages people to go and use that fork." Beyond that, **"this optionality is very important to companies that we work with"** — enterprises will not buy a harness that forecloses model choice.
	- **The strategic statement**: "I want us to win users by having the best models, the most efficient models, the best product." The host's read is the sharper one — it **forces the company to compete on both the model layer and the harness layer simultaneously**, with no lock-in to fall back on.
	- **This is OpenAI conceding the routing layer**, and it converges with the independent conclusion in [[2026-09-05-circuit-broadcom-custom-asic-unmodelable-2027-peak-constraint]] that tools will route between open and closed models. When the leading closed lab ships a model-agnostic harness, routing is settled as the architecture and the contest moves to per-token merit.
- ## How OpenAI actually ships
	- **"Surprisingly similar process whether you ship on Codex or ChatGPT, even though ChatGPT goes out to a billion active users."** A PR ships "the next day or even the same day, and it goes out to a billion users and it's fine."
	- Code review, deploys and regression-catching are "pretty much automated," leaving engineers on the idea and product coherence.
	- **The AI-native operating model has a prerequisite most companies will not meet.** Codex is "plugged into everything" — Slack, documents, code — and the standard answer to a new hire's question is "have you asked Codex?" That works only because **"we do a lot of work in public channels. We open up documents with fairly broad permissions"** specifically so agents can reason over them. Deliberately loosening internal information boundaries is the enabling condition, and it is the opposite of most enterprise data-governance posture.
	- During the merge, **Codex acted as "a journalist"** — present in every Slack conversation and document, producing "a full recounting" of the debates and decisions. The host records his own discomfort and it is worth preserving: "this part felt a little bit of a Big Brother feel to it, where the AI is always watching, but it could well become the new normal."
	- Sottiaux's personal pattern is a usable adoption template: dictation into ChatGPT Work on mobile between meetings, with custom skills and instructions tuned to emit reports and slide decks in his preferred format, and **"any question I have, I can get an answer to within 30 minutes."**
- ## Product direction
	- The stated north star is **"a delightful, simple to use, personal AGI"** that knows the user's schedule and goals, holds the right resource access, is proactive, and **"can take sometimes risky actions on your behalf, but then you get the push notification and then you can verify."**
	- That is notify-and-verify rather than approve-in-advance — a materially weaker control model than the permissioned, pre-authorized gating enterprises say they need, and the same unresolved gap flagged from the buyer side in [[2026-09-05-circuit-broadcom-custom-asic-unmodelable-2027-peak-constraint]].
	- One design note with read-through: Codex was written in **Rust despite models being off-distribution for it** at the time, chosen for a clean agent/product boundary, robustness and efficiency — and because **compile-time static verification "is great for agents too."** If languages with strong static guarantees are structurally better substrates for agent-written code, that is a slow tailwind for verification-heavy toolchains.
