- ## A veteran investor's case that memory isn't cheap at 3× earnings
	- **Source**: User's in-person conversation with a veteran investor (unnamed), shared as an edited summary. These are opinions, not data. The historical claim about LTAs breaking is the investor's and is unverified. Related: [[2026-09-04-dram-feed-cxmt-share-break-and-lta-floor-arithmetic]], [[2026-06-03-memory-two-tier-market-and-ltas]], [[2026-07-21-memory-positioning-bull-bear-debate]], [[2026-07-04-dram-commodity-vs-decommoditization-synthesis]], [[2026-06-02-memory-cycle-bear-case-arguments]], [[TSM-2026-Q2]].
	- **Thesis**: The investor argues that a 3× P/E on 2027 earnings isn't cheap, because those earnings are a cyclical peak and **long-term agreements (LTAs) won't protect the years after it**. In his view, LTAs make customers fund part of the memory makers' capex and do little else: when prices fall far enough, buyers give up their prepayments and walk away. Because memory raised prices like a commodity, the market will keep pricing it like one, discounting the next oversupply year however far the shortage runs. TSMC isn't discounted the same way because its price increases were gradual.
- ## The argument, step by step
	- | Step | Investor's claim | Comment |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | 1. LTAs | "Basically meaningless." Their main effect is that customers shoulder part of capex through prepayments | This disputes the central bull claim that LTAs smooth the cycle ([[2026-06-03-memory-two-tier-market-and-ltas]]) |
	  | 2. History | Whenever prices fell **more than 60% from peak**, customers broke LTAs even after signing them | Unverified, but it sets a specific threshold at which the contract floors stop working |
	  | 3. The next year | Even if a 2027 shortage is certain, 2028 profit isn't, so a 3× multiple on 2027 doesn't justify a higher price | The investor's core point: a multiple on one peak year says little about value |
	  | 4. Regress | Even if 2028 were certain, 2029 would still be in doubt. Even with shortage through 2030, the market prices oversupply in 2031 | For a commodity, the market always looks to the next downturn, so the multiple never expands |
	  | 5. TSMC | TSMC raised prices "in a healthy way," while memory "hiked prices like a commodity," so the market treats it as one | The cause is how prices rose, not how big the AI exposure is |
	  | 6. Conclusion | If 2028 prices fall enough to break LTAs, 3× 2027 earnings becomes **7.8–10× 2028**, which isn't cheap | See the arithmetic below |
- ## Checking the 7.8–10× arithmetic
	- 3× ÷ 7.8 = 0.38 and 3× ÷ 10 = 0.30. The investor's range therefore implies **2028 operating profit of 30–38% of 2027, a 62–70% decline**.
	- **Is that consistent with a ~60% price fall? Roughly, and it may be generous.** Rough model, my assumptions: 2027 operating margin ~75%, 2028 bit growth ~15%, total cost flat because cost-per-bit declines offset the extra bits.
		- Price −53% → operating profit falls to ~38% of 2027 → **7.8×**.
		- Price −59% → ~30% → **10×**.
		- Price −60% with no bit growth → ~20% → **15×**.
	- So the investor's range corresponds to prices falling about 53–59%, just under his own 60% threshold for breaking LTAs. If prices actually cross 60% and LTAs fail, the 2028 multiple is more likely **11–15×** than 7.8–10×. Operating leverage cuts both ways: the same 75% margin that makes 2027 look so cheap is what makes 2028 profit fall faster than price.
	- **What the claim does not say**: the investor doesn't argue that a fall of more than 60% is likely in 2028, only that it can't be ruled out. The argument is about how uncertain the outcome is, not a forecast of where prices go.
- ## Why breaking an LTA is rational for the buyer
	- The investor's mechanism is that customers "gladly forfeit their deposits." From the buyer's side, breaking a contract pays whenever the gap between contract and spot prices on the remaining volume exceeds the forfeited prepayment. After a 60% price fall the saving is about 60% of the remaining contract value. Unless prepayments are a very large share of contract value, which hasn't been disclosed, walking away is the cheaper option.
	- **What enforcement looks like for the seller**: a memory maker can sue, but its biggest buyers (hyperscalers, Apple, Nvidia) are repeat customers it needs in the next upturn. In practice, sellers in a glut tend to renegotiate rather than litigate. This is why contract floors are weaker than their headline terms in a downturn.
	- **How this-cycle contracts differ from past ones**: SanDisk's 8 contracts guarantee **\$94B** of minimum revenue at floor prices, and ~70% of Samsung's capacity is reportedly under LTA through 2031 ([[2026-09-04-dram-feed-cxmt-share-break-and-lta-floor-arithmetic]]). Those floors already sit **26–28% below realized ASP**. A price fall of up to roughly 30% therefore lands **inside** the floors, and only deeper falls test whether they hold. The investor's 60% threshold and the floor design are compatible: the contracts protect against an ordinary correction, not a crash.
- ## TSMC versus memory: why the market prices them differently
	- **The investor's explanation: it's about how prices rose.** TSMC raises wafer prices gradually, while memory prices rose violently: NAND ASP rose **~4× in a year** and DRAM **80–90% quarter-to-date** in early 2026. Price moves that size tell investors that supply and demand set the price, not the seller. Gradual increases suggest pricing power.
	- **Underlying causes the investor didn't spell out**:
		- **Substitutability**: TSMC is effectively the only supplier of leading-edge logic, and a design built for TSMC can't move fabs quickly. Memory is a standard product, with three major suppliers plus **CXMT at 10%** DRAM share and rising.
		- **What sets the price**: TSMC prices are negotiated against a customer's design-in commitment. Memory prices clear against the marginal bit, and a mid-single-digit oversupply can reverse a 4× move.
		- **Margin path**: TSMC's gross margin ranges over the mid-50s% to high-60s% (67.7% in Q2 2026, per [[TSM-2026-Q2]]) and reflects long-term value sharing. Memory margins swing from losses to 70%+ within a few years.
	- **Where the TSMC answer is weaker**: an AI capex slowdown would hit TSMC's N3/N2 utilization too, and TSMC has its own capex cycle. The difference in valuation reflects how **predictable** each company's pricing is, not whether it is exposed to AI. That matches the investor's point.
- ## Where the argument is strongest and weakest
	- **Strongest**: it explains why memory stocks have historically looked cheapest on P/E at the peak. A low multiple on peak earnings is the normal market treatment for a commodity business, not a mispricing. The real argument for owning memory has to be about **normalized, mid-cycle earnings**, not about 3×.
	- **Weakest**: the infinite-regress step ("even if shortage through 2030, the market prices 2031") would justify a low multiple whatever the evidence, so it can't be tested. It also ignores two things that would change the market's treatment: HBM's design-in, customer-specific qualification (closer to TSMC's model than to commodity DRAM), and how much of profit comes from contracted rather than spot volume. Bristlemoon's SanDisk downside case (EPS \$154–218 at 8×, roughly today's price) suggests part of the bear case is already priced in.
	- **What would settle it**: whether LTAs hold through the first real price fall. If contract prices drop 30–50% and floors are honored, the investor's model is wrong for this cycle. If buyers renegotiate at a 30% fall, his model is right and contract floors give little protection.
