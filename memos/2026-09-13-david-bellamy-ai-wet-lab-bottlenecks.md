- ## AI Does Not Eliminate Wet-Lab Bottlenecks
	- **Source**: David Bellamy X thread, September 13, 2026. Bellamy says he has both trained a frontier LLM and personally designed and synthesized custom viruses.
	- **Thesis**: AI can accelerate biological research and design, but it cannot collapse the capital, procurement, automation, experimental-cycle, and human-translation constraints of physical biology. The likely near-term effect is more demand for wet-lab infrastructure and safeguards—not autonomous biological capability unconstrained by the physical world.

- ## The Binding Constraints
	- An LLM may propose a genome, but producing and validating a viable biological system requires specialized equipment, materials, expertise, and repeated physical experiments.
	- Bellamy estimates that a hypothetical fully automated synthesis laboratory would cost **well over \$100M**, excluding high ongoing operating expenses.
	- Procuring the required equipment could take **most of a year**. Much existing laboratory hardware lacks APIs and would require custom integration.
	- A basic synthesis-and-test cycle takes **several days on average**, and more than a week for some viruses. AI cannot accelerate incubation or organism-development time beyond physical limits.
	- No single laboratory can work with every conceivable virus, so biological capability remains fragmented across facilities, equipment, protocols, and containment levels.

- ## Why Digital Scaling Does Not Transfer Directly
	- Recursive improvement works fastest on software, mathematics, and other digital tasks with cheap, rapid, verifiable feedback.
	- Biology has slow and noisy feedback, incomplete scientific understanding, and costly real-world iteration.
	- Results in cells or animals do not reliably predict human outcomes. Bellamy compares this with drug discovery, where many promising animal results fail in human trials.
	- AI therefore increases the rate of hypotheses faster than it increases the rate of trustworthy experimental validation.

- ## Infrastructure and Safeguards
	- DNA-synthesis providers screen requested sequences, creating a control point between digital design and physical production.
	- Equipment procurement, facility regulation, supply chains, biosafety procedures, and limited laboratory access add further barriers.
	- A completely autonomous, API-driven laboratory capable of end-to-end work does not exist today, according to Bellamy, who previously worked on automated laboratory development.
	- AI may make technical knowledge easier to access, but it does not remove these physical and institutional constraints.

- ## Commercial Consequences
	- Faster AI-assisted design should increase demand for laboratory automation, robotics, instruments, consumables, contract research, and experimental capacity.
	- The throughput mismatch may shift the bottleneck—and bargaining power—from model developers toward companies that own reliable physical validation infrastructure.
	- Instrument interoperability and APIs become more valuable as laboratories attempt to connect AI systems with fragmented equipment fleets.
	- Screening, auditability, identity controls, and compliance software should become more important as biological design tools improve.
	- AI-first drug discovery economics will remain governed by wet-lab validation and clinical translation; cheaper hypothesis generation alone does not eliminate development time or failure rates.

- ## Predictions
	- Biological hypothesis volume will grow faster than laboratory throughput, creating demand for automated validation capacity.
	- Near-term AI adoption will augment scientists and laboratory operators rather than replace the physical research stack.
	- Biosecurity policy will increasingly focus on physical access, synthesis screening, procurement, and laboratory controls rather than model capability alone.
