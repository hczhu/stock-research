- ## WSJ: flash makers get their revenge on Apple
	- **Source**: *The Wall Street Journal*, Rolfe Winkler, "The Beleaguered Chip Sellers Getting Their Revenge on Apple," Oct 28, 2026. Flash prices and volumes are TechInsights estimates. Storage margins are the Journal's own analysis, explained in its methodology section at the end of the article.
	- **Thesis**: AI hyperscalers have taken over as the main buyers of flash, ending the bargaining power that let Apple buy flash for pennies and resell it to consumers at 80–90% gross margins. Apple's flash bill roughly **quadruples for the same volume**. Hyperscalers are signing long-term contracts that protect supplier profits if prices fall. Both point to a NAND cycle that is structurally stronger for Kioxia, Sandisk and Micron than past cycles, and to Apple's storage upsell earning lower margins even as dollar profit holds up.
	- Related: [[2026-11-03-stratechery-apple-memory-purchase-commitments-consumer-memory-market]], [[2026-07-04-horace-dediu-memory-shock-apple-supply-chain]], [[2026-06-24-apple-price-skimming-iphone-dram-passthrough]], [[AAPL-2026-Q2]].
- ## Hyperscalers have displaced Apple as the main flash buyer
	- **Prices**: AI demand has **quintupled flash prices** in the past year. The price per gigabyte went from \$68 in 2005 to a nickel in 2023, and is now **more than 30 cents (+550%)**.
	- **Volumes**: two years ago, Apple bought nearly **2×** the flash of Microsoft, Amazon, Meta and Google combined. Now the hyperscalers buy **double Apple's share**.
	- ^^**"Meta, Microsoft and Amazon signed contracts with Kioxia this year to secure long-term supply by committing to higher prices. The contracts protect Kioxia's profits if the market turns."**^^
	- **Why hyperscalers can pay more**: flash is a smaller share of a data center's cost than of an iPhone's, and accounting rules let them spread the cost over years.
	- ^^**"Apple is expected to spend \$25 billion on flash storage this year for about the same amount it bought for \$6 billion in 2025"**^^ (TechInsights). Tim Cook, now executive chairman, called it a "100-year flood." People familiar with Apple's procurement say it had ignored analysts' warnings and was caught off guard.
- ## Storage upgrades: where Apple's flash margin was made
	- ^^**"Apple recorded an average gross profit of more than 80 cents for every dollar it charged iPhone and iPad buyers for extra storage since it first released those devices through 2025, the Journal found. For Mac computers sold since 2010, it averaged nearly 80 cents."**^^
	- ^^**"Roughly 100 million iPhone buyers paid for storage upgrades last year, according to IDC estimates"**^^
	- ^^**"Many paid \$200 for an extra 256 gigabytes and as much as \$800 for an extra 1.75 terabytes. At an 85% margin, that translates to more than \$10 billion in gross profit"**^^. Mac and iPad storage upgrades add billions more, and iCloud monetizes users who run out of space.
	- **2023, the bottom of the cycle**: flash fell to 5 cents a gigabyte. Bernstein estimates the flash industry lost **\$19B** in operating income that year, while Apple made **\$14B** in gross profit selling extra storage.

- ## Apple's old playbook
	- **Scale**: as the iPhone took off, Apple took **more than a fifth** of the world's flash supply, spending \$3–4B a year. Suppliers accepted the lowest prices in the industry because their high fixed costs meant they had to keep their factories full.
	- ^^**"The company had another hardball tactic: Avoid commitments. After ordering flash at rock-bottom prices, Apple would refuse delivery at times if flash prices were falling. That left suppliers holding piles of unsold chips, including ones custom-made for Apple. Kioxia executives joked they felt like Charlie Brown trying to kick the football—with Lucy always pulling it away."**^^
	- **Most-favored-customer terms with audits**:
		- Apple could audit suppliers' books to confirm that no one got a lower price. The audits also showed Apple each supplier's factory output, and so how badly each one needed an order.
		- More than a decade ago, Apple caught Micron selling cheaper to another customer and won an arbitration settlement worth tens of millions of dollars.
	- **Earlier supplier pushback, and Apple's retaliation**:
		- Around the end of 2015, Sandisk refused Apple extra supply because other customers were paying more.
		- Around 2017, Western Digital charged Apple a fee to reserve flash. When prices fell the next year, procurement head Tony Blevins made WD refund the fee and pay Apple to test its chips.
		- Blevins left Apple in 2022.
- ## My notes
	- **Margin falls but dollar profit holds up**: Apple's response to the cost shock is to raise upgrade prices, not to absorb the cost.
		- The 1TB step rose from +\$400 to +\$600, and the new 2TB step is +\$1,200. The 18 Pro's 1TB upgrade earns *more* gross profit (\$408) than the 17 Pro's did (\$358), even though cost rose 4.6×.
		- In the second chart, average profit per upgrade step rises from about \$177 to about \$223, while margin falls from about 89% to about 63%.
		- That average weights every step equally, so the new 2TB step pulls it up. The real mix effect depends on how many buyers move up to the higher tiers at the new prices.
	- **The flash bill grows faster than the storage profit**: the roughly **\$19B** increase in Apple's flash spend (\$6B to \$25B) is nearly **twice** the more than \$10B of iPhone storage-upgrade gross profit.
		- The flash spend also covers base storage across all devices, so storage-upgrade pricing alone can't offset it. This is why the across-the-board price increases (+13% on iPhones) were needed, consistent with the gross-margin pressure in [[AAPL-2026-Q2]].
	- **For suppliers, the long-term contracts make this cycle different**:
		- In past downturns, Apple's refusal to take delivery and its audit rights let it push losses back onto suppliers, as in 2023's \$19B industry operating loss.
		- Hyperscaler contracts with price commitments that protect profit in a downturn put a floor under Kioxia's earnings. That supports a higher trough multiple for Kioxia, Sandisk and Micron's NAND business.
		- *My note*: the article names Meta, Microsoft and Amazon contracts with Kioxia only. It gives no contract terms or comparable deals at Sandisk or Micron.
	- **The open question is whether Apple gets its bargaining power back**. The article ends on "when—or if," and Apple has a record of punishing suppliers once prices fall (the WD reservation-fee episode).
		- Against that: Apple is now the *smaller* buyer, its most demanding negotiator has left, and [[2026-11-03-stratechery-apple-memory-purchase-commitments-consumer-memory-market]] shows Apple itself now making purchase commitments.
		- The supplier risk is a demand downturn in AI storage, not Apple retaliating.
