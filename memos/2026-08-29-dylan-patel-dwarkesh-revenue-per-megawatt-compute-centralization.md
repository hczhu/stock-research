- ## Dylan Patel on Dwarkesh: revenue per megawatt, compute centralization, and the rate shock
	- **Source**: Dwarkesh Patel podcast with **Dylan Patel**, founder of SemiAnalysis. **Three caveats that matter throughout.** Patel sells the models and research these figures come from, so the forecasts are his product. The transcript is machine-generated and garbles numbers badly — several are internally inconsistent and flagged below. And the forward figures are explicitly vibes in places ("this is just vibing a number"). Prior Patel coverage: [[2026-07-16-dylan-patel-podcast-ai-infra-memory-cpu-optics-power]], [[2026-02-05-mad-podcast-dylan-patel-nvidia-moat-china-ai-capex]].
	- **Thesis**: The whole argument runs on one metric — **revenue per megawatt** — and one consequence. Compute costs \$10–15M/MW/year to rent, frontier labs now monetize it at up to \$50M/MW and are heading toward \$100M/MW, and **that spread is what lets them outbid everyone else for the world's compute**. The consequences follow mechanically: centralization into two labs, compute prices inflecting upward, capex that cash flows cannot fund, and **rates rising enough to be a macro event rather than a sector event**.
- ## Revenue per megawatt — the metric everything else hangs on
	- | Item | Figure |
	  |------------------------------------------|------------------------------|
	  | Cost to rent compute | **\$10–15M per MW per year** |
	  | GPT-4 era on Hopper | **negative gross margin** for OpenAI |
	  | Anthropic today, peak | **up to \$50M per MW** |
	  | Stated trajectory | **"on the path to \$100M per MW"** |
	  | End-2027 forecast | **\$50M+/MW**, possibly **\$70–80M/MW blended** |
	- **The \$50M/MW claim is checkable, and it holds.** Against the \$65bn annualized figure in [[2026-08-28-ft-anthropic-openai-run-rates-ramp-ai-index]]: Anthropic at roughly 3 GW mid-year with ~40% allocated to inference gives ~1,200 MW of revenue-generating capacity, or **~\$54M per MW**. Run the same math on the ~5 GW end-year exit rate and it falls to ~\$32M/MW — which is why the *timing* of the compute base matters when quoting this ratio. **Do not compare revenue to total fleet; only the inference share earns.**
	- **The mechanism this enables is the actual point**: spend \$10–15M/MW on inference capacity, generate \$50M, and **recycle the entire profit into training**. That is the flywheel, and it is new — a year ago this was all venture-funded losses.
	- **Profitability inflection**: Anthropic turned profitable in **Q2**; OpenAI "could potentially" turn profitable in **Q3**. New capital still arrives, but to accelerate growth rather than to cover losses. Compare [[2026-07-07-semianalysis-anthropic-ipo-financials-profitability]].
- ## Compute share: the centralization trajectory
	- | Period | Two labs' combined position |
	  |--------------------|---------------------------------------------|
	  | Start of 2026 | OpenAI ~2 GW, Anthropic <2 GW |
	  | End of 2026 | **both above 5 GW** — a 3–4× year |
	  | Share of compute *added* in 2026 | **~30%** |
	  | 2027 | **40–50%** of additions; by end of year, **half of all incremental compute** |
	  | 2028 | requires **70–80%** of incremental compute to reach 100 GW combined — called "a very aggressive goal" |
	- **World supply, in gigawatts added per year**: **30 (2026) → 50 (2027) → 70 (2028) → 90–100 (2029)**, then perhaps +100 GW annually. That puts world compute **above 200 GW by end-2028**. Patel calls 80 GW for 2028 "the upper bound… I'm so bullish."
	- **The efficiency multiplier is why share of watts understates share of capability.** GB300, TPUv7, and Trainium 3 deliver **3–5× the performance per watt** of prior generations, so a lab taking half of *new* watts takes materially more than half of new FLOPs. Patel's conclusion: by end-2028 the two labs plausibly control **most of the usable FLOPs in the world**.
	- **Cross-check against what is actually contracted**: the commitment ledger in [[2026-08-28-aws-15gw-base-capacity-doubling-by-2027]] shows OpenAI at 30 GW and Anthropic at ~14.5 GW of announced vendor commitments — **~44.5 GW combined**, against Patel's ~10 GW of *deployed* capacity at end-2026. The commitments are consistent with his trajectory; they are also a reminder that **contracted ≠ energized**.
- ## The 100× fab arbitrage — the most striking claim
	- Inputs for **1 GW of Vera Rubin**: **~55,000 N3 wafers, ~6K N5 wafers, ~170K DRAM wafers**.
	- **Fab tooling to produce 1 GW of compute per year: \$3–4B**; add cleanrooms and shell and call it **~\$6B of fab capex**. That \$6B produces **one gigawatt every year**, and each gigawatt generates on the order of **\$100B of revenue per year**.
	- **Cumulated over five years the arithmetic is extreme**: the first gigawatt earns five years, the second four, and so on — **~\$1.5 trillion of end-AI revenue from \$6B of fab capex, or ~250×**. Halve it for every middleman and you still have **>100×**.
	- **The implication Patel draws is that capitalism will attack this, but slowly** — "it's a whip… it takes a long time for the whip signal to get to the tail end." Concrete evidence of the lag: **Zeiss now says it needs to make 100 EUV tools by the end of the decade**, having not believed it needed that many earlier this year. Arbitrages are already visible — traders buying **turbines** to resell, and Patel's aside that anyone able to buy a **\$400M EUV tool** could resell it "north of a billion."
	- **How to use it**: the 100× gap is the strongest available argument that **upstream semiconductor capacity is chronically underbuilt relative to end demand**, and that the binding constraint sits with a handful of sub-suppliers (Zeiss optics, turbines) rather than with the fabs themselves.
- ## Compute pricing: why it inflects, and where he thinks it lands
	- **Anyone can make money at \$10–15M/MW today** — Patel's example is buying a GB300 rack, downloading open weights and vLLM/SGLang, and listing on OpenRouter. That floor is what has started pushing prices up.
	- **The observed high mark**: SpaceX reportedly sold compute to Google at **~\$40M per MW**, and "Elon wouldn't have sold if it was 15." For the labs to secure 70% of 2028 compute, Patel thinks they must pay **\$25–50M/MW**.
	- **But his base case is more conservative than that implies**: *"most compute will still continue to transact at sub-\$20M per megawatt"* even at end-2027 — **because most compute is contracted before it is built**. A developer needs a customer commitment to raise the capital, which locks pricing early.
	- **The exception is the structural insight**: **Meta and SpaceX can build without a pre-committed customer**, funding from balance sheet and credit markets. That buys them **optionality** — deploy internally, or sell into a spot market at \$25–50M/MW. Patel frames them as the plausible number three and four in compute, monetizing at "crazy margins." **Balance-sheet capacity is what converts a data-center builder into a price-maker.**
- ## The non-consensus call: inference share falls, not rises
	- **The stated allocation today**: roughly **60% training / 40% inference** — and the training half decomposes further into **~50% research** (architectures, data mixes, hyperparameters) and only **~10% development**, i.e. actual training runs.
	- **A striking scale datapoint**: the Mythos **pre-training run was sub-200 MW for about two months**, with RL smaller at any instant. Against a multi-gigawatt fleet, **the headline training run is a rounding error — the compute goes to research.**
	- **Patel's call**: labs will **allocate a declining share to inference over time**, which he flags as against consensus. The logic is a straight capital-allocation comparison — at \$60–70M/MW of inference revenue, do you take the profit and pay dividends, or "go build AGI"? He expects the board-level answer to be the latter, because *"the only reason to have inference compute be so large is so you can grow your training fleet."*
	- **His evidence is the one number I would check hardest.** He argues Anthropic adds more compute every month while revenue additions have plateaued, implying the marginal megawatt goes to R&D. But the transcribed figure — *"only adding \$25 billion in ARR every month"* — **is inconsistent with the FT series**, which shows \$47bn to \$65bn over roughly two months, or **~\$9bn/month**. The qualitative claim may hold; **the specific number as transcribed does not, and should not be reused.**
	- **Why it matters commercially**: if inference share falls while capability rises, then **token supply grows more slowly than compute**, which supports token prices and argues against the fastest commoditization scenarios.
- ## Funding: the capex cannot come from cash flow
	- **SemiAnalysis modelling: ~\$11 trillion of capex from 2024–2029 — ~\$6T funded by cash, ~\$5T by debt.**
	- 2028 alone is **\$3–4T** (~\$2.5T IT capex plus \$1–2T of data centers and energy). At 100 GW/year the arithmetic is **~\$5T at ~\$50M/MW all-in** — but Patel argues the true figure is **\$7–10T**, because power plants and shells for *next* year's gigawatts must be built and paid for *this* year. By end of decade that approaches **a tenth of the world economy**, and a quarter to a third of the US economy.
	- **The critical structural point about hyperscalers**: they funded most of the build to date but **now generate no free cash** — they spend everything on capex *and* raise debt on top. Google and Meta stopped buybacks to fund it. So the incremental dollar has to come from somewhere new: semiconductor vendors financing customers, infrastructure funds rotating from bridges into data centers, and ultimately **households and institutions choosing hyperscaler and lab debt over mortgages and government bonds**.
	- **This is the same conclusion Ben Thompson reached from the other direction** in [[2026-08-21-ben-thompson-ilb-risk-relocation-commodity-logic]] — *"maybe the nearest term question is are we going to have enough money."* Patel is answering it with a number: **\$5T of new credit**.
- ## The rate thesis — where this becomes a macro call
	- **The mechanism**: if data centers return several times cost, borrowers will pay far more for capital, and that repricing propagates. Patel's illustration — **Meta recently raised at 5–6%; "I don't see why they wouldn't pay 8%,"** and a **250bp move for Meta is a 250bp move for everyone else**.
	- **The US fiscal arithmetic he walks through**: corporate income tax is **<10% of federal revenue** while payroll and income taxes are **80%+** — the base most exposed to automation. Today **~20% of tax revenue services the debt**. A 1pp rate rise takes that toward 25% over a five-year rollover; a larger move plus **\$2T/year of new borrowing** pushes it **north of 60%**.
	- **The emerging-market call is the sharper one**: countries with heavy debt, thin tax revenue, and frequent rollovers — **Pakistan and Nigeria are named** — face the analogue of the **Volcker shock, which triggered defaults in roughly 40 countries in the 1980s**. Patel and the host expect a repeat.
	- **The equity implication is genuinely non-obvious and worth holding**: a higher discount rate craters long-duration, stable-cash-flow equities even as the index holds. Patel's formulation is the memorable one — *"if you're really AI-pilled, everything in the economy should trade at two or three times earnings."* He uses it to explain why **memory can do extremely well as a business while memory stocks should not re-rate 10×**: the same conditions that create the demand also compress every multiple in the market.
	- **And this is why he thinks the exponential bends.** Rates, regulation, and politics are the forces that stop compute from reaching what pure economics would justify — *"how much does the rest of the world let that happen?"*
- ## Regulation as the binding constraint on lab economics
	- **The concrete instances cited**: OpenAI not releasing Astra and pausing training for two weeks; Anthropic withholding a model pending safety assessment (believed to be the next Mythos); and — the part with financial consequence — **the labs reportedly being restricted in what they can deploy internally**.
	- **The economic consequence is direct**: if labs cannot ship their best models, **revenue per megawatt stalls or declines** as competitors catch up, and with it their ability to outbid everyone else for compute. That is the mechanism that would prevent the 100 GW outcome.
	- **Regulation now also targets supply**: **New York banning data centers, Texas moratoriums, Ohio requiring property-tax payments within a radius.** These reduce supply and raise cost — **inflationary, and passed through**.
	- **The asymmetry he stresses**: this restrains US labs far more than open-weight Chinese models, which is a direct competitive transfer.
- ## China
	- | Period | US share of watts deployed | China |
	  |------------|----------------------------|------------------|
	  | 2022 | 45–50% | **30–35%** |
	  | Today | **~70%** | **sub-10%** |
	  | 2028 | — | **~30 GW or less**, on materially worse chips |
	  | 2029 | — | **50 GW "completely reasonable"**, but worth "as much as 20 GW" of American chips |
	- **The inflection is 2027–28**, when SMIC and CXMT fabs come online and domestic production reaches "many millions of units a year," adding **5–10 GW of domestic chips in 2028**.
	- **The compute gap is currently enormous at the lab level**: leading Chinese labs run **100–200 MW total** (ByteDance Seed excepted), against Anthropic near 5 GW — yet their models are "not that far behind… relative to the amount of compute they have." **That efficiency gap is the thing to watch**, and it is consistent with the architecture convergence noted in [[2026-08-26-ainews-nvidia-q2-hf-confirmed-glm53-flash-economics]].
	- **The honest caveat Patel gives**: some of the gap is export controls, but some is financial systems — and **Chinese semiconductor subsidies exceed the rest of the world's combined**, so *"if takeoff is not as fast… China will catch up drastically on the semiconductor side."* **Slow takeoff favours China; fast takeoff favours the US labs.**
- ## Where the value has actually gone
	- **The value-capture layer has rotated repeatedly**, which is the most useful historical frame here: in 2023 the hardware supply chain captured everything while model providers ran negative gross margins and **memory vendors earned nothing on HBM despite delivering the scarce input**. Today the model layer captures large positive margins and **TSMC captures less than the memory vendors**.
	- **But end users still capture most of it.** Patel's examples: a trading firm with an exclusive fast-mode contract generating far more from the tokens than it pays; **Meta at one point rumoured to be as much as 10% of Anthropic's business** while extracting far more from ad-ranking improvements than Anthropic earns from serving it. Customers may be capturing **\$300–500M per MW** while paying \$100M.
	- **The forward risk this implies**: the same logic that says labs should move compute from inference to training also says they should **stop selling tokens to customers who capture more value than they pay** — *"why would Anthropic allocate compute to that?"* **Enterprise buyers currently enjoying large consumer surplus on frontier tokens should expect it to be priced away.**
- ## The labour-supply framing, and what to distrust
	- **The compounding argument**: frontier compute grows ~5×/year in FLOPs while compute needed for a given capability falls ~3×/year, so **effective AI "population" at the frontier grows ~10×/year** — a lab going from ~10M to ~100M to ~1B labour-equivalents, and plausibly exceeding world population within a lab by end of decade. Note the deflating check the host applies: **a gigawatt sustaining ~1M white-collar workers at \$100k each is only ~\$100B** — "surprisingly low."
	- **What I would not carry forward**: the **\$25bn/month ARR figure** (contradicted by the FT series); the **2030s "rate of interest in the tens of percent"** speculation, which both participants flag as beyond forecastable; and the 2029+ gigawatt figures, which Patel explicitly declines to defend past four years.
	- **What survives**: revenue per megawatt as the organizing metric, the 60/40 training-inference split with research dominating training, the \$11T/\$5T capex-and-credit model, the fab-capex-to-revenue gap, and the observation that **hyperscalers have stopped generating free cash** — that last one is checkable against filings and is the load-bearing fact under the entire credit argument.
