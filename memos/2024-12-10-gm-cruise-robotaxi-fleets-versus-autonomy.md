- ## GM Kills Cruise: Fleet Robotaxis Versus General Autonomy
	- **Source**: User-provided Stratechery Update, “GM Kills Cruise,” citing Bloomberg's December 10, 2024 report on GM winding down Cruise's robotaxi operations.
	- **Thesis**: Cruise's failure reflects more than high development cost or poor execution. Thompson argues that robotaxi fleets and consumer autonomy require different architectures and industry structures: centralized fleets favor a modular service layer that commoditizes vehicles, while personally owned autonomous cars favor an integrated product. GM funded a robotaxi service whose economic purpose conflicted with GM's desire to differentiate and monetize its own vehicles.

- ## Two Autonomous-Driving Architectures
	- | Dimension | Centralized robotaxi fleet | General vehicle autonomy |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | Representative systems | Waymo and Cruise | Tesla and Comma.ai |
	  | Operating assumptions | **Pre-mapped service area**, central operator, remote assistance | Vehicle drives independently without assuming high-definition maps or central support |
	  | Hardware approach | Wider sensor array and higher per-vehicle cost | Primarily cameras and lower-cost hardware |
	  | Optimization target | Maximum reliability and safety inside a constrained domain | Geographic flexibility and affordability for an individual owner |
	  | Natural product structure | Modular stack with competing vehicle, sensor, and fleet-management suppliers | Integrated consumer product that tightly coordinates hardware and software |
	  | Economic offset | High utilization can absorb expensive hardware and centralized operations | Consumer affordability requires lower hardware and operating cost |
- ## Why GM's Ownership Model Was Structurally Conflicted
	- A robotaxi customer chooses a transportation service, not a specific vehicle. The service layer therefore benefits from interchangeable cars, sensors, maintenance providers, and fleet operators.
	- Cruise's ideal strategy would have been to define interfaces across the stack and force suppliers—including automakers—to compete on price.
	- GM wanted Cruise to protect GM vehicles from commoditization, yet owning a closed service limited Cruise's ability to source the best and cheapest inputs.
	- GM contributed capital but little differentiated capability to the service layer. Cruise returned neither durable strategic advantage nor favorable publicity after its pedestrian incident.
	- Integrating Cruise back into GM to develop safety systems and future consumer autonomy is more consistent with GM's place in the stack: autonomy can differentiate a car and support premium pricing.
	- Thompson doubts that Cruise's fleet-oriented technology will translate cleanly into consumer vehicles or recover value commensurate with the **\$9B-plus** investment.
- ## Robotaxis Are Supply; Transportation Is the Market
	- Thompson rejects framing Waymo, Zoox, and Tesla as competing for a standalone “robotaxi market.” The relevant demand pool is transportation, where Uber already aggregates customers.
	- From the rider's perspective, human-driven Uber rides and robotaxis share the core experience: summon a vehicle, take the trip, and leave without owning the asset.
	- Human drivers give Uber an asset-light elasticity advantage:
		- Drivers finance vehicle depreciation and operating costs.
		- Pricing signals add supply during peaks without Uber owning idle fleet capacity.
		- Drivers cover routes and geographies outside centralized autonomous service areas.
	- A robotaxi fleet faces a utilization tradeoff: size for peak demand and leave cars idle, or maximize normal utilization and lack supply during surges.
- ## Platform Economics and Bargaining Power
	- Waymo operating directly can cause riders to check multiple apps, but Thompson expects utilization economics to encourage AV suppliers to join the platform with the deepest demand; Waymo's planned Atlanta and Austin launches with Uber supported this view.
	- Uber gained pricing power as it won demand aggregation from Lyft while sourcing rides from atomized individual drivers.
	- A robotaxi operator represents hundreds or thousands of vehicles and may offer a differentiated customer experience. That supplier concentration gives it far more negotiating power than an individual driver.
	- Uber can remain the dominant transportation interface while capturing a smaller share of the profit pool as autonomy providers bargain for better economics.
- ## Thompson's Predictions
	- Uber remains relevant during the robotaxi transition because a hybrid network can match centralized AV fleets with elastic human supply.
	- Waymo and other AV operators should favor Uber partnerships where incremental demand and utilization outweigh the value of owning the customer relationship.
	- Small Tesla fleet owners are more plausible than individual owners routinely renting out personal cars, but both need access to a high-demand network.
	- If Tesla reaches full autonomy, self-driving Teslas are most likely to join Uber alongside human drivers; growing autonomous supply would lower prices and gradually compete human drivers off the platform.
	- **Falling driver costs would benefit Uber's demand and transaction volume, but supplier consolidation could reduce its take-rate economics**.
	- GM's retreat to consumer-facing autonomy is strategically more coherent, but success depends on whether Cruise's centralized-fleet technology can be repurposed into an integrated vehicle product.
