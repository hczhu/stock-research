- ## Uber's Robotaxi Risk: From Aggregator to Potentially Disrupted Incumbent
	- **Source**: Ben Thompson, Stratechery, “Robotaxis and Suburbia,” November 18, 2025 — https://stratechery.com/2025/robotaxis-and-suburbia/
	- **Thesis**: Uber has validated its network-effects model through scale, profitability, and expansion far beyond the historical taxi market. Thompson nevertheless revises his earlier robotaxi optimism: purpose-built autonomous fleets could create a larger market around children, suburban mobility, and package delivery that is qualitatively inaccessible to human-driven rides. If that happens, Uber's hybrid network may become a transitional liability, centralized AV operators may capture the bargaining power, and Uber may have to assume more capital intensity merely to preserve relevance.

- ## What Uber's Original Thesis Got Right
	- Uber created a new transportation experience instead of merely digitizing taxis. Network effects expanded both supply and demand, invalidating historical TAM assumptions.
	- Human drivers finance their own vehicles and absorb depreciation, energy, and insurance costs. Uber can add or remove supply through market pricing without owning a fixed fleet.
	- A suburban ride arriving in roughly **six minutes** in Madison illustrates how marketplace density expanded service into areas that previously had little practical taxi supply.
	- Human drivers remain useful for peaks and geographic edges: a centralized robotaxi fleet must choose between idle capacity during normal periods and insufficient vehicles during surges.
	- Under this view, robotaxis are another supply type inside the broader transportation market, while Uber remains the demand aggregator.

- ## Why Robotaxis Could Create a Different Market
	- Thompson argues he may be repeating the mistake made by early Uber skeptics: extrapolating current economics and current demand rather than considering how a better product expands the market.
	- **Purpose-built fleet economics**: Manufacturing robotaxis at scale could lower vehicle cost, while fewer accidents and fleet scale could make insurance manageable. This would weaken the assumption that vehicle depreciation permanently protects Uber's asset-light model.
	- **Children and safety**: A driverless cabin may offer greater perceived security and privacy than a human-driven ride. Suburban parents who would not send children alone in an Uber may trust a Waymo-like service.
	- **Packages fill idle hours**: Autonomous fleets could move children and commuters during passenger peaks, then perform low-cost deliveries during off-peak periods. This raises utilization and reduces the peak-capacity disadvantage.
	- **New demand is priced against energy, not labor**: Robotaxis do not decide that a fare is too low to work as long as it covers marginal operating costs. This can support same-hour delivery and trips uneconomic with a human driver.
	- If these use cases become the growth engine, the autonomous market is not merely today's Uber network with robots substituted for drivers; it is an orthogonal and potentially much larger market.

- ## Bargaining Power and Profit-Pool Shift
	- Uber negotiates today with a fragmented base of individual drivers. A robotaxi future could concentrate supply among a few sophisticated fleet and autonomy operators, weakening Uber's take-rate leverage.
	- Waymo's direct service in three of its five cities showed that a differentiated AV operator could own the customer rather than act only as marketplace supply.
	- Safety, privacy, and trust may become branded product attributes. If consumers specifically request Waymo or another robotaxi brand, Uber's demand aggregation becomes less defensible.
	- Uber can remain relevant during the transition while still losing long-term economics: hybrid dispatch may sustain trip volume even as a larger share of contribution profit migrates to vehicle and autonomy owners.

- ## The Nvidia Partnership as a Defensive Signal
	- Nvidia and Uber proposed a unified network of human drivers and autonomous vehicles using Drive AGX Hyperion 10 hardware, compatible autonomy software, and a shared data factory built around Nvidia Cosmos.
	- The modular approach could help Uber aggregate manufacturers and AV developers that lack their own demand network.
	- Thompson reads the announcement bearishly because it envisions gradual automation of Uber's existing marketplace rather than creation of a fundamentally better product.
	- It may also pull Uber toward higher capital intensity. Retrofitted, general-purpose vehicles could cost more than purpose-built robotaxis while leaving Uber exposed to fleet depreciation.

- ## Thompson's Updated Predictions
	- Uber's human-driver network should remain valuable for the transition, demand surges, and areas outside centralized AV coverage.
	- Uber's **long-term profitability is less secure than its continued near-term relevance** because centralized robotaxi suppliers can demand more favorable economics than individual drivers.
	- Robotaxis may expand suburban mobility by making independent travel safer for children and non-drivers, reinforcing a broader shift toward suburban living.
	- Passenger transport and delivery are likely to converge as autonomous fleets seek utilization across dayparts.
	- Uber could be “Uber-ed” if a driverless, purpose-built network creates new use cases that do not require—and may be incompatible with—its human-driver marketplace.
	- The decisive split is whether autonomy is absorbed into Uber's existing network or creates a separate market whose superior safety, cost, and utilization advantages pull demand away from Uber.
