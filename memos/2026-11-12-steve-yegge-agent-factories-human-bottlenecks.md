- ## Steve Yegge: agent factories move the bottleneck beyond writing software
	- **Source**: User-provided X-post excerpt by [Turing Post / Ksenia Se](https://x.com/TheTuringPost), summarizing remarks by [Steve Yegge](https://x.com/Steve_Yegge). The exact post URL, publication date, and underlying interview were not supplied. The excerpt relays his experience secondhand; predictions below are attributed to him, not established forecasts.
	- **Thesis**: If agents make software production abundant, spending can grow on inference and coordination while human attention, quality control, distribution, and enjoyable products become the scarcer resources. Cheap code does not automatically create profitable software or prove that total employment will rise.

- ## First-person cross-check: operating cost is not enterprise API pricing
	- In a separate [IT Revolution Fall 2026 talk](https://videos.itrevolution.com/watch/1233771796/), Yegge describes roughly 50 agents, personal spending of **\$10,000–\$12,000 per month**, and extensive use of discounted subscription access. The discount comparison is his own estimate, not an independently verified pricing study.
	- That talk reports approximately **2,000 commits per week**, including fixes and features, and players overwhelmed by the pace. Commits and features are different units: neither figure verifies the excerpt's daily feature count.
	- He also describes agents stalling under accumulated constraints and recommends directing excess capacity toward quality and security rather than ever more features. This reinforces the human-absorption bottleneck, but a separate talk does not authenticate every claim in the supplied post.
	- **Interpretation**: his personal bill should not be extrapolated into enterprise fleet economics. Subscription limits, API charges, model mix, utilization, retries, and supervision can materially change cost per accepted outcome.

- ## Where value could accrue across the software stack
	- **Inference providers**: more agents and longer-running workflows can expand usage even as cost per task falls. Spending rises only if workload growth outweighs lower prices and efficiency gains; this anecdote does not disclose any provider's revenue or margin contribution.
	- **Coordination, security, and observability**: fleets need permissions, memory, scheduling, evaluations, audit trails, and cost controls. Those needs create potential demand, but bundled infrastructure and open implementations could pressure standalone vendors. A genuine moat would require dependable operation, embedded workflows, or accumulated context rather than merely launching many agents.
	- **Application and content platforms**: cheaper production may increase the volume of games and software, making discovery, trusted distribution, curation, and monetization more valuable. It can simultaneously lower barriers for challengers and dilute attention across more products; this is not automatically bullish for every game studio or software incumbent.
	- **Human complementarity is narrower than net job creation**: more review, product judgment, and customer interaction may be valuable when output expands. Whether companies hire, redeploy existing staff, or automate those tasks in turn depends on demand and economics. The anecdote supports a shifting bottleneck, not a labor-market conclusion.
	- **The useful economic unit is an accepted outcome**: compare cost and elapsed time per reliable, customer-used improvement, including rework and review. Agent counts and output counts without quality, retention, or revenue evidence can overstate the benefit.
