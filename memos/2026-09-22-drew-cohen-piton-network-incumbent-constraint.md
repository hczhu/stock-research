- **Source**: Drew Cohen (@DrewCohenMoney), *Five Minute Money* — "The Mental Model That Explains Any Business's Success." Cohen hosts The Synopsis podcast; this repo already holds five memos from his interviews. This is a framework piece, not a data piece: no forward estimates, no company financials beyond illustrative figures.

- **Thesis**: The predictive content of the framework is not that incumbents cannot copy a challenger — Walmart and RH both prove they can — but that copying requires ripping out load-bearing decisions, and the cost and duration of that restaking is estimable in advance. That is the part an investor can underwrite.

- ## The construct

	- A **piton** is a strategic decision that simultaneously supports a business on its current path and constrains it from taking others. The climbing metaphor is the point: the stake holds you up while the rope limits where you can go next. A **piton network** is a stack of such decisions that optimizes a business for one outcome and, critically, makes it hard to optimize for a different one.

	- The mechanism Cohen names is the **decision cluster**: you believe you are making one decision, but you are making several. His example — leasing a small, high-traffic storefront with parking, before deciding what restaurant to run — forecloses sit-down and full-service, forces takeout, forces volume, and therefore forces a low price point. The real-estate decision decided the menu.

	- Applied to sourcing: "sell products cheaply" implies offshore sourcing → long lead times → domestic warehousing of buffer inventory → a hard demand-forecasting requirement → discounting or an outlet channel when the forecast is wrong. One stated decision, five inherited ones.

- ## Floor & Decor: two load-bearing pitons

	- Value proposition: most selection, lowest prices, most in-stock inventory. The in-stock leg is the binding one because over half the business is professional installers, who have no warehouse of their own and need same-day pickup or they lose the job.

	- Two load-bearing decisions support it: an ~80,000 sq ft **warehouse store** that collapses back room into selling floor and pushes inventory from a distant DC down to store level, and **specialty focus** — the entire box is flooring, versus roughly 10% of the box at Home Depot and Lowe's.

	- Everything else is downstream, not separately chosen: enough single-category volume to **direct source** from manufacturers (240+ suppliers, some custom SKUs), which cuts distributor margin and simultaneously widens SKU count beyond what a distributor would carry; spare square footage for in-store designers; and category identity strong enough to become the pro's default stop.

	- **Lowe's tried and could not follow.** It expanded hard-surface selection, but its warehouse is already allocated across categories and it does not own the flooring identity. Cohen's framing: bake the best cake in the world using only what is already in your cabinet. The general-merchandise decision was staked years earlier and cannot be pulled out retroactively.

	- **LL Flooring is the negative case**, now bankrupt. Its storefront model — order in store, delivery in days from a distant DC — bought cheaper urban locations, more of them, and higher-touch service. Those are real advantages, but they are structurally incompatible with same-day in-stock for pros. The piton that held it up was the one that killed it.

- ## Amazon vs. Walmart: the store base is the piton

	- Amazon's fulfillment network was purpose-built for one motion: pick millions of individual items, box them, and get the box to a house fast. Walmart's was built for the opposite — full truckloads into a store, unpacked onto shelves — and shelf inventory is not addressable by an order-fulfillment system.

	- Cohen's observed workaround (an employee walking a store aisle to hand-pick a Walmart+ order and drive it over) is the piton showing through. Walmart has since made e-commerce work, but **not by leveraging the store base — by building market fulfillment centers, frequently carved out of store square footage.** The asset it already owned had to be partially converted, not reused.

	- The test he extracts generalizes: is the business optimizing for the variables the customer value proposition actually requires, or optimizing around the pitons it already owns? Starting from zero, Walmart would not build that store base for e-commerce.

- ## RH: the one case where the pitons came out

	- Pre-Friedman Restoration Hardware paired infrequent, high-margin furniture with a high-frequency, low-margin gimmick-goods business (the "atomic robot man" merchandise) whose only job was driving mall traffic between furniture purchases. When the knick-knacks stopped working, comps went negative and the company breached debt covenants.

	- Gary Friedman, passed over at Williams-Sonoma, put \$5M of his own capital in and set out to move the brand upmarket. The binding constraints were inherited: gimmick merchandise incompatible with luxury positioning, and no direct sourcing, which made goods expensive without being high quality.

	- **The restaking was slow and visibly reversible.** He pulled gift merchandise, comps slumped, and he reintroduced some of it around 2004–2005 because the business still needed those sales to stabilize. Mall footprint → galleries up to 100,000 sq ft; third-party logistics → insourced delivery; knick-knacks → in-gallery restaurants doing over \$10M each.

	- The restaurants are functionally the same piton as the knick-knacks — a high-frequency reason to visit a low-frequency store — restaked to serve a luxury proposition instead of a discount one. RH also has no interior-designer channel because customers walk the showroom themselves, so the gallery-and-restaurant traffic draw is not a flourish; it is replacing a distribution channel the model gave up.

- ## The three questions

	- **What are the load-bearing pitons** — the decisions the value proposition cannot survive without? (Warehouse box; single category.)

	- **What is downstream** of them and therefore not independently choosable? (Direct sourcing; SKU breadth; pro attach.)

	- **Does the downstream set actually serve the stated value proposition**, or does it merely serve the existing asset base?

- ## Where the framework is weak

	- **It is nearly unfalsifiable after the fact.** Any winner's decisions can be narrated as a coherent piton network and any loser's as a mismatched one. The examples are selected on outcome: Floor & Decor and RH are chosen as winners, LL Flooring as a corpse. The framework produced no ex-ante call here.

	- **The central claim is undercut by two of its own cases.** RH pulled its pitons out and re-staked them; Walmart built a parallel fulfillment network. So the constraint is cost and time, not impossibility — which makes the useful version of the question quantitative: how much capital, how many years, and how much revenue disruption does restaking cost, versus the challenger's cost of building clean? Cohen gestures at this ("you have to pull the pitons out and restake them") without ever pricing it.

	- **It says nothing about when a piton stops binding.** Lowe's could not become a flooring specialist, but that is a statement about identity and shelf allocation, both of which change on multi-year, not permanent, timescales.

- ## Where it does apply to this repo's names

	- **The framework is the same object this repo has been calling "legacy as a forward performance obligation"** in [[2026-09-21-expert-call-cpu-shortage-gpu-overbuild-correction]]. An ISA is a load-bearing piton: it holds the installed base up and constrains what the next generation is allowed to be. Arm's "Chinese menu" of variants is a piton network that bought broad licensing at the cost of generation-to-generation compatibility, and the architectural license is the gate that decides which licensees can even reach for a new stake.

	- **Merchant vs. captive silicon is a piton choice, not a capability gap.** Hyperscaler captive programs are optimized for one internal workload and one internal customer; that is what makes them cheap per unit of that workload and what makes them non-general. Reading the gap as "captive can't compete" misreads a deliberate constraint as weakness.

	- **CUDA is the clearest live example of a piton that is still load-bearing but visibly narrowing the holder's options** — it supports the entire software position while constraining how aggressively the architecture can break compatibility. The repo's Jalapeño/Rubin TCO comparisons in [[2026-08-25-semianalysis-jalapeno-specs-tco-vs-rubin]] are partly a measurement of how much that constraint costs per generation.

	- **The seat-priced SaaS incumbent facing consumption-priced agents** is the Lowe's problem exactly: the pricing model, the sales compensation plan, and the renewal motion are one decision cluster, and none of the three can be changed alone. Compare against [[2026-03-28-saas-ten-moats-destroyed-weakened-intact-stronger]].
