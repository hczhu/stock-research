- ## AI changes the questions people ask of software

	- **Source**: User-provided excerpt titled “Abstraction”; author, publication date and source URL not supplied. The arguments and historical analogies below are the author's, not independent investment conclusions.
	- **Central argument**: AI's larger opportunity is to let people express business objectives rather than operate software, and then redesign work around what becomes possible.

- ## From operating applications to asking business questions

	- | Existing interaction | Higher-level interaction proposed in the excerpt |
	  |---|---|
	  | Construct database queries and operate application controls | Ask which customers need a call or are likely to churn |
	  | Assemble spreadsheets and reports | Ask whether the quarter is on track, what changed, and why |
	  | Specify each technical step | Ask what data matters and how sensitive the outcome is |
	- Salesforce is an illustration of the interface problem, not evidence about its product performance. The proposed change is from specifying software operations to expressing the problem the user needs solved.

- ## Faster existing work is only the initial phase

	- The author sees two common deployment patterns: **narrow task automation** and **summarizing or collating information across systems**. Both improve existing work without necessarily changing its structure.
	- The deeper change is organizational: when people spend less time preparing data, writing queries or populating spreadsheets, they can ask different questions and undertake different processes. Improving the software alone is not the same as redesigning the job.
	- **SQL analogy**: The author contrasts record retrieval with the ability to ask new questions across data, describing the resulting shift from clerical work toward analysis. AI is presented as another increase in what users can ask computers to do.

- ## Workflow redesign and new business models remain human work

	- Reinventing tasks, jobs and companies requires work by operators, consultants, designers, CEOs and entrepreneurs. The author argues that technical capability does not automatically produce organizational change.
	- **Spotify analogy**: The excerpt contrasts making individual tracks cheaper to obtain with offering access to an entire music catalog through a subscription. Its point is that a new technology can support a different customer proposition, not merely a cheaper version of the old one; that proposition still needs to be invented, built and explained.
	- **Prediction**: AI's eventual impact depends on the development and adoption of new workflows and questions, rather than only on faster execution of today's tasks. The excerpt supplies no adoption timetable, company forecasts or evidence that a particular vendor will capture the value.
