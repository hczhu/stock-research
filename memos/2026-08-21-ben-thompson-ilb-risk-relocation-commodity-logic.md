- ## Ben Thompson on Invest Like The Best: risk relocation and commodity-market logic
	- **Source**: *Invest Like The Best* with Patrick O'Shaughnessy, guest Ben Thompson (Stratechery). Machine transcript with garbled names normalized (Morris Chang, Lou Gerstner, Vercel, Kimi, Strait of Hormuz, ATT). Sponsor reads stripped. Long-form and wide-ranging; the sections below are ordered by investable weight, not by the order discussed.
	- **Thesis**: The organizing idea, stated three separate times about three different companies, is that **risk never disappears — it relocates, and the party that absorbed it has been paid for doing so whether or not the payment is visible in margins.** Applied: Nvidia's circular financing is *a price cut that does not appear in gross margin*; TSMC's capex conservatism converted its overbuild risk into **foregone hyperscaler profits**; the memory oligopoly's discipline converted cycle risk into a **permanent target on its back**. The second organizing idea is that tech understands differentiation and does not understand **commodity markets**, where price is set by the marginal supplier and only cost-to-serve matters.
- ## The Nvidia price cut that does not show up in margins
	- The setup: Nvidia has maintained headline margins into 2026 despite universal competitive attack. Thompson's claim is that **it hasn't actually maintained them** — it has moved the concession off the income statement. The mechanics: Nvidia takes equity in neoclouds, guarantees to buy their compute through 2030, and reportedly **backstops ~25%** of the marquee deal. The counterparty's reward is a **lower cost of capital**.
	- **The step that matters**: *"They get a lower cost of capital because Nvidia assumed risk. Taking on risk has a price."* That assumed risk has an expected value strictly between zero and the full amount — and **"what that is is a price cut."** So: *"We have seen price cuts, they're just manifesting in these very bizarre sort of ways."*
	- **How to use it**: a DCF on Nvidia that models gross margin off reported segment data is **double-counting the pricing power**. The correct adjustment is to charge the expected loss on backstops, guarantees, and equity stakes against realized ASPs. Extends [[2026-06-11-nvidia-coreweave-nebius-circular-financing]] with the specific reframing that circularity is *pricing*, not just balance-sheet engineering.
	- **The competitive endgame is a cost-of-capital fight, not a silicon fight.** Nvidia's real competitors are Google and Amazon, and the asymmetry is that **they sell chips as commodities while Nvidia sells differentiation**. Google already sells ~20% of TPU output to Anthropic; Jassy "practically confirmed" external Trainium sales. Critically, this doesn't cannibalize their clouds because **nobody chooses AWS in order to get Trainium**. And *"the number one advantage that the hyperscalers have [is] lower cost of capital. It's a capital fight."*
	- **On CUDA**: *"CUDA's moat is dramatically diminished because the models don't care what they run on"* — what matters is what's built on top of the models. Still a moat, but not the one people cite. **Nvidia is now bought for fungibility rather than superiority**: if your model is to build capacity and rent it out while reserving the right to reclaim it, you buy Nvidia because Nvidia is the easiest thing to rent.
	- Nvidia's sovereign-cloud and enterprise push is read as **seeking buyers who won't run the chip-versus-chip evaluation** — the Intel precedent being that AMD cleaned Intel out at hyperscalers (scale justifies the porting cost) while government and enterprise kept buying what they already had.
	- **The power bet, and why it is slipping**: Nvidia's hoped-for structural moat is a power-constrained world, where token efficiency decides and Nvidia is most token-efficient. But the US has brought on **more power than expected** (behind-the-meter, West Texas gas, nuclear restarts). *"The longer we have enough power, the more time Amazon has to make Trainium better. The more time Google has to make TPUs competitive."*
- ## TSMC offloaded its risk onto big tech — and scarcity may be what saves Intel
	- TSMC's feared risk is **overcapacity**, and it is unusually costly for them because *"if we build a fab, we expect that fab to run for 30 years"* — an overbuild poisons the system for years. Hence structural conservatism, reinforced by culture.
	- **Where the risk went**: *"Risk doesn't disappear, it just gets handed off. And sometimes that risk doesn't manifest in losing money, it manifests in **not making money**."* Every hyperscaler that could earn more with more compute is carrying TSMC's transferred risk as **foregone revenue and profit**. Compare the capacity-bridge mechanics in [[2026-08-21-tsmc-cross-node-utilization-truck-fleet]].
	- **The compute-shortage timeline is a past-decisions problem**: today's shortage traces to insufficient 2023–24 investment, and **TSMC's growth rate declined in 2023, 2024, and 2025**. Money going in now *"all manifests in compute in 2028 and 2029"* — fab lead times exceed data-center lead times.
	- **The prediction worth tracking**: scarcity is what rescues Intel Foundry. It never made rational sense for a customer to endure Intel when *"we could just go to TSMC. They are awesome."* But with acute shortage and large foregone profits, big tech will now absorb the pain of qualifying Intel and Samsung logic. *"TSMC brought it on themselves… the cure for high prices is high prices."* Thompson expects **a major Intel foundry partner announcement at some point** and treats it as a big deal.
	- **The elegant formulation of the whole geopolitical problem**: *"The way to solve the geopolitical problem of dependence on TSMC is to come up with a compute use case that is so massive that everyone is economically incentivized to bring other people up to speed. Then we get the geopolitical insurance for free."* Because nobody voluntarily buys insurance with negative expected value — which is also his answer to why supply chains never actually leave China.
	- Historical note: Morris Chang returning post-Great Recession, reversing the spending cut, and investing through the downturn on the strength of the iPhone launch is what created the leading-edge franchise. Thompson puts him "one-of-one on the Mount Rushmore" of tech executives.
- ## Memory: the discipline that created a target
	- Consolidation to three players produced discipline — *"they're not colluding but they all are on the same page about let's not do that"* — which is exactly why the industry was slow to recognize a **genuine secular shift in memory demand**.
	- **The insight worth keeping**, via a Strait of Hormuz analogy: a coercive capability *"is more effective if you don't use it, because then it's always hanging out there as something you could do."* Once used, everyone builds around it — new pipelines, new ports — and by 2035 the threat is inert. *"My concern for the memory makers is they might have done the same thing."*
	- Evidence already visible: Apple lobbying to qualify Chinese memory, and **"what is the number one focus of algorithmic changes? How can we use less memory."** The pricing power is real and is simultaneously **manufacturing its own demand destruction and second-sourcing**. Sits directly against the bull framing in [[2026-07-21-memory-positioning-bull-bear-debate]] and the supply history in [[2026-08-21-sk-hynix-hbm-history-mr-muf-moat]].
- ## Commodity-market logic, and the challenge to management's overbuild story
	- The shipping model: a ship's cost is depreciation (already paid); **marginal cost is fuel, crew, and port fees**. So you run the ship regardless and bid the container price down toward marginal cost. Paper losses can be enormous because accounting losses include depreciation — but *"the depreciation is an accounting [artifact]. You already paid the money."* Exit only happens when price falls below **cash** marginal cost, which then tightens supply and restarts the cycle. COVID took containers from **\$3–4K to \$17–18K**; the two-year build time meant everyone ordered simultaneously.
	- **The direct challenge to Jassy and Nadella**: both have said they build shells and only buy GPUs against known demand. Thompson: *"I'm not sure — I think [that] is a lot of BS, because the reality is, if you've built the shell, that money is sitting there. You're not going to let it just sit there."* Read alongside the flexibility framework Hood presents in [[MSFT-2026-Q2]] and the capital-cycle disclosure in [[2026-07-30-jassy-aws-data-center-server-capital-cycle]] — this is the strongest available counterargument to both.
	- **The sharpest single line for underwriting AI capex**: *"You're measuring your payback period in a time of scarcity. Is that payback period going to hold in a time of abundance?"*
	- **Why commodification is bullish for the world and bearish for margins**: differentiated products have structurally **lower TAMs** because willingness to pay varies — *"Apple is never going to serve the whole world by selling a device, whereas Google can, because it's free."* The internet is a commodity and it changed the world; so intelligence ending up a commodity, and changing the world, is not strange. Commodities change the world; they just don't hold margins.
- ## The capital curve: the near-term constraint is money, not compute or power
	- Capex path cited: **~\$800B this year, ~\$1.3T next year** on current estimates, with installed compute absorbed immediately.
	- **The descent down the capital stack, in roughly one year**: free cash flow → debt markets (blown through "kind of incredible[ly]" fast) → **Google issuing equity** → Nvidia's **\$500B vehicle** reaching for pension funds and insurance floats. *"What's after that? Where's the money come after that?"* The benign path is flipping back to FCF funding; the gap between here and there is where a blowup lives.
	- **The railroad analogy is specifically about duration mismatch**, not about mania: building a railroad paid back over a decade or more while financing was short-term, and *"the world just ran out of money"* in the 1870s. Hence: *"everyone talks about, are we going to have enough compute? Are we going to have enough electricity? Maybe the nearest term question is **are we going to have enough money**."*
	- **What survives a bust matters more than whether there is one.** Dot-com left fiber — Google built its business on cheap post-bust dark fiber and *"our core internet still runs on WorldCom fiber."* Railroads still throw off cash (BNSF's earnings are literally what Berkshire routed into Google). For AI: **GPUs don't last, data centers partially, so it has to be power.** A world that overbuilt power is *"an amazing world to be [in]"* — we have never experienced energy abundance.
- ## Google as Berkshire: See's Candies → BNSF
	- The mechanic: See's Candies has spectacular *percentage* margins but almost no reinvestment capacity, so **absolute** profit is capped. BNSF has far worse margins but such large absolute dollars that **one recent year of BNSF free cash flow exceeded See's Candies' entire lifetime output**. *"Once your capital gets so large, you start operating in a world of absolute numbers as opposed to percentage numbers."*
	- **Applied to Google**: Search is the purest zero-marginal-cost aggregator ever built — the See's Candies. AI has a TAM of *"basically all white collar work"* and eventually more, at structurally worse margins but vastly larger absolute profits. Hence the equity issuance, which Thompson found shocking *"because it's Google — they can't raise money?"* His resolution: **a smaller percentage of an astronomically larger pie, and nobody complains.** Berkshire's Google position is therefore not just an investment but **a template** for what Google is becoming.
- ## Where the models can and cannot go
	- **The verifiability challenge, stated precisely**: AI is spectacular at coding and math, but those are verifiable domains. *"What is the evidence that being very good at verifiable domains cleanly translates to being very good at unverifiable domains, or domains that have very long verification loops?"* He rejects the standard rebuttal — chess and Go were **bounded**, and scale was always the plausible answer. He wants an example in *"a genuinely new, unknowable space."*
	- **The extraordinary counter-idea**: models trained on the internet distilled *"the end state of human thought"* — the typed output — not the **traces**: the reasoning, the emotion, the process that produced the text. *"What if the actual payoff from Neuralink is capturing the traces of human thought?"* i.e. **verifiability may be solved by acquiring a new data class rather than by better algorithms.** That reframes brain-interface and process-capture assets as potential training-data plays rather than medical devices.
	- **The bullish half, which is more investable than the debate**: an enormous share of economic activity already lives in verifiable-ish domains — *"a lot of people in the world are kind of like sentient AIs… they operate very well in verifiable domains."* So **even with zero further model improvement, the economic opportunity is massive.** He calls himself a *"reluctant accelerationist."* Medicine is named as both the largest opportunity and the most obstructed (regulation and record access).
	- **Open-weight models are not free**: *"the talk about them being free is bizarre to me, because it's marginal costs."* You skip the R&D, not the inference — and Kimi in particular is *"very expensive to serve. The cost per answer is significantly higher."*
	- **An underexplored competitive axis**: if a lab can apply AI to optimizing its own stack, *"is your cost to serve structurally lower than anyone else's?"* Frontier capability and unit economics may compound together rather than trade off.
	- **The inference-cost spread breaks aggregation theory's zero-marginal-cost assumption unevenly**: the free chat user costs *"basically similar to serving them a web page,"* while a test-time-scaling user is *"directly marginal cost — every second longer you're thinking is costing more money."* These are not the same business, and blended gross margin hides it.
	- **Frontier opacity risk**: the Mythos/Hugging Face scare may cause labs to simply stop releasing, so outsiders lose calibration entirely — *"everyone's basing their understanding of Mythos on Fable, but how good is Fable actually relative to Mythos? That gap is only going to increase over time."*
- ## Advertising as the verification machine — and Meta's content-margin asymmetry
	- **The best AI monetization right now is not at the labs**: it is the incremental gain at Google and Meta. The reason is a verification loop nobody else has — how do you verify a generated ad image is good? **Does it sell.** The ad marketplace is *"a gazillion A/B tests,"* a **global-scale verification machine whose verifiers are humans deciding whether to click and buy**. Most ads are throwaway and that is fine.
	- **The next step is prediction, not matching**: today's targeting is crude embedding-vector similarity between user attributes and ad attributes. *"What do LLMs do? LLMs predict."* Meta moves to *"this person probably wants to see this next,"* then goes and finds it. **A few percentage points of relevance improvement is worth billions** — which by itself justifies frontier-model investment. This is the strongest available answer to the Meta-capex skepticism in [[2026-08-03-stratechery-meta-timing-problems-financial-tail]] and [[META-2026-Q2]], and Thompson's complaint is that **Meta does not tell this story** — Zuckerberg shares Altman's discomfort with ads, Sandberg used to fill that role, and *"that hole never got filled."*
	- **The content-cost asymmetry is the non-obvious one**: YouTube pays creators, so content carries a marginal cost (though revenue-share-after-the-fact beats Netflix's pay-upfront). **Meta pays nothing for content.** Therefore **AI-generated content could be margin-accretive for YouTube** — inference cost below the revenue share it replaces — while for Meta it is **margin-dilutive**, because it replaces something that was free.
	- **Why TikTok was a blind spot**: TikTok was classified as a social network and is not one — it is an entertainment product where following is irrelevant. The insight was that **limiting content to your social graph is an artificial constraint**, and it is the absolute-versus-percentage logic again: even if the share of great content is infinitesimal, at sufficient volume the absolute amount of great content is enormous. Reels was the forced response.
	- The bullish twist: in a world saturated with AI interaction, **the desire for verified human connection may make actual social networking valuable again.**
- ## Microsoft: the Gerstner playbook, and the pricing trap
	- **The historical template is IBM in the 1990s, not Google.** Gerstner's insight was that IBM was *"pretty mediocre at everything"* — the price of monopoly, because *"once you've been a monopoly, you kind of lose your capacity to be good… and once you lose that muscle, it's gone."* So don't break it up; **the biggest asset is being big.** IBM built middleware between mainframes and the web, plus a huge consulting force, and bought itself a **30-year lease on life** by bringing corporate America online.
	- **Microsoft's AI play is the same play**: middleware and harness, dependable, *"backwards compatible to the 80s,"* managing model churn on the customer's behalf so they don't hand the crown jewels to the labs. The admitted cost: *"middleware saws off the sharp edges. You sort of get a lowest common denominator."* And the oldest enterprise motion — Oracle sold "don't get locked into IBM" and then locked customers in harder — is exactly what every cloud portability pitch is doing now.
	- **Why Microsoft has the money**: *"the reason why Microsoft has [large] free cash flow… their play is, we're going to play all these off each other."* Not being on the frontier is what funds the dividend. They build for **inference, not training**, so their demand-response story is more credible — *"they're not having to tell a fungibility story."*
	- **The E7 critique — the most directly investable segment of the episode.** Microsoft is moving to **\$100 per user per month with usage billed on top**, and Thompson thinks the position is fraught for three reasons:
		- **It untethers revenue from headcount.** The old model was thoughtless: a license was baked into the loaded cost of an employee, decided once at hire. *"It was kind of a thoughtless revenue stream for Microsoft."*
		- **Companies budget annually, not monthly.** *"This idea we're going to be thinking about our budgetary allotment on a monthly basis doesn't compute."*
		- **It invites unbundling.** Once a customer reviews a usage bill monthly, they start asking *"what am I paying for? How good is each of these products? Should I start splitting this out?"*
		- The bind: heavy AI users cost far more than \$100/month so Microsoft **had** to meter them, but it needs customers to think *a little* and not *too much* — *"that breaks the model in very surprising ways."*
		- **This directly contradicts management's own framing.** In [[MSFT-2026-Q2]] Hood presents E7 and seat-plus-consumption as TAM expansion and sells token **observability and manageability** as the feature. Thompson's read is that making token spend visible is precisely what erodes the thoughtless-renewal dynamic. **Both cannot be right, and the resolution should show up in M365 Commercial cloud growth and seat retention over the next few quarters.**
	- **The deeper threat**: *"why are we using Microsoft products again? Because we did before."* Systems of record are sticky because migration is tedious and repetitive — *"oh, AI is actually surprisingly good at that."* And Microsoft's real asset is the **user interface**, which is what Codex, Claude, and Cowork are *"aimed like an arrow to the heart"* of. Strategy sound, position *"desperate in an existential way — and also in a they-might-pull-it-off-because-they're-desperate sort of way."*
- ## Setups: Amazon, Apple, and the labs
	- **Amazon is his pick**, on one repeatable mechanism: **they are their own first best customer**, which provides the scale to launch anything internally and then sell it externally. AWS was not spare capacity — it came from wanting internal compute with a pure API surface and no meetings. Logistics ran the reverse path (outsource → build → resell). **Graviton and Trainium worked because managed services hide the silicon**: a customer buying Redshift is not told what runs underneath, so Amazon could deploy early, weak processors, earn the volume, and iterate to the point of external salability. Same pattern now running through AI services. See [[AMZN-2026-Q2]].
	- **Apple — "better lucky than good."** Owning customer access means suppliers come to them (the classic aggregator position), so they can source AI as needed. If consumers mostly want a chatbot, Apple can serve one, eventually **on-device — "using the customer's electricity,"** with no inference cost to Apple. Physical goods and retail insulate them further.
		- **The structural objection**: *"on what basis should we expect Apple to be good at this? AI is this probabilistic endeavor. Apple is the king of deterministic products."* Ship an iPhone once and it must be right; Apple has never had an iPhone recall. Conclusion: *"I'm fine with Apple not doing AI. I want them to keep making great devices."*
		- **The real risk is the Microsoft-mobile trap**: Microsoft didn't miss mobile, they assumed the PC stayed the center and shipped a small PC. The question is whether Apple assumes the phone is permanently the center while ambient AI turns out to be the disruptive form factor.
	- **The labs**: OpenAI and Anthropic are riskiest with the biggest upside — *"never discount the power of belief. They think they're creating God. The most impactful things in history have usually been fueled by religion,"* OpenAI as mainline, Anthropic as evangelical. Needing revenue to survive is itself an advantage. **Google just needs search to not die too quickly.** Meta being on the frontier at all is *"one of the purest manifestations of founder energy"* and would not happen under any other CEO. **xAI/SpaceX is the weakest case** — data centers in space are so differentiated that owning a model may be unnecessary, so *"why are you wasting billions and billions of dollars in the meantime?"* He likes the Cursor acquisition.
- ## Consumer monetization: the Dropbox lesson, replayed at 100x
	- **Two things the industry relearns every decade**: consumers will not pay for software, and **consumers do not care about being productive** — *"I spent all day working. Why do I want to come home and be more productive?"* Enterprises pay because productivity gains have a computable return; consumers have no such calculus.
	- Dropbox is the canonical arc: beloved consumer product, Jobs' *"you're a feature, not a company,"* a two-year trough, and a ground-up rebuild for enterprise permissions and controls because consumer payments were never going to be enough.
	- **OpenAI replayed it "at like 100x"** — sold consumer subscriptions, sold a lot, *"but they didn't sell enough."* The counterfactual is the strong claim: had they leaned into advertising as soon as ChatGPT hit, *"I think they would have a killer product right now. I think Google would be in much bigger trouble. I think Meta would be in much bigger trouble."*
	- **Why advertising is structurally superior for consumer scale**: **the advertiser bears the price increase, so there is zero consumer elasticity constraint** — versus Netflix, which must ask how far it can raise prices before subscribers revolt. *"Charging people money is hard. Giving people things for free is easy."*
	- Current state read as incoherent: OpenAI pivoted to ads (checkout, retailer connections, conversion tracking) **at the same moment** it needs to chase enterprise because Anthropic is winning there — *"I'm not quite sure what they're doing there."*
- ## Geopolitics, and one figure to verify
	- **He thinks decisive US AI superiority would be dangerous, not desirable.** In a world where AI confers real military advantage, *"what is the game-theory optimal response of China? To blow up TSMC."* And the premise that the US could be indifferent to that is fantastical, because dependence on China for fabs, actuators, and precursors is underappreciated and *"not something that is going to be fixed outside of a conflict"* — reshoring is an insurance premium too expensive to pay while competitors don't. Apple's India diversification is cited as the limit case: real, but not an exit.
	- **His preferred equilibrium is roughly the status quo**: OpenAI and Anthropic on the frontier, Chinese labs capable and distilling to stay **6–9 months behind**, which he judges favorable to the US and possibly more durable than assumed — closing that last 6–9 months is the hard part. His policy objection is that US responses have trended toward *"trying to be like China"* when the advantage lies in more openness and less top-down control.
	- **One number to check before reusing**: Thompson states Microsoft had *"\$40 billion of free cash flow last quarter"* and paid *"a \$10 billion dividend last quarter."* The FY26 Q4 call recorded in [[MSFT-2026-Q2]] gives **FCF of \$19.6B** and **\$10.2B total returned** via dividends *and* buybacks. The dividend figure is roughly consistent; **the FCF figure is off by about 2x** against the reported quarter — possibly conflating operating cash flow (\$55.4B) or a different period. **Do not carry the \$40B forward.**
