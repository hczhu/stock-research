- **Thesis frame**: The core problem is that **foundation models have no network effect or winner-take-all lever** — unlike Windows/Google/iOS, there's nothing you can pull to pull permanently ahead; 3–6+ labs just leapfrog every few weeks. That forces a binary long-run outcome: LLMs become **commodity infrastructure sold near marginal cost (value captured up-stack, à la TSMC)**, or **2–3 players capture value like Windows/iOS ecosystems**. Evans leans toward the former, which frames OpenAI's whole strategy as **swapping mindshare for hard assets before the window closes**. Second big idea: **"a better model doesn't solve the problem"** (the jagged frontier), and the winners come from a reframed **software taxonomy** where AI massively expands "improvised/ephemeral software."

- ## The Foundation-Model Problem (no moat)
	- **No network effect**: "there's no lever you can decide to pull whereby you'll pull ahead of everybody else and they won't catch up." Contrast **Google vs Bing** — no amount of Microsoft money/effort lets Bing catch Google (durable moat); LLMs have no such lever. Result: **3–6+ frontier labs leapfrogging every few weeks/months**; you can fall off the ladder (capital/difficulty) but can't build an unassailable lead.
	- **The TSMC analogy** (the key one): chips have no network effect, but each generation gets harder/costlier → one leader, 2–3 a generation behind, 5–10 further back. **TSMC makes lots of money but doesn't capture all the tech industry's value** — a consumer doesn't know TSMC made their iPhone chip, and TSMC gets no cut of App Store sales. That's the "makes money but not platform economics" outcome for LLMs.
	- **Cloud is *not* the right comparison**: AWS/Azure/GCP are actually different businesses (AWS infra, MSFT services, Google catching up), and crucially **nobody signs into a SaaS app with their AWS account** — "that's not the right level of abstraction, it's just infrastructure." LLMs today feel like that: infrastructure, not the aggregation layer.

- ## OpenAI's Strategic Bind ("if you're Sam Altman…")
	- **The setup (verbatim logic)**: commodity technology; competing against players with **giant legacy cash flows** (Google/Meta); **no own infrastructure**; **no real differentiation**; but **massive mindshare** → so you **swap mindshare for hard assets and talk your way into a self-fulfilling prophecy**.
	- **Usage is "a mile wide, an inch deep"**: **900M weekly actives, but ~10% use it daily, ~50% weekly-or-less, and only ~5% pay**. Most people with a ChatGPT account "can't think of anything to do with it." (Evans grabbed ~300 Reddit screenshots of OpenAI's year-end usage graphic: **~80% of users hit return <1,000 times all year** — i.e., <3×/day; most aren't daily users, so **there's no memory/stickiness for them**.)
	- **The scramble for durability**: reported deals with **PE firms**, a **video app (Sora) that didn't work**, "the second or third app store now," and an **e-commerce integration** — but commerce integrations are brutally hard (millions of merchants, billions of SKUs; **Google and Meta failed at it twice**), so OpenAI concluded it "probably can't build that ourselves right now." The whole effort is converting lightly-engaged mindshare into "developers, users, corporate accounts — something locked in that doesn't just depend on having the best model next week." Directly matches the Codex-refocus pivot in [[2026-07-16-openai-superapp-chatgpt-codex-whither-chat-stratechery]].
	- **Monetizing the free user is the unsolved piece**: little of the usage is commerce, "loads more people use it for porn" (unmonetizable); so they chase **commerce take-rates + advertising** — and hire ad people from Meta.

- ## "A Better Model Doesn't Solve the Problem" — the jagged frontier
	- **A year of huge model progress (reasoning, RL) did not solve the product problem.** "What is a better model?" is almost meaningless for most use cases — like the late-night bit showing people last year's iPhone as "new" and they can't tell.
	- **The math of jaggedness**: if you ask a model to compile 50 things and it gets 8–12 wrong instead of 10, **you still have to check all 50 — nothing changed.** Value arrives at **right-vs-not-right thresholds**, not at 90%→91%→95%. Where you need *the* right answer (not "sort of" right), "better" is literally meaningless.
	- **Capabilities expand → the frontier gets *more* jagged**, so it's *harder* to predict whether it'll do X or Y (e.g., models "struggle massively to read PDFs" — not intuitable from the outside). This is also why **agents are a fuzzy term ("like metaverse")** — a spectrum from L0 (calls tools invisibly) to L5 (reorders your dog food autonomously, Stripe's framing).

- ## The Software Taxonomy (the richest new framework)
	- Evans' anti-"vibe-code-your-own-Stripe" reframe — a border taxonomy of software:
		- **Systems of record** (big ERP/SAP): store the same data the same way across 50k people / a billion transactions — "you can't have David Brent making his own." Safe from DIY.
		- **Vertical tools**: where the monolith is too inflexible, you unbundle into dedicated apps — hence the typical big US company runs **400–500 vertical SaaS apps**. (frame.io = unbundling Google Sheets + Dropbox + email for video workflows.)
		- **Improvised software**: the Excel / CSV-export / email / Perl-script middle where a process **isn't repeated enough (or nobody realized it's big enough) to justify a dedicated tool.**
	- **AI's impact**: coding gets much cheaper/easier **and** a whole class of things you *couldn't* do in software now becomes possible → **"way more software,"** picking up use cases that were too small or previously impossible to automate.
	- **Ephemeral software** (the new category): instead of exporting a CSV to Excel, you ask ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini to do the one-off analysis — "look across all these PDFs/PowerPoints and tell me what's missing" — the fuzzy-answer task LLMs suit. **Nobody vibe-codes their own ERP or frame.io**; they generate improvised/ephemeral analyses that are the AI-native analog of an Excel one-off.

- ## Jevons / Price Elasticity — "way more software," not "software is dead"
	- **The staircase**: mainframe (~5 apps/company) → on-prem (dozens) → cloud (hundreds) → **AI (way more)**. Cheaper/easier to build + go to market unlocks two orders of magnitude more software, screws some incumbents, and automates tasks that couldn't be automated before (the SaaS pattern, repeated).
	- **Elasticity, three steps**: cheaper/easier to do a thing → (1) same thing for less money, (2) **more for more money** (spreadsheets *grew* finance headcount — a week per DCF meant one DCF; 10 minutes per DCF means many), (3) **things you couldn't do at all** ("you can't run a quant fund with 10,000 people instead of a computer, no matter how many people" — like horses can't pull an express train). The interesting value is step (3).
	- **"Software as a stock-market category"**: way more software gets built and used — but *by whom* is a combination of who understands software **and** who has deeply understood the problem (frame.io wasn't built by a video-suite operator). Dumb rules-based "expert-system" software gets eaten; new AI-native software gets created.

- ## The TAM Fallacy & "It Depends"
	- **"AI TAM = global GDP" is the Uber-TAM fallacy** (the NYU professor who valued Uber at "all taxi spend"). Charging AI at a **human-wage price "doesn't seem right"** — automation only happens where **labor is expensive** and needs an **ROI vs a person** (1930s southern Italy: one car in the province because you can send a peasant to walk 16 hours for free). GDP won't jump from single-digit to double-digit growth.
	- **Physical-asset unbundling** is the real lens: the internet unbundled physical assets from products; **if your defensibility was a physical asset that stopped mattering, your model exploded** (regional newspapers died; Disney was fine). Same lens, opposite outcomes: **Uber demolished taxis** (NYC Uber rides ~2–3× old yellow-cab volume; yellow cabs down ~75%) while **Airbnb was mostly additive** to hotels (business travel is ~half the market and wants a Marriott with room service).
	- **You can't precisely "score professions' AI exposure."** Directionally right, like a 1997 internet analysis — mostly true in direction, but you'd never have predicted Uber from it, and the precise numbers (this job 96.5, that one 78) are "ludicrous." (Direct caution on the GDPval-style benchmark framing.)

- ## The Back-Office Reality (outside view vs inside view)
	- **Sorrell/WPP**: AI's big advertising impact is **not "more creative assets" — it's operations**, the buildings full of people doing boring spreadsheet/fax work loading ads into Facebook. Outsiders say "AI will make more pictures"; insiders know the value is in the back office.
	- **Audit costs have been ~flat since Sarbanes-Oxley** despite decades of software; digging into the '70s–'80s, the 50-page reports on what changed audit "don't mention computers" — 700 other things mattered more. Lesson: **the outside view of where AI hits an industry is usually wrong; the hard part is somewhere else.**
	- **Enterprise sentiment**: not bewildered — everyone deployed Copilot ("okay, that wasn't very successful"), now has a pile of pilots (some in production, some not). Most industries are "well down the path of using AI to do a thing we already did, but better," plus a few newly-possible things. The unsolved question everywhere: **"yes, but what's the *new* new thing?" — and nobody in tech knows either.**

- ## Capex, Leverage & the Bubble
	- **Financial gravity on capex**: **Meta will spend >50% of *revenue* (not profit) on capex this year**; Google/MSFT not far behind; **they can't double to 100% of revenue next year**, and leases/borrowing push the true number double-digit-% higher. This is "the only hard financial limit we have." Nvidia (~\$70–90B TTM FCF) "can't give money to TSMC fast enough" — TSMC won't triple capacity (cyclical), ASML can't ship fast enough.
	- **Vendor financing / circular deals / SPVs = leverage** — "nothing wrong in principle if disclosed," but "leverage is always fine until it isn't." Oracle/neoclouds flagged as where leverage could end badly (echoes [[2026-06-11-nvidia-coreweave-nebius-circular-financing]]).
	- **Bubble framing**: "this time it's different" (double meaning — bubble-deniers are wrong *and* each bubble genuinely is different). Not dot-com: **no no-profit consumer IPOs, not retail-speculation-driven, not VC-funded.** Still can be a bubble; over-investment is certain, "smoking holes" unclear, timing uncallable.
	- **The one true novelty vs prior platform shifts**: **we don't know the physical limits** of LLMs. A paper next week could cut compute 10–100× (unlike chips, where no one could publish "same performance at 1% of transistors"). So far the surprise runs the *other* way — we keep inventing ways to use **10–100× more tokens** (chat → reasoning → agents → video). Token-usage forecasting is like predicting bandwidth in 2003: several unknown multipliers × Nadella's "inference cost halves every 3 months."

- ## Investment Read-Throughs
	- **OpenAI / frontier labs (pre-IPO)**: the "swap mindshare for hard assets / talk into a self-fulfilling prophecy" framing is the sharpest articulation yet of the bear case behind the raises and infra deals — and it dovetails with the Codex-refocus pivot ([[2026-07-16-openai-superapp-chatgpt-codex-whither-chat-stratechery]]) and the no-durable-moat evidence from open weights ([[2026-07-16-kimi-k3-open-weight-frontier-commoditization]]). "OpenAI = the next Netscape" is the podcast's provocation.
	- **Software / SaaS (nuanced, not "dead")**: **"way more software"** — systems-of-record incumbents (SAP-class) are safe from DIY; vertical tools proliferate; a **new improvised/ephemeral-software category** emerges. Bullish the application/agent layer and problem-first builders; bearish undifferentiated middleware and **incumbents whose defensibility was a now-irrelevant physical asset or artificial friction** ("marginal cost of arguing is now zero" — health-insurance-style moats).
	- **Big Tech ([[$META]], [[$GOOG]], [[$MSFT]])**: can absorb commodity LLMs into already-profitable businesses and **needn't monetize the LLM itself** (Meta doesn't sell its cloud) — the structural advantage over standalone labs. But the **>50%-of-revenue capex ceiling** is the hard constraint to watch.
	- **[[$NVDA]] / [[$TSM]] / memory**: token growth (10–100×) vs efficiency gains (halving/3mo) makes net demand genuinely unknowable; TSMC "captures money but not all value"; the capex-can't-compound-forever point is the macro governor.
	- **[[$ORCL]] / neoclouds**: leverage/vendor-financing/SPV risk — "fine until it isn't."
	- **Builder takeaway**: most SaaS was a **"database wrapper"** where someone saw a problem + the people who have it + turned it 90°; the hard part was never the code — it's **problem-selection + GTM**, which didn't change. Best outcomes likely come from founders who **started from the problem**, not the tech.

- ## What to Monitor
	- Whether LLMs settle toward **commodity-infra (marginal-cost) pricing** or **oligopoly pricing discipline** among 2–3 players.
	- OpenAI's conversion of 900M lightly-engaged WAU into locked-in developer/corporate/paying relationships (attach, paying-mix, ads/commerce take-rate).
	- The **>50%-of-revenue capex** trajectory at Meta/Google/MSFT and any efficiency paper that resets the compute curve 10×+.
	- Emergence of genuinely **AI-native software** (the step-3 "couldn't-do-before" category) vs. more "PDF-catalog-on-the-website" incrementalism.
