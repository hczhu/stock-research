- **Source**: RepVue Data, "Observability · Pricing Power," two pages, dated 0916 (ratings to 15 Sep 2026). Built from RepVue's competitor survey (6 Feb – 28 Sep 2026, each respondent names three rivals), rep reviews and Q&A since 1 Apr 2025, and 12-month quota-carrying deal-size and product-market-fit ratings. **This is sales-rep data, not customer or financial data.** RepVue's own limits: price comments get thin below the top three (3 for Grafana, 7 each for New Relic and Elastic); deal size is the median of reps' *average* deal, not a unit price, so a larger deal can reflect more scope as easily as a higher price; and the survey started in February 2026, so there is no year-over-year comparison on that side yet.

- **Thesis**: Datadog is the only vendor that holds list price *and* has the product rating to justify it, and its rivals now treat it as the incumbent to beat. Their way in is the bill, not the product. The part of this report that matters most for [[Datadog-DDOG-thesis]] is Grafana Labs. Its product-fit rating and Enterprise deal size rose faster than any other vendor's, and both Datadog and Dynatrace reps describe losing accounts to it. That contradicts the thesis's "Low-Medium" threat rating for Grafana, although Grafana's most recent quarter weakens the case.

	- **Datadog stands out in two ways.** It is ranked #1 in every resample, named 252 times by reps at 33 companies, and rivals look up to it far more than it looks back at them (gaze −10.7pp). Its own reps mention price increases in 4.0% of comments, less than a third of Splunk's 12.6%, and describe "zero flexibility on discounting." Its fit rating held flat while the peer group fell 0.09.

	- **The cost of holding price shows up at the top end.** Datadog's Enterprise median fell 3.5% while the peer group's rose 8.0%, an 11.5-point gap. Dynatrace and Splunk now carry Enterprise medians **1.44× larger** than Datadog's, and Grafana's is **1.65× larger**.

	- **The report reads the SMB figure too harshly.** It says holding price costs Datadog "in the land," pointing to its \$11,725 SMB/mid-market median (14th percentile). That median is **17–26% of every peer's** — but it also *rose* 17.3%. A small first deal is how usage-based, land-and-expand selling is designed to work, and RepVue itself says deal size reflects scope as much as price. The Enterprise decline is the real signal; the SMB level is not.

- ## Two kinds of incumbency

	- **The older incumbents keep accounts with contract terms.** Dynatrace uses "steep discounts" and five-year lock-ins. Splunk relies on Cisco bundles of hardware, services and software, and on "cheap or free Phantom / Splunk SOAR licenses" inside enterprise-wide license agreements. New Relic pushes "early renewals on heavily discounted multi-year deals." These vendors retain accounts through contracts rather than product.

	- **Datadog keeps accounts through the product and the cost of migrating away.** An Elastic rep: "We lose deals because developers love Datadog and its a difficult migration historically." Rivals concede the product rather than the price: "Lose. Users love the DataDog UI" (Elastic); "Straight up observability feature to feature we struggle" (Splunk); "Easy to use & buy" (Dynatrace); "current product is not viewed as competitive" (IBM).

	- **Price is how challengers attack Datadog.** It is the leading theme in rival comments about Datadog, at 57% (31 of 54). "Where Elastic wins: Bill shock." "Datadog's per-host pricing leads to astronomical bills for companies based on K8s" (Grafana). "Win: Pricing Lose: Solution Consolidation" (Sentry). Datadog's own reps agree: "a lot of companies dismiss Datadog from evaluations for perceived cost," and "severe pressure from new, way cheaper solutions." This is the same whale-bill risk the Datadog thesis already lists, now reported directly by sales reps.

	- **The market splits by customer type, and the deal sizes match.** Hybrid large enterprises favor the older incumbents: "We typically beat Datadog because they don't do well with complex large enterprises that have hybrid environments" (Dynatrace). Cloud-native customers favor Datadog. A Datadog rep adds that its "strict definition of an Enterprise customer as 5000+ FTE really narrows the TAM." **Europe is Dynatrace's strongest region**, according to three independent sources.

- ## Grafana Labs is the test of the Datadog thesis

	- **The data for Grafana conflicts with the thesis.** Its fit rose **+0.33 to 4.27**, the 97th percentile of peer-group change and the only material fit gain in the group. Its Enterprise median rose **79% to \$230,000**, the largest in the peer group. Reps at the market leaders report losing to it: "We're losing more and more deals to competitors like Grafana" (Datadog Enterprise rep, Nov 2025); "We have recently been loosing lots of accounts to Grafana" (Dynatrace). The Datadog thesis rates Grafana Cloud a **Low-Medium** threat, with "inferior dashboards and logs."

	- **Two data points weaken the case, and they are the most recent ones.** Grafana's fit in the latest three months fell back to **3.95 on 20 ratings**. The panel of returning raters also does not confirm the rise (**−4 on 25**). A 79% jump in the Enterprise median across 63 ratings can come from a shift in which deals are being reported as easily as from real traction. Grafana is not yet proven, but it is the one challenger showing gains on both product rating and deal size.

	- **Datadog's defense relies on complexity, which weakens as AI tools get better.** "Win if use cases are more complex than 3 pillars of observability." "Most technology teams are not competent enough to manage Grafana at scale effectively." That argument is about the cost of operating Grafana, not a product advantage. Agents that run infrastructure reduce exactly that cost.

- ## The rest of the field

	- **Splunk is now competing mainly in security.** **47%** of Splunk reps' named rivals are security vendors. The mechanism they name is Microsoft bundling: "Sentinel rolled into E5 and therefore its 'free'." A Splunk rep: "people dont think of Splunk for replacing datadog." Its fit has fallen 0.44 over two years to the 36th percentile. It is the only vendor priced above its rivals with a bottom-half fit rating, and its own reps say it has "lost the pricing premium." Reps disagree on whether Cisco's ownership helps: "Cisco integration is creating more value. 1+1=3" versus "since Cisco acquisition Splunk has been going downhill."

	- **Elastic competes on price without a strong product rating.** Its fit is 3.64 (down 0.34) with a panel reading of −25. Its own reps say its "value prop is purely based on price," and rivals place it in logs rather than full observability. Its Enterprise median rose 66.7%, but its SMB median fell 19.2%. One rep: "the Elastic observability product is not gaining traction in a very crowded market."

	- **New Relic is losing on deal size in both segments** (Enterprise −16.1%, SMB −13.0%). Rivals point to its private-equity ownership and its "recurring pricing changes."

	- **Every vendor competes against free open source.** For Datadog, free tools set a price floor at startups and SMBs. For Grafana and Elastic, the free version is their own product: "It's open source; you are selling against yourself and the competition."

- ## What comes next

	- Newer entrants run on open standards and low prices, and rivals rarely name them (Chronosphere 3 times, Dash0 twice). Rival comments raise three new threads: **OpenTelemetry and Prometheus** replacing proprietary agents (Chronosphere on Datadog); **"agent native traces, not spans level telemetry"** (LangChain on Datadog), meaning tracing built for AI agents rather than the per-request spans current tools collect; and **ClickHouse**, a database increasingly used to store observability data ("Lose. Clickhouse is starting to gain market momentum," Elastic). The LangChain comment matters most for the Datadog thesis's AI-observability wedge: it suggests the new AI-agent workloads may be monitored by tools built specifically for them rather than added onto Datadog.
