- ## Stratechery: Dara Khosrowshahi on Aggregation and Autonomy
	- **Source**: Stratechery Interview, Ben Thompson with Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi, February 13, 2025. Note this predates the memo date — treat the AV timelines below as dated forecasts to score against, not current guidance.
	- **Thesis**: Dara's central economic claim is that Uber's ~25% utilization uplift more than covers its ~20% take rate, so an AV operator is better off on Uber's network than going direct — the quantified core of the "aggregation beats integration" argument. The strategic hedge is a hybrid AV + human network, where humans supply peak-load variability that fixed-cost AV fleets cannot.

- ## The five preconditions for AV commercialization
	- Dara's checklist for when AVs become a real business rather than a money-loser — useful as a scoring rubric over time:
		- **Superhuman safety record** — not merely better than a human, "multiple times better," because society tolerates human failure far more than machine failure.
		- **Regulatory pathway** — national, state, and city; US is a leader, Middle East opening, but progress is inconsistent and slow.
		- **Cost-effective scaled hardware platform** — with redundancy, compute, and sensor kits. Said to be **3–4 years out**; over the next 2–3 years, "with the exception of Tesla, it doesn't exist."
		- **On-the-ground operations** — city-by-city depots, cleaning, charging.
		- **High-utilization network** managing variable demand.
	- Longer-horizon call: **10 years out, every new car sold comes with some kind of FSD software** — rideshare alone is too subscale to support AV software economics, so personally owned vehicles must be the volume market.

- ## Aggregation vs. integration — the structural argument
	- **Supply-led, not demand-led**: Dara's stated biggest lesson. In certain categories supply brings demand — more restaurants raise both eyeballs *and* conversion, which are "multiplicative." He credits Booking.com for getting this right against Expedia, and says he'd run Uber supply-led from the start if he could redo it.
	- **The hotel-stack analogy for AVs**: demand layer (Expedia/Booking/Marriott) → brand → operator/management company → asset owner (REITs) → financier. Mapped to AVs: network layer (Uber, Lyft, Bolt, possibly Waymo direct) → the AV "driver" brand (Waymo) → operator (Uber or Moove.io) → vehicle owners → financiers. Marriott owns almost no hotels; the implication is AV brands need not own fleets either.
	- **Where the value accrues**: "the player who can drive the greatest utilization of these really expensive assets called cars is going to be very, very valuable." Highest utilization → lowest cost of capital → fastest market expansion.
	- **Hybrid network as the load-balancing model**: AVs as the fixed-cost base layer (the "gas" baseload), humans flexing in at peaks. Economic logic: you pay for AV capacity whether or not it runs; you pay humans only for utilization.
	- **Why Uber exited ATG (2020)**: capital scarcity during COVID, plus a structural conflict — potential partners like Waymo "didn't want to partner up with a platform that was also a competitor." Framed as a forced choice between vertical and platform, plus a candid read that hardware "was not our superpower" (naming Tesla and Apple as the rare hardware+software companies).
	- **On Tesla as the integration threat**: Dara argues the same economic laws apply — a Tesla on Uber's network generates more revenue per "box with wheels," improving residual value; if Tesla declines, another OEM takes the slot. Analogy to McDonald's keeping its own app while still using Uber Eats and DoorDash to drive utilization.
	- **The margin-compression risk Thompson pressed on**: today's supply is fragmented (individual drivers); an AV world means negotiating with a handful of "airline"-like counterparties. Dara's rebuttal is the utilization math above, not a denial of the dynamic.

- ## Competitive and capital-cycle observations
	- **Overcapitalization reduces execution discipline**: in the ZIRP era, three months of engineering for a 0.6–0.7% marketplace gain was drowned out when "someone would just raise \$200 million and blow you out of the water." Value creation shifted from raising money to spending it wisely.
	- **Reliability beats price**: over a 16-week experimental window, availability of inventory wins over price every time. Surge is characterized as "a feature of the marketplace, not a bug."
	- **Incentive pool is a marketplace-balance lever, not a P&L line** — shifted between demand and supply by time of day and cycle. Post-COVID the platform was severely supply-constrained for 2–3 years; as of the interview it had moved to balanced/slightly oversupplied, with incentives tilting toward demand generation and lower rider prices.
	- **China/OEM hardware gap**: Chinese manufacturers described as "a leap ahead" of Western OEMs on scalable platforms, with the US "behind" on hardware — a constraint on US AV scaling independent of software progress.
	- **DoorDash contrast**: DoorDash's superpower is focus, Uber's is the platform (multi-service, international). Dara concedes multi-service is harder short-term but claims a stronger long-term position.
