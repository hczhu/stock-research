- **Thesis**: AI music has taken over **supply** but not **listening**. It accounts for **more than half of songs uploaded to streaming services daily**, but **"way lower" than 0.5% of total listening** on Spotify. As long as that gap holds, AI music clutters catalogs and adds moderation costs without diluting the royalty pool. The more investable threat is where Kyncl's own anecdote points: background and functional music, the plays a label earns least from, which is exactly why Warner could afford to license.

	- **Uploads and listening differ by at least 100×.** AI is over half of what gets uploaded and under half a percent of what gets played. Most of it is never heard. The labels' "slop" argument is largely right for the bulk of output, while a small number of AI acts break through: the chart entries and the six-figure monthly earner.

	- The October listening figure is from Spotify's head of policy and is a ceiling, not a measurement ("way lower" than 0.5%). Spotify also has an interest in describing the problem as contained.

- ## The labels have split

	- **Warner licensed.** It settled its lawsuit and signed with Suno in November 2025. Kyncl says Warner and its artists will make **more money per user from Suno than from any other partner**, calls it **"a whole new revenue stream,"** and argues it adds to industry sales rather than taking from other sources. He has taken criticism from peers for it.

	- **Sony and Universal are still suing.** Their argument is not that AI music reduces demand for music. They want **control over where users can distribute** music made with Suno, and they say most AI music is slop listeners don't want.

	- **The Warner deal covers less than it sounds like.** Warner is the **third-largest of the three major labels**. Suno will add Warner songs **"later this year,"** letting users remix acts such as Led Zeppelin or Madonna, but **only if the artists opt in**, and few have publicly endorsed licensing their music to any AI product. The article also mentions a deal with BMG, a large independent company, but that column is cut off in the photograph.

- ## Where AI music substitutes first: background play

	- Kyncl's favorite example: in an Albanian restaurant in New York, Shazam couldn't identify the music. The restaurant was playing songs its **owner had made with Suno**. Warner **"makes almost nothing when a bar or restaurant plays its song,"** and now it was getting a share of Suno's sales.

	- **This is the key point in the article.** The first music AI replaces is background music in shops, restaurants and playlists, where nobody cares who the artist is. **That is the revenue line where labels earn least**, so licensing costs Warner little and earns it a new cut. The exposure sits more with whoever is paid for those plays, and with royalty-free and production-music libraries that sell background tracks.

	- The article says Warner gets "almost nothing" from those plays but doesn't say who does. The usual answer is publishers and performing-rights organizations (the collecting societies that license public performance); I'm inferring that, the article doesn't state it.

- ## Detection and platform policy

	- **Shulman says AI-detection software is unreliable.** Listeners who want to avoid AI music, and who believe it takes money from human artists, have built their own trackers. Two websites, **Soulless Music** and **SlopTracker**, flag AI artists on Spotify and estimate their earnings. SlopTracker's creator: *"Human artists have no chance to compete against the amount of output that can be produced with AI."*

	- **Spotify's response is moderation, not a ban.** It has "rolled out policies that target harmful AI behaviors" and has tools to help listeners spot "authentic artist profiles." Spotify is treating labeling and moderation as an ongoing cost of keeping listeners' trust.

- ## Suno's industry outreach

	- Suno hired **Paul Sinclair**, who spent most of his career at Warner and joined Suno late last year. He leads a team of **almost two dozen** working with the music business, and holds events for musicians and songwriters, many still working day jobs, at a Hollywood venue to demonstrate the product. The headline quote is his.

	- Shulman had hoped Kyncl, coming from Netflix and YouTube, would be more open-minded than the career executives running Universal and Sony. The split described above suggests he was right.
