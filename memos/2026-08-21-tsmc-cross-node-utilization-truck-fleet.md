- ## TSMC's cross-node utilization: how a truck fleet bridges the 2026-to-2028 capacity gap
	- **Source**: Asianometry video transcript on TSMC fab construction and cross-node utilization. Machine-transcribed with garbled Taiwanese place names and personal names (Baoshan/Hsinchu, Nanzih/Kaohsiung, Taichung, Tainan, Chiayi, Miaoli, Mark Liu, Lora Ho, Vera Rubin, FOUPs, CoWoS/InFO all appear mangled — normalized below). The underlying facts are drawn from TSMC conference-call disclosures going back to 2015; the narrator is explicit that *"this is not some ground-breaking piece of news."* Companion to [[TSMC-TSM-thesis]] and [[2026-07-12-tsmc-manufacturing-innovation-platform-cusumano]].
	- **Thesis**: The binding constraint on AI compute is not TSMC's capex but its **lag** — CC Wei's own framing is that **2026 capex does not reach the market until 2028**. The non-obvious offset is that TSMC can add effective leading-edge capacity *without new capex* by treating adjacent nodes as one fungible tool pool, and has now extended this to **splitting a single wafer's manufacturing across fabs 100 miles apart, moved by ordinary trucks**. The investable consequence: **node-labeled wafer-start forecasts systematically understate TSMC's near-term leading-edge supply**, and this specific capital efficiency is **structurally unavailable to TSMC's own overseas fabs**.
- ## The constraint being worked around
	- **2026 capex: over \$60B**, against simultaneous Taiwan builds — **Fab 20** (Baoshan, Hsinchu; 4 phases, 1 complete), **Fab 22** (Nanzih, Kaohsiung; 5 phases planned, 1 complete, 2 shells up with tools moving in), **Fab 25** (Taichung; still bare dirt), plus advanced packaging including **AP7** in Chiayi.
	- **The hard truth from management**: capex spent in 2026 *"will not hit the market until 2028. Maybe a bit earlier."* Consistent with the supply-constraint message in [[2026-06-04-tsmc-agm-cc-wei-ai-demand-2026]].
	- **This is the single most useful takeaway for modelling**: any thesis that treats a capex increase as near-term supply relief is wrong by roughly two years. The bridge across those two years is conversion and cross-utilization, not construction.
- ## Why "process node" is not a unit of capacity
	- **Nodes are iterative, and the names are marketing.** A node is a curated package of technologies sold to customers via design-technology co-optimization — which is why the roadmap fragments into N3/N3E/N3P/N3X/N3C and N2/N2P/N2X/A16 rather than progressing cleanly. Once in the fab, the manufacturing team keeps running experiments, walking yield up to the **mid-80s percent**. When enough improvements accumulate, management may **rebrand the result as a new node** — *"that is basically the story of TSMC's N6 node, which was birthed out of N7"* — or **hold improvements back to reserve for a future node**.
	- **The physical reason adjacent nodes share tools**: chips are built in layers. Transistors at the bottom, then back-end-of-line metal interconnects. In N5, **M1 pitch is ~28–30nm center-to-center**, while the **top metal layers are ~1.5–3 µm wide**. The critical transistor layers differ sharply between nodes; **the upper metal layers are similar or identical across N16, N10, N7, N6, N5, N4, and N3**. This is also why leading-edge fabs still run so many older DUV tools.
	- **Disclosed tool overlap** — TSMC has been telling investors this for a decade:
	  | Year | Speaker | Nodes | Tool overlap |
	  |--------|-------------------|----------------------|-----------------|
	  | 2015 | Mark Liu | 20nm ↔ 16nm | **95%** |
	  | 2018 | CC Wei | N10 / N7 / N7+ | **90%** |
	  | 2018 | Lora Ho (CFO) | N7 → N5 | **>90%** |
	  | 2025 | CC Wei | N7 → N5 | **~90%** |
	  - N7+ is a modified N7 with a single EUV layer — TSMC's first EUV production, a custom node built for Huawei.
	- **The implication to hold onto**: "N3 capacity" is not a fixed physical quantity. It is a scheduling decision over a shared tool base. **Supply models built on node-labeled wafer starts are estimating a marketing category, not an asset.**
- ## The stranding problem this solves
	- TSMC's convention is a new fab per node — **Fab 15 in Taichung for N7**, **Fab 18 in Tainan for N5** — versus Intel's practice of upgrading one shell over time. The structural risk is that when a volume customer steps up a node, *"this leaves the TSMC manager in charge of that fab with an Apple-sized hole in their production volumes."*
	- **The economics are unforgiving**: a fab's largest cost is depreciation, and it is real — the narrator invokes **Munger's "EBITDA is BS earnings"** because tools break and need maintenance regardless. Full depreciation takes **~5–7 years**. Below roughly **60–70% utilization a fab loses money**, and if the market never returns, the fab becomes a stranded investment.
	- **This has already happened once, recently**: in **early 2023**, post-COVID mobile weakness pushed the **N7 fabs in Taichung under 70% utilization**.
	- **N7/N6 is the node in no man's land** — nearly ten years old and too far behind for leading-edge customers, but too expensive and complex for trailing-edge customers who prefer a mature planar node like 28nm. Current positioning by node: **N2** too new and immature (and gate-all-around); **N3** the sweet spot and what NVIDIA's **Vera Rubin** platform runs on; **N4** well established and where AI-accelerator startups are likely to begin.
- ## What is actually new: splitting one wafer across two fabs
	- **The 2024 baseline**: TSMC disclosed it was converting **N5 capacity to N3**, accepting a **gross-margin hit** to do so. Asked on the **Q1 2024** call whether N7 could be converted to N3 the same way, Wei said that would be **far harder** — less overlap, and critically **N5 and N3 sit in the same building** (Tainan's Fab 18, whose phases are joined by a wafer bridge), whereas N7 is ~100 miles away in Taichung.
	- **By early 2025 the answer changed.** Wei said TSMC now had to convert N5 to N3 for future AI products while N5 itself remained in high demand — making the timing tricky — and that they had **figured out how to use N7 capacity to support N5**.
	- **The mechanism is logistics, not lithography**: Tainan fabs perform **only the critical layers** of an N3 or N5 wafer. The unfinished wafers are then loaded into trucks, driven **~100 miles to underutilized N7/N6 fabs in Taichung**, and the remaining (non-critical, high-metal-layer) steps are completed there. Tainan spends **100% of its capacity on critical work**.
	- **This rides on infrastructure that already existed.** TSMC has long trucked FOUPs of finished wafers from front-end fabs in Hsinchu and Tainan to advanced packaging fabs in Miaoli and Chiayi for CoWoS and InFO, plus a small third-party wafer shuttle service for things like failure analysis. Ordinary trucks, carrying **~\$20,000 wafers** — *"tens of millions of dollars of chip inventory"* per vehicle. TSMC's own ESG site documented driver working conditions in 2020.
	- **Why this is more than a curiosity**: it converts a stranded-asset problem into a capacity asset. **N7/N6 fabs facing obsolescence become back-end-of-line finishing capacity for the leading edge**, which extends their useful economic life and changes the depreciation/stranding calculus on a large installed base. It also means leading-edge output can rise faster than leading-edge *tool* installations.
- ## The part with the sharpest read-through: this only works in Taiwan
	- The narrator's claim is that cross-node utilization *"is something no other company is capable of. And it can only be done in Taiwan."* The reason is mundane and therefore credible: it requires **many fabs at many nodes, densely clustered, under one operator, connected by short-haul road logistics**.
	- **The consequence nobody in the transcript draws out**: every fab TSMC builds in Arizona, Japan, or Germany is a **geographically isolated island** with one or two nodes and no adjacent underutilized capacity to borrow from or lend to. Those fabs therefore cannot access this capital-efficiency mechanism at all. **Overseas expansion structurally dilutes the very advantage described here** — a cost that is separate from, and additive to, the widely discussed labor and construction cost gap.
	- It also sharpens the concentration risk in a specific way. The usual framing is that Taiwan concentration is a geopolitical liability; this shows it is simultaneously an **operational asset that cannot be relocated without being destroyed**. Both are true, and they trade off directly.
- ## Predictions and markers to watch
	- **The narrator's forecast**: the trucking arrangement is a bridge *"while TSMC is converting its N5 capacity to N3."* If N3 demand stays robust, he expects TSMC to **convert N7/N6 capacity outright** as the natural next step.
	- **What to listen for on calls**: (1) further conversion disclosures and the associated **gross-margin drag**, which is the explicit price of this bridge; (2) N7/N6 utilization commentary — rising utilization there may now reflect *leading-edge* finishing work rather than genuine trailing-edge demand recovery, which would make it a misleading demand signal; (3) any tool-overlap figure quoted for **N3 → N2**, since GAA breaks the pattern at the transistor layers and is the test of whether this mechanism survives into the N2 generation.
	- **The honest limit of the thesis**: overlap is concentrated in the **upper metal layers**, so cross-utilization relieves pressure on non-critical steps only. It does not create EUV capacity, it does not help advanced packaging (the actual bottleneck for AI accelerators), and it cannot substitute for the 2028 arrival of the fabs being built now. It buys throughput at the margin — which, in a shortage, is exactly where the marginal wafer is worth the most.
