- ## Stance comes first, fake news follows
	- **Source**: User's own observation.
	- **Thesis**: People don't hold irrational stances because they were fooled by fake news. Usually **the causation runs the other way**: they already hold the stance, then believe the fake news that fits it. The false story is a symptom of the stance, not its cause.
- ## Why this matters
	- **Correcting the facts rarely changes minds.** If the stance came first, debunking one story takes away a justification, not the belief. The person picks up the next story that fits. This matches research on motivated reasoning and identity-protective cognition, where people judge evidence by whether it supports a conclusion they already prefer.
	- **What to ask instead**: not "what did they read?" but "what stance were they already defending, and why?" Incentives, identity, group membership and past public commitments usually explain more than the information itself.
- ## Investing read-across
	- **Market narratives work the same way.** Holders of a position adopt bullish stories readily, and bears adopt bearish ones. Many rumors that move a stock are stories people wanted to believe, so how widely a story spreads says more about positioning than about the facts.
	- **Self-check**: when a new data point feels very convincing, ask whether it would feel as convincing if it pointed the other way. If the answer is no, the stance is doing the evaluating. The memo habit of separating "source claims" from "fact check" columns is a guard against this.
	- **Changing your mind**: a stance built before the evidence won't be overturned by evidence alone. Write down in advance what would falsify a thesis, before positioning and identity attach to it.
