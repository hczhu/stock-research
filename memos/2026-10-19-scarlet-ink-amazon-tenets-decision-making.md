- ## How Amazon Uses Tenets to Decentralize and Lock In Decisions
	- **Source**: David Anderson (ex-Amazon Tech Director and GM), *Scarlet Ink* newsletter, "How Amazon Uses Tenets to Make Better Decisions at Every Level," October 19, 2026 (free-tier excerpt provided by user).
	- **Thesis**: Amazon's `Tenets` section — embedded across project proposals, product designs, and annual operating plans — is an organizational scaling mechanism that converts senior-executive trade-off judgments (`X > Y`) into reusable rules, pushing routine decisions to lower-cost frontline employees and preventing expensive re-litigation of settled debates.

- ## Where Tenets Sit in Amazon's Planning Stack
	- | Document format | Primary use case | Role of the `Tenets` section |
	  | --- | --- | --- |
	  | **1-pagers and 2-pagers** | Project proposals | Defines the decision skeleton and priority ordering for the project |
	  | **PR/FAQ documents** | Outward-facing view of a new product | Aligns product trade-offs before engineering investment begins |
	  | **6-pagers** | Full OP1 (annual operating plan) proposals and funding requests | Encodes department- or business-level resource and operating priorities |
	- A tenet is only useful if it adjudicates a real trade-off. Consensus truisms that 100% of employees already agree with (e.g., "Amazon should make money") do no decision-making work and are treated as wasted space.

- ## Two Mechanisms That Reduce Decision Cost and Latency
	- **Pushing decisions to the lowest, cheapest level**:
		- Senior leaders have the deepest context on business fundamentals, but routing routine choices through them creates an organizational bottleneck.
		- Because leadership cannot pre-specify answers to every operational question, they encode the *inputs and priority weights* into tenets so junior engineers and frontline operators can resolve low-impact decisions autonomously without peer consensus or manager sign-off.
	- **Amortizing expensive escalations ("Avoid redeciding")**:
		- When peers and their managers deadlock on a high-stakes choice (such as a critical pricing decision), they escalate up the chain to a co-SVP. At Amazon, necessary escalation is treated as "working as designed" to force fast resolution rather than as a management failure.
		- When the SVP resolves the dispute ("We care more about X than Y, and therefore we should do Z"), the team records the underlying preference ordering — *"We care more about X than Y"* — as a formal tenet rather than merely logging the one-off outcome `Z`.
		- Encoding the trade-off rule prevents new hires or existing teams from re-opening the same debate six months later when underlying business conditions have not changed, reducing the risk of granting frontline autonomy at scale.
