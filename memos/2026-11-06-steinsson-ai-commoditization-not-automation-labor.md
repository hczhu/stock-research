- ## Steinsson: AI may commoditize jobs rather than automate them away, giving plentiful jobs at lower wages
	- **Source**: Jón Steinsson (UC Berkeley), X thread, Nov 6, 2026, plus the underlying paper: Masao Fukui, Emi Nakamura and Jón Steinsson, ["The Commoditization of Labor"](https://jonsteinsson.com/papers/Commoditization.pdf) (Sept 19, 2026). The paper models past standardization, not AI directly; applying it to AI is Steinsson's extrapolation in the thread. Related: [[2026-04-09-ai-labor-substitution-elasticity-demand-argument]], [[2026-06-01-labor-market-impacts-of-china-shock-vs-ai-shock]], [[2026-08-03-sf-controller-q2-economy-ai-boom-without-jobs]], [[2026-07-27-vibe-coding-demand-destruction-vs-saas-operating-cost]].
	- **Thesis**: The usual frame for AI and jobs is **automation**: machines replace workers and jobs disappear. Steinsson proposes **commoditization** instead. AI makes skilled tasks easier ("vibe coding vs. real coding") without removing the worker, so many more people can do the job and any one worker matters less. The predicted outcome is **plentiful jobs at lower wages**, not mass unemployment. If the effect is concentrated at superstar firms, the **labor share falls** and profits rise.
- ## Two models compared
	- | | Automation (Acemoglu–Restrepo) | Commoditization (Fukui–Nakamura–Steinsson) |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | Mechanism | Machines take over tasks | Tasks are standardized: workers still do every task, but output no longer depends on worker skill |
	  | Jobs | Destroyed | Preserved; easier to find, because firms become "less picky" |
	  | Wages | Fall through job loss | Fall because workers become interchangeable, so firms can pay further below productivity |
	  | Labor market needed in the model | Competitive is fine | Must be imperfectly competitive (search frictions, firm wage-setting power); the effect doesn't exist in competitive models |
	  | Labor share | Falls as capital replaces labor | Unchanged if all firms standardize equally; **falls** if standardization is concentrated at high-productivity "megafirms" |
	  | Historical examples | Industrial robots, ATMs | Adam Smith's pin factory → McDonald's → warehouse handhelds, scripted customer service → white-collar work with AI (the thread's extrapolation) |
	- **Steinsson's own verdict**: "AI will likely lead to some of both. But it might be more commoditization."
- ## What the paper shows
	- **How it works**: each job is a set of tasks. A standardized task's output depends on the firm's technology, not the worker's know-how. As standardization rises, firms lower the minimum worker quality they will accept, and **vacancies get easier to fill**. That improves the firm's position when setting wages, because any worker is replaceable.
	- **Why superstar firms matter**: if all firms standardize equally, firms find workers more easily but workers also find firms more easily, and the paper proves the two effects cancel, leaving the labor share unchanged. With heterogeneous firms, the high-productivity, low-labor-share firms gain most, workers move toward them, and the **aggregate labor share falls**.
	- **Calibration**: matching the **4.3 percentage-point rise in the employment share of megafirms (10,000+ employees) from 1980 to 2023**, more standardization at megafirms lowers their relative wages by **about 8%** while raising output per worker and firm value.
	- **What it explains**: the gap between productivity and wages in services; wage markdowns (how far pay sits below a worker's output) rising even as local employer concentration fell, which standard monopsony models get backwards; and the **collapse of the large-firm wage premium**, which has completely disappeared in services and among workers without college degrees. The authors also present new evidence that **hiring costs are much lower at megafirms**, especially in low-wage services.
- ## Applying it to AI and white-collar work
	- **The coding example**: if vibe coding lets non-specialists produce working software, the number of people who can do the job grows and the premium for scarce engineering skill shrinks. That would mean more people employed in software roles at lower pay per role, not mass unemployment of engineers.
	- **What shows up in the data**: commoditization predicts **easy hiring, flat or falling real wages in affected occupations, and falling hiring costs**. Automation predicts **rising unemployment and job losses** in those occupations. Comparing unemployment and wage data by occupation over the next few years can tell the two apart. The SF Controller's "AI boom without jobs" pattern ([[2026-08-03-sf-controller-q2-economy-ai-boom-without-jobs]]) is ambiguous between them so far.
	- **Where the profits go**: under commoditization the AI productivity gain mostly goes to firms through wider wage markdowns, not to workers. Companies with the best standardized AI workflows (large platforms and service firms) gain margin twice: from higher output per worker and from lower pay per worker. That supports the margin-expansion case for AI adopters even without headcount cuts.
	- **What it means for macro and policy**: a no-job-apocalypse, lower-wage outcome is gentler for consumer demand than mass unemployment ([[2026-04-09-ai-labor-substitution-elasticity-demand-argument]]), but it widens inequality between profits and wages. That can create political pressure (minimum wages, sectoral bargaining, AI taxes) aimed at the firms capturing the gains.
	- **Limits**: the paper's evidence comes from past standardization in low-wage services, and extending it to AI-assisted white-collar work is a hypothesis. Commoditization also assumes the job stays whole. If AI handles enough of the standardized tasks, commoditization turns into automation, and that boundary may move quickly for coding and customer service.
