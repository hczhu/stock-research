- ## Steinsson: AI may commoditize jobs rather than automate them away, giving plentiful jobs at lower wages
	- **Source**: Jón Steinsson (UC Berkeley), X thread, Nov 6, 2026, plus the underlying paper: Masao Fukui, Emi Nakamura and Jón Steinsson, ["The Commoditization of Labor"](https://jonsteinsson.com/papers/Commoditization.pdf) (Sept 19, 2026). The paper models past standardization, not AI directly; applying it to AI is Steinsson's extrapolation in the thread. The sections on the paper's arguments and historical examples come from the full 36-page PDF (user-provided). Related: [[2026-04-09-ai-labor-substitution-elasticity-demand-argument]], [[2026-06-01-labor-market-impacts-of-china-shock-vs-ai-shock]], [[2026-08-03-sf-controller-q2-economy-ai-boom-without-jobs]], [[2026-07-27-vibe-coding-demand-destruction-vs-saas-operating-cost]].
	- **Thesis**: The usual frame for AI and jobs is **automation**: machines replace workers and jobs disappear. Steinsson proposes **commoditization** instead. AI makes skilled tasks easier ("vibe coding vs. real coding") without removing the worker, so many more people can do the job and any one worker matters less. The predicted outcome is **plentiful jobs at lower wages**, not mass unemployment. If the effect is concentrated at superstar firms, the **labor share falls** and profits rise.
- ## Two models compared
	- | | Automation (Acemoglu–Restrepo) | Commoditization (Fukui–Nakamura–Steinsson) |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | Mechanism | Machines take over tasks | Tasks are standardized: workers still do every task, but output no longer depends on worker skill |
	  | Jobs | Destroyed | Preserved; easier to find, because firms become "less picky" |
	  | Wages | Fall through job loss | Fall because workers become interchangeable, so firms can pay further below productivity |
	  | Labor market needed in the model | Competitive is fine | Must be imperfectly competitive (search frictions, firm wage-setting power); the effect doesn't exist in competitive models |
	  | Labor share | Falls as capital replaces labor | Unchanged if all firms standardize equally; **falls** if standardization is concentrated at high-productivity "megafirms" |
	  | Historical examples | Industrial robots, ATMs | Adam Smith's pin factory → McDonald's → warehouse handhelds, scripted customer service → white-collar work with AI (the thread's extrapolation) |
	- **Steinsson's own verdict**: "AI will likely lead to some of both. But it might be more commoditization."
- ## What the paper shows
	- **How it works**: each job is a set of tasks. A standardized task's output depends on the firm's technology, not the worker's know-how. As standardization rises, firms lower the minimum worker quality they will accept, and **vacancies get easier to fill**. That improves the firm's position when setting wages, because any worker is replaceable.
	- **Why superstar firms matter**: if all firms standardize equally, firms find workers more easily but workers also find firms more easily, and the paper proves the two effects cancel, leaving the labor share unchanged. With heterogeneous firms, the high-productivity, low-labor-share firms gain most, workers move toward them, and the **aggregate labor share falls**.
	- **Calibration**: matching the **4.3 percentage-point rise in the employment share of megafirms (10,000+ employees) from 1980 to 2023**, more standardization at megafirms lowers their relative wages by **about 8%** while raising output per worker and firm value.
	- **What it explains**: the gap between productivity and wages in services; wage markdowns (how far pay sits below a worker's output) rising even as local employer concentration fell, which standard monopsony models get backwards; and the **collapse of the large-firm wage premium**, which has completely disappeared in services and among workers without college degrees. The authors also present new evidence that **hiring costs are much lower at megafirms**, especially in low-wage services.
- ## The paper's key arguments in more detail
	- **Smith versus Marx**: the paper starts from an old split. Smith's pin factory (18 operations, productivity up "a thousand fold") stresses the gain from simplifying tasks. Marx stresses the cost: "His labour becomes a labour that anyone can perform. Hence, competitors crowd upon him on all sides." Braverman's work on "de-skilling" under Taylor's scientific management continues Marx's line. The paper models both effects at once: simplification raises output per worker **and** lowers workers' bargaining power.
	- **The firm's outside option is the new piece**: the recent monopsony literature explains low wages through the *worker's* outside option (how many differentiated employers they can go to). This paper focuses on the *firm's* outside option, meaning how easily it can replace a worker. Wage markdowns follow a standard Lerner formula, and anything that makes replacement easier widens them.
	- **Durable jobs are essential**: the result needs jobs that outlive any one worker, such as a machine to operate or an outlet to staff, that cost money to create. Only then is a vacant job valuable and a worker "disposable." In standard search models, where a job disappears when the worker leaves and new jobs can be created freely, the effect doesn't exist. Evidence that most hiring is replacement hiring supports the assumption.
	- **McDonald's versus Burger King**: when every firm standardizes equally, "if McDonald's threatens to replace its workers, they can respond that it's easy to get a job at Burger King." The two threats cancel, which gives the neutrality result. Wages fall in aggregate only when standardization is uneven, with the best firms standardizing most.
	- **Commoditization can explain how megafirms grow without raising wages**: in standard monopsony models a firm must pay more to attract more workers, so large firms pay more. Here a firm can standardize instead, become less picky, and grow without raising wages. That explains why the large-firm wage premium collapsed even as megafirms grew. It also explains Dube, Freeman and Reich's puzzle that large employers don't pay more to recruit.
	- **Rising markdowns despite falling local concentration**: megafirms like Walmart entering local markets made local labor markets *less* concentrated. Conventional monopsony models therefore predict falling markdowns, and Berger, Herkenhoff and Mongey's model implies a 4-point *rise* in labor share. Commoditization links rising national concentration to rising markdowns through firm technology instead.
	- **Calibration detail**: standardization becomes feasible after 1980 (e.g. through ICT). Megafirms raise their standardized share of tasks from **0.5 to ~0.63**, while smaller firms barely change. Megafirms' output per worker rises **more than 25%**, relative wages fall **~8%**, and hiring costs fall **nearly 40%**. The model's megafirm wage premium falls from over 50% to ~30%; in the data (Bloom et al.) it fell from **almost 60% in the 1980s to ~20% in the 2010s**. If too many firms standardize, concentration rises more than in the data, which is why only megafirms standardize in the calibration.
	- **Not commodification**: the authors use "commoditize" (make indistinguishable) deliberately, not "commodify" (turn into something bought and sold).
- ## Where commoditization already happened: the paper's examples
	- | Example | What was standardized | Evidence in the paper |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | **Pin factory** (Smith, 1776) | Pin-making split into 18 simple operations | Productivity up "a thousand fold"; the original case of the division of labor |
	  | **Fast food (McDonald's)** | Nearly every step of meal preparation broken into simple tasks needing minimal skill | Steinsson worked at McDonald's in the 1990s: the only step needing judgment was salting the burger, because no machine yet dispensed a standard amount of salt. Accommodation and food services have the **lowest cost per hire, \$105** |
	  | **Warehouse picking** | Workers once had to remember where products were stored; now they follow real-time directions from handheld devices. Pick-by-voice systems in retail logistics | "Before… you had to be an expert in your trade, now you just have to know how to use the tool." Training time fell **from two to three weeks to two to three days** (Gautié et al., 2020) |
	  | **Customer service and call centers** | Workers once had to understand the products; now they read scripted answers from computer systems | Guendelsberger (*On the Clock*, 2019): as skill is stripped out, "the cost of turnover falls," until training a churning stream of new workers is cheaper than paying people to stay |
	  | **Hotels and low-wage services** | Jobs simple enough that any applicant can fill them | Ehrenreich (*Nickel and Dimed*, 2001): big hotels run job ads "almost continually" to keep a supply of replacements. Lazear and McCue document very high churn in these jobs |
	  | **Retail and megafirms (Walmart, Amazon)** | ICT for inventory, logistics and replicating stores across many locations (the "industrial revolution in services") | Strong productivity growth with weak wage growth in retail (Hortaçsu and Syverson). Retail cost per hire **\$250**. The "falling bottom" of wages in retail and fast food drove a large share of earnings-inequality growth from 1996 to 2018 |
	- **Hiring-cost evidence (new in the paper)**: in SHRM's 2022 and 2025 HR benchmarking surveys, median cost per hire falls steadily with firm size. Firms with 5,000+ employees spend only **about a third** as much per hire as firms with 1–99 employees. A second method (HR costs from OES divided by hires from JOLTS) finds the lowest costs in food services (\$105), leisure and hospitality (\$132), construction (\$218) and retail (\$250). Low-wage services have hiring costs several times lower than manufacturing. SHRM is a convenience sample, which the authors acknowledge.
	- **What the examples share**: in each case technology moved know-how from the worker into the firm's system (the procedure, the handheld, the script, the store format). Workers still do every task, but any worker can. That is why these industries combine **growing employment with stagnant wages**, unlike manufacturing under routine-biased automation, where employment fell (Autor and Dorn).
	- **Stock read**: the companies that did the standardizing (Walmart, Amazon, McDonald's and other service megafirms) captured the gains. The model shows megafirm value rising sharply on higher output per worker, lower wages and more employment, consistent with the low labor share of superstar firms and the rising value of intangibles. The investable question for AI is which firms build the equivalent of the handheld or the script for office work, and which are on the other side of it.
- ## Applying it to AI and white-collar work
	- **The coding example**: if vibe coding lets non-specialists produce working software, the number of people who can do the job grows and the premium for scarce engineering skill shrinks. That would mean more people employed in software roles at lower pay per role, not mass unemployment of engineers.
	- **What shows up in the data**: commoditization predicts **easy hiring, flat or falling real wages in affected occupations, and falling hiring costs**. Automation predicts **rising unemployment and job losses** in those occupations. Comparing unemployment and wage data by occupation over the next few years can tell the two apart. The SF Controller's "AI boom without jobs" pattern ([[2026-08-03-sf-controller-q2-economy-ai-boom-without-jobs]]) is ambiguous between them so far.
	- **Where the profits go**: under commoditization the AI productivity gain mostly goes to firms through wider wage markdowns, not to workers. Companies with the best standardized AI workflows (large platforms and service firms) gain margin twice: from higher output per worker and from lower pay per worker. That supports the margin-expansion case for AI adopters even without headcount cuts.
	- **What it means for macro and policy**: a no-job-apocalypse, lower-wage outcome is gentler for consumer demand than mass unemployment ([[2026-04-09-ai-labor-substitution-elasticity-demand-argument]]), but it widens inequality between profits and wages. That can create political pressure (minimum wages, sectoral bargaining, AI taxes) aimed at the firms capturing the gains.
	- **Limits**: the paper's evidence comes from past standardization in low-wage services, and extending it to AI-assisted white-collar work is a hypothesis. Commoditization also assumes the job stays whole. If AI handles enough of the standardized tasks, commoditization turns into automation, and that boundary may move quickly for coding and customer service.
