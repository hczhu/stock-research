- ## Investment Philosophy: Private Equity Perspective on Public Markets

	- Oakmark describes itself as a long-term value investor, but the defining framing is bringing a **private equity perspective** to public equities. That means looking much further into the future than typical public market participants — specifically about **seven years out** — estimating what investors are likely to pay for the business at that point, discounting that terminal value back to the present, and then looking for a large gap between that estimate and the current stock price.

	- The seven-year horizon is not arbitrary. It is long enough to be genuinely differentiated — most investors are looking out one to three years, if that — but not so long that the projections become coin flips. After seven years, Oakmark assumes the business earns a normal rate of return and trades at a market multiple; it does not project "super normal" growth beyond that window. This is itself a form of margin of safety: you do not have to be right about what the business looks like in 2040 to make money. A historical reminder of why this matters is that landline telephone companies and newspapers were once considered so durable that analysts felt comfortable projecting 20 years out with confidence. Entire industries failed.

	- The practical target is to identify companies where **doubling the investment over a four-to-five year period is a likely outcome**. That maps roughly to buying at 60 cents on the dollar relative to estimated business value — a **40% discount**. The 40% is not a precise cutoff; it exists because it provides enough buffer that if Oakmark is off by a standard deviation or more on the value estimate, the position still doesn't create downside. False precision is avoided deliberately. A business selling at $60 where a private buyer would likely pay somewhere between $90 and $110 is good enough — the goal is the right ballpark, not the right decimal place.

- ## Oakmark Accounting vs. GAAP

	- One of the most important distinctions Oakmark makes is rejecting the idea that value investing means owning the lowest-PE or lowest-price-to-book names. Those screens are based on GAAP, and GAAP accounting increasingly diverges from economic reality for companies that make growth investments via the income statement.

	- Forty years ago, if a steel company wanted to grow, it built a new plant. That capital expenditure went onto the balance sheet and was depreciated over the plant's useful life — the full investment value was visible. Today, advertising spend, R&D, and customer acquisition costs are expensed immediately in the year they are incurred, even though their economic benefits may persist for a decade. A company reinvesting aggressively in growth through these channels will show depressed reported earnings — it will look expensive on a P/E basis even if it is genuinely cheap when those investments are treated as the long-duration assets they actually are. Oakmark calls the adjustment "Oakmark accounting": **treat intangible growth investments as having long-term economic value to the company**. This is what allows them to end up in names that would never show up in a traditional value screen.

	- The Netflix case is the clearest illustration. At the time of original investment, Netflix was nearly breakeven on GAAP earnings. Gap accounting was recognizing massive customer acquisition costs immediately while the value of those subscribers — ~$800 per sub at the time, comparable to HBO sub values — was not on the income statement at all. Oakmark reframed the question: Netflix was adding ~25 million subscribers per year at ~$800 each, implying $20 billion of annual value creation. On that basis, the stock was trading at roughly **6x the incremental value being created** — not an expensive stock at all. The same logic applied on the revenue side: Netflix was charging $10/month while consumer surveys showed it was more valued than HBO, Spotify, or XM at $20/month. The pricing power was visible; the GAAP earnings were not.

- ## Valuation: Discount Rates, Precision, and Rank Ordering

	- Oakmark builds discount rates from the ground up: start with long-term Treasury yields, add the implied premium for moderate-quality corporate credit embedded in the market, add the additional premium that equities should carry above bonds, then apply a company-specific risk score on a 1-to-5 scale (1 = lower discount rate than the S&P; 5 = significantly higher). Buying at 40% discount to that DCF result is economically equivalent to applying a higher discount rate across the cash flow stream — and the gap is deliberately large enough to survive estimation error.

	- On the question of whether fair value is a precise number: it is not, and pretending otherwise is a mistake. Managers who publish sell targets with two decimal places are confusing precision with accuracy. What the exercise produces is a range, and within that range the most important output is not the exact number but the **rank ordering** across the opportunity set. Applying consistent assumptions across 200 businesses and identifying the most undervalued ones — whether by 20%, 40%, or 60% — is worth far more than false precision on any single name. The ranking is the deliverable.

- ## Mid-Cycle Earnings and Cyclicality

	- For any business, the earnings figure fed into a DCF should reflect **mid-cycle earnings**, not the current peak or current trough. The goal is to weight both the good years (how far above normal they go) and the bad years (how far below normal they fall) proportionally, so the discounted value represents what the business generates on average across a full cycle.

	- This is why Oakmark rarely ends up in semiconductors or biotech — in those industries, the earnings that really matter are either very far out in the future (biotech) or extremely hard to normalize across the cycle (semis). It is not that those sectors are excluded by rule; it is that they do not show up on the approved list because the analyst cannot demonstrate high-enough confidence in mid-cycle earnings. Similarly, electric utilities are rarely in the portfolio because their managers tend not to think entrepreneurially, which undermines the management-alignment criterion.

	- On banks specifically: Oakmark has a strong overweight to banks that comes from a structural argument. Post-GFC, investors reflexively stress-test banks against the GFC scenario, which was a generational event, not a normal recession. A normal recession — two consecutive quarters of negative GDP, a brief headline earnings hit, no capital event — would show banks to be quite resilient. Banks today carry roughly **twice the capital per dollar of assets** as they did pre-GFC, with tighter lending standards. The economies of scale now favor large banks more than at any point in the past 30 years (compliance, fraud prevention, and regulatory burden scale sub-linearly with assets). The multiple re-rating probably requires getting through one or two normal recessions and showing the world what bank earnings resilience looks like in a typical downturn.

- ## The Three Investment Criteria

	- Every investment at Oakmark must satisfy three conditions simultaneously.

	- **First**, the stock must be selling at a meaningful discount — approximately 40% — to the estimated business value. This is the quantitative hurdle and the primary screen.

	- **Second**, management must be aligned with shareholders. Alignment means: compensation structured around per-share metrics (EPS growth, business value per share, ROIC — not just revenue or total earnings); a history of rational capital allocation (the next dollar invested goes to the highest-return opportunity, whether that is organic growth, acquisitions, or share repurchases); and a demonstrated willingness to shut down businesses that are not working. Denominators matter: management that can hit growth targets by making dilutive acquisitions is not aligned. Oakmark looks for the CEO who, looking back at the end of a 10-year tenure, cares most about the same things a long-term shareholder cares about.

	- **Third**, the value growth rate of the business (plus any dividends) must be expected to at least match the market. This is the floor — Oakmark is not looking for mediocre businesses at a discount, but for good-or-better businesses at a large discount.

- ## Management Evaluation in Practice

	- The management-meeting process at Oakmark is designed to get at things that a conference call cannot reveal. The standard sell-side analyst meeting is essentially a play button: IR teams have prepped the CEO so thoroughly that the meeting is a polished repetition of the last conference presentation. That kind of meeting has limited information content. Oakmark's meetings ask different questions: What do you hope this business looks like in five to ten years? If you look back at the end of your tenure as CEO, what are the first things you will check to decide whether it was successful? Those questions get CEOs off script, produce more candid answers, and reveal whether the CEO's success metrics are the same as a long-term shareholder's.

	- Preparation for management meetings is extensive: full resume review, background checks through mutual contacts from business school or prior board seats, printed books for everyone in the meeting with biographical detail on everyone present. The goal is not to refine the DCF but to answer one question: is this someone I want to be invested with?

	- Highly levered businesses require especially high trust in management. With an unlevered business, you can analyze the financial statements and get reasonable comfort on the range of outcomes. With a bank at 9% tangible capital to assets, if you do not trust the representations being made about the asset quality, no amount of financial statement analysis gets you to confidence. Leverage amplifies the importance of the management-alignment criterion.

- ## ROIC: Important but Not Sufficient

	- Return on invested capital is a crucial quality metric at Oakmark — high ROIC is a sign of a good moat, and good moat businesses deserve higher multiples and lower discount rates. Charlie Munger's maxim is correct in principle: hold a business with high enough ROIC for long enough and your investment return converges to the ROIC. But **the convergence can take 30 to 40 years**, and the entry price matters. Buying Costco at 50x earnings and waiting for your return to converge to Costco's ROIC requires a very long holding period and assumes competition never erodes that ROIC. Oakmark trims this risk in two ways: the seven-year horizon caps how much "above-normal" ROIC needs to persist, and the 40% discount provides a valuation cushion.

	- A broader concern: capitalism is supposed to work by attracting capital to high-return businesses until competition erodes those returns back toward average. The past decade of growth-stock outperformance has been almost entirely justified by actual earnings growth — the Magnificent Seven in aggregate earned roughly twice what any reasonable analyst would have projected for 2026 back in 2019. That is not a valuation story; that is a fundamentals story. But a lot of investors have drawn the wrong lesson: that you can simply buy great businesses at any price and the compounding will take care of itself. Oakmark's view is that this misreads the evidence. The winners did generate the earnings; the companies that failed to maintain competitive position — and there were many — got less attention because they did not become famous examples. Looking at the full sample, reversion to mean in ROIC is real, just slower in a winner-take-most digital economy.

- ## Sell Discipline: Updating Business Value, Not Anchoring

	- The sell decision is where many value investors fail. The most common failure modes are (a) selling too early because you hit an initial target without updating it for new information, or (b) holding too long because you have emotionally anointed the stock as a forever holding.

	- Oakmark's sell targets are set against business value estimates, and those estimates are **updated aggressively with every new piece of information**. The Apple case: Oakmark bought Apple believing it was selling at 60 cents on the dollar. Over roughly a decade, they made approximately 20x on the investment. They never held Apple above their sell target during that period — because every quarter, as growth materially exceeded expectations, the analyst raised the value estimate, and the stock kept catching up. The company's perceived risk (over-earning, ecosystem lock-in uncertain) kept the stock below a market multiple for most of the holding period. When the stock eventually reached the revised fair value — and the remaining upside was primarily from multiple expansion rather than earnings growth — they sold.

	- The corollary is that when a stock price drops sharply, the knee-jerk reaction of "the stock overreacted, my value estimate is only down 10%'' is dangerous. Oakmark's own data shows an anchoring bias in analyst estimates: once an analyst starts lowering the value estimate, there tends to be momentum to that downward revision. The right question when a stock drops is not "did the market overreact?" but **"if I didn't own this stock today, would I buy it at this price?"** If the answer is not clearly yes, it does not belong in the portfolio.

	- This anchoring bias runs in both directions. When a stock rises, there is a tendency to raise the estimate more slowly than the evidence supports, leading to premature selling. Oakmark explicitly trains analysts to look for conservatism in upside estimates before triggering a sale. The goal is accuracy in both directions, not conservatism as a virtue.

- ## Research Process and Portfolio Construction

	- Oakmark analysts are generalists — each analyst covers names across sectors rather than being siloed into a single industry. An idea matures over months of work before it is ever presented: all publicly available material from the company, competitor and customer commentary, expert network calls, and at least one in-person management meeting. The analyst writes a three-to-four page report explaining why the name meets Oakmark's three criteria. That report is distributed Monday afternoon; the entire investment team spends Monday evening looking for holes. Tuesday morning, the analyst presents and the team debates actively — the explicit goal is to find everything wrong before a dollar of client capital is committed.

	- Three senior investment partners vote on whether a name goes on the approved list. If they vote no, no portfolio manager can buy it — including the CIO. Once on the list, each portfolio manager independently decides whether and how much to own, requiring a two-of-three vote among the portfolio managers. This separation prevents any single person, including senior management, from forcing an idea into the portfolio or keeping it out.

	- The Oakmark Fund targets **50 to 60 names**, with an average position around 2%. New positions enter at ~1% and build to ~3% as conviction develops; market appreciation can take a name higher, at which point automatic trimming begins. The Oakmark Select Fund is more concentrated and has outperformed on absolute return but with higher standard deviation — a better fit for investors building a diversified portfolio of portfolios. Primary risk management is **drawdown risk**, not tracking error. Oakmark is explicitly not trying to look like the S&P or the Russell Value index — clients hire them to look different when the risk-return case justifies it.

- ## Case Studies

	- ### Meta / Facebook
		- Oakmark owned Meta during an early phase when you could value Instagram and Blue Facebook separately, add back net cash, and arrive at a number significantly above the stock price without ascribing any value to WhatsApp or metaverse investments. That gave downside protection: the case did not require precision on the speculative assets. They held into the four hundreds. As the stock continued to rise, justifying further ownership required believing the hyperscaler capex cycle would generate a premium multiple on top of Instagram and Facebook values — and that the metaverse or AI investments would eventually pay off. Oakmark could not get there with confidence and sold. A key decision point during the stock's collapse to ~$100: the new CFO at the time could not articulate an economic framework for why the capital spending was justified — the answer was "this is what we do" rather than "here is the ROI logic." That gap in management communication kept Oakmark from making the position much larger at the trough.

	- ### Netflix
		- Netflix was introduced to Oakmark by an analyst who surveyed colleagues on which subscription service — Netflix ($10/month), HBO, Spotify, XM — was most valuable to them. Netflix won overwhelmingly, yet it was half the price of competitors. The conclusion: Netflix had massive untapped pricing power and was choosing to use low price as a weapon to achieve scale. Under Oakmark accounting, Netflix was adding ~$20 billion of value annually at $800/subscriber, trading at 6x that value creation. The second Netflix thesis: after the subscriber growth hiccup, Oakmark traveled to California to meet the CFO directly. The meeting established that management was applying economic thinking to the path to recovery — password-sharing monetization, ad-supported tier pricing. They fully restored the position. That bet generated more alpha than the Meta position that Oakmark did not add to at the trough.

	- ### Salesforce ($CRM)
		- Current thesis rests on three legs. (1) Enterprise software replacement via AI-generated code is many years away — compliance departments require extensive track records, security audit history, and error-tested deployments; the switching cost of tearing out an enterprise software stack is enormous. (2) Even if per-seat headcount falls due to AI, Salesforce prices on seats as a proxy for usage; if seats and usage diverge, they will change pricing to track economic value added to the enterprise. (3) The valuation gap has closed: five years ago Salesforce traded at twice the multiple of high-quality banks; today it trades near parity on free cash flow yield. The hurdle is now much lower. A 150-basis-point increase was added to the discount rate to reflect the wider range of AI-driven outcomes.

	- ### Airbnb ($ABNB) vs. Booking
		- Oakmark currently prefers Airbnb over Booking despite Booking's lower cash flow multiple and slightly faster growth, because: (a) adding back Airbnb's growth investment spending (experiences, new verticals) makes Airbnb appear cheaper on recurring economics; (b) Airbnb's take rate is currently below Booking's despite providing comparable-or-greater value per transaction — a gap that should close over time, implying upside in monetization. The experiences spending is viewed as a natural adjacency with a credible three-year profitability timeline, unlike Meta's metaverse spend which was far too long-dated. Both companies remain interesting; Oakmark has owned Booking instead at various points in the past.

- ## Distilled Principles

	- | Principle | Application |
	  |---|---|
	  | Private equity perspective | Look 7 years out; discount to present; require large gap to price |
	  | Oakmark accounting | Capitalize intangible growth investments — don't let GAAP limit your universe |
	  | 40% discount as margin of safety | Survive a standard deviation of estimation error without creating downside |
	  | Mid-cycle earnings | Never value on peak or trough; discount the average through a full cycle |
	  | Rank ordering > precision | Consistent assumptions across 200 names; identify the most undervalued |
	  | Three criteria: price, management, value growth | All three required; management alignment is EPS/ROIC denominator-based |
	  | ROIC matters but entry price matters more | Convergence to ROIC can take decades; Costco at 50x is not a value investment |
	  | Sell targets are live | Update value estimates aggressively; owning a stock is not a reason to keep owning it |
	  | Fresh-start sell test | "If I didn't own it today, would I buy it?" If no, sell |
	  | Avoid anchoring in both directions | Value estimates have momentum; actively hunt for conservatism before selling |
	  | Accuracy over conservatism | Model upside as honestly as downside; don't prize caution as a virtue |
	  | Trust in management scales with leverage | Highly levered businesses require higher bar on management credibility |
	  | Drawdown risk, not tracking error | Diversify to reduce drawdowns; do not manage to a benchmark |
