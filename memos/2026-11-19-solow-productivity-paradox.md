- ## Solow's paradox: technology everywhere except the productivity statistics
	- **Source**: owner's note, November 2026.
	- **Thesis**: rapid advances in technology do not always show up right away in national productivity statistics. The lag matters for AI: heavy investment and visible adoption can coexist with flat measured productivity for years.

- ## The paradox
	- **Definition**: Solow's paradox, also called the productivity paradox, is the observation that rapid advances in technology do not always show up right away in national productivity statistics.
	- **The original line**: economist Robert Solow summed it up in 1987: **"You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics."**

- ## Common explanations (background from the economics literature, not from the source)
	- | Explanation | Mechanism |
	  |---|---|
	  | Adoption lags | A new general-purpose technology needs complementary investment (reorganized workflows, new processes, retrained workers) before it pays off. Measured productivity can dip before it rises, sometimes called a "productivity J-curve" |
	  | Mismeasurement | Quality gains, free products and consumer surplus are poorly captured in GDP and price statistics |
	  | Redistribution | Gains can shift share between firms rather than raise total output |
	  | Mismanagement | Firms can invest in technology without changing how work is done |
	- **How the computer-age paradox ended**: US productivity growth accelerated in the mid-1990s to mid-2000s, about a decade after Solow's remark, as IT investment and the business changes around it matured.
