- ## "RISC-V: They Should Have Known Better" — the segmentation case against the RISC-V bull story
	- **Source**: Long-form technical essay, "RISC-V: They Should Have Known Better." Author not named in the text; the reference to writing a kernel for **rePalm** points to Dmitry Grinberg. Explicitly opinionated — the piece opens by describing the author's "distaste for RISC-V" and disclaims any employer's views. **Treat as an expert bear case, not a neutral survey**; the technical claims are specific and checkable, the market conclusions are the author's inference.
	- **Thesis**: The investable claim is not "RISC-V fails" — the author expects it to **win** the cheap-embedded and ML-accelerator-control markets outright. It is that RISC-V wins **on price, in the segments with the lowest royalty per unit**, and that concrete ISA design choices cap it below the high-margin application-processor tier that [[$ARM]] actually monetizes. A RISC-V design win is therefore weak evidence about ARM's revenue, because unit share and royalty share diverge by segment.
- ## The segmentation, which is the whole investment content
	- | Segment | Author's call | Why it matters commercially |
	  |--------------------------------|-------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|
	  | Cheap single-purpose MCUs (SD cards, USB sticks, MP3 players, vapes) | **RISC-V wins** — displaces 8051/PIC | Lowest royalty-per-unit tier; ARM's Cortex-M0 economics are already thin |
	  | ML accelerator control cores | **RISC-V wins** | Core is a DMA babysitter; perf irrelevant; avoids licensing a Cortex-A55 |
	  | RISC-V + wide vector engine | **RISC-V wins** | Performance comes from vector width, not the integer pipeline |
	  | Desktops, SBCs, interactive compute | **Not a serious player** | Segment has margin to license a better aarch64 core, and a far larger software corpus |
	  | Servers / supercomputers | Not designed for it | Fixed-length 4-byte decode is what aarch64 proved works |
	- **The stated selection mechanism is price, not merit**: *"RISC-V will own the cheap-as-dirt single-use microcontroller space eventually. Not due to its ISA design, but despite it."* And: *"good-enough solutions are always chosen via the 'ORDER BY price ASC LIMIT 1;' process."*
	- **The openness argument gets rebutted directly**, which is the part most bull cases skip: *"an open spec does not magically materialize a well-designed out-of-order core for you for free. And if someone were to design a good out-of-order core, they would not be giving it away for free."* Free ISA ≠ free implementation. The value capture in high-end cores sits in the microarchitecture, which stays proprietary either way.
- ## The technical claims that carry the segmentation
	- **Interrupt latency — RISC-V loses at its own best use case.** The only thing a cheap embedded core does is service interrupts fast and take little die area.
	  | Core | Entry | Exit | Total overhead before C handler runs |
	  |------------------|--------|--------|--------------------------------------|
	  | RV32I + Zicsr | ~21 cyc | ~20 cyc | **~44 cycles** (incl. JAL/RET) |
	  | RV32E | — | — | **~38 cycles** |
	  | **Cortex-M0** | 15 cyc | 12 cyc | **27 cycles** (hardware register stacking) |
	  - The gap is structural: ARM pushes ABI-clobbered registers **in hardware**, so the handler is written in C directly. RISC-V must do it in software. The author's read of the workarounds is the sharper point — CLIC and proprietary "fast IRQ"/auto-stacking extensions are *"an additional indictment. The base ISA forces vendors to invent non-standard silicon to reach parity with a decade-old Cortex-M0,"* which fragments the standard further.
	- **Code density, the headline RISC-V selling point, is weaker than advertised.** Compressed store offsets: **byte 0–3, halfword 0 or 2**, word 0–124 — versus Cortex-M0's byte 0–31, halfword 0–62, word 0–124. Worse, compressed byte/halfword stores are not in the C extension at all but in **Zcb**, so they may be absent entirely.
	- **No register+shifted-register addressing.** Array access takes three instructions where x86 and ARM take one. The fusion defense is dismissed on the grounds that **no known core fuses more than two consecutive instructions** (combinatorial explosion). The fix, **Zba** (SHxADD), was only ratified in **2021, over two years after the base spec** — and because SHxADD is always 4 bytes, an array access costs **6–8 bytes against ARM's 4**, which reverses the density argument.
	- **Two missing primitives, quantified against a real binary** (Raspbian aarch64 kernel 6.1.0-49): **35,393 TBZ/TBNZ** (test-bit-and-branch) against **70,109 RET** — i.e. roughly **every other function** could use a bit-test branch RISC-V does not have. Bitfield insert (6,284) plus extract (8,881) work out to **~2 of every 9 functions**. Both are described as "just wires" in hardware. The bit-ops extension that does exist, **Zbs**, is characterized as *"clean, simple, easy to explain, elegant, and completely useless."*
	- **Encoding**: immediates are scattered non-contiguously across the instruction word (J-type bit order 20/10..1/11/19..12), with **9 compressed instruction formats plus 8 more from Zcb**. The practical cost lands on emulators, JITs, and toolchains, and — noted as an unforced error — **3/4 of the encoding space is spent on compressed instructions**, space Qualcomm proposed reclaiming for better addressing modes and prototyped. It went nowhere.
- ## Optionality and the fragmentation tax
	- Nearly everything is optional: multiplication, division, user mode, supervisor mode, CSRs, compressed instructions. The feature-detection register **`misa` is itself optional, is permitted to read as all zeroes even when present, and is machine-mode only** — so user and supervisor code cannot query hardware capability at all. The author's structural point is the one to keep: *"every single thing you make optional, you split the possible implementations into two incompatible groups."*
	- **The contrast with x86/ARM is the load-bearing argument**, and it is fair: on those architectures optional features fall into two safe classes — (1) hand-intrinsic/libc-targeted performance extensions, and (2) NOP-compatible instructions (`endbr64`, most of aarch64 PAC). **Nothing a normal C compiler needs is optional.** On RISC-V, multiply, divide, and sane addressing are.
	- **RVA23 profiles are the tell, not the fix.** *"The ecosystem has had to invent a second layer of standardization whose entire purpose is to say which parts of the first standard you are actually allowed to assume exist."* Ubuntu, Red Hat, and Android are standardizing on RVA23.
	- **The commercially consequential fact**: of the currently recommended RISC-V SBCs — VisionFive 2, Banana Pi BPI-F3, Lichee Pi 4A, Orange Pi RV2, HiFive Premier P550 — **none are RVA23-compliant**, and none will run Ubuntu LTS or future AOSP. A published edit concedes one exception, the **SpacemiT K3**. The installed base is being stranded by the very profile meant to unify it, and vendors below the RVA23 cut *"are likely now screwed, as Android and Linux leave them behind."*
- ## The encoding collision — a product-risk item, not an aesthetics complaint
	- Some extension combinations are **mutually exclusive** (Zcmp and D), and the same bytes decode differently depending on which are implemented:
	  | Bytes | On one core | On another core |
	  |----------|--------------------------------|----------------------------------|
	  | `0xA002` | `C.FSDSP f0, 0(sp)` (FP store) | `CM.JT 0` (jump) |
	  | `0xAC66` | `C.FSDSP f25, 0x18(sp)` | `CM.MVA01S s0, s1` (register moves) |
	- The `0xAC66` case is the dangerous one: **no control-flow change, no illegal-instruction trap** — just silently corrupted registers and a store that never happens. The realistic failure path is a vendor-supplied binary blob (MEMS sensor calibration is the cited example) linked into firmware, working until an MCU upgrade changes the extension mix.
	- **Why this is an underwriting item and not a nerd complaint**: it converts a routine part upgrade into an unbounded debugging liability, and it is invisible to disassemblers and debuggers because both decodings are *valid*. The comparison lands — x86 preserved semantic stability across ~50 years of accumulated cruft; a clean-sheet design did not manage five. Anyone modelling RISC-V adoption in shipping products should treat **NRE and qualification cost, not silicon cost, as the real switching expense.**
- ## What the piece leaves out — and where I would push back
	- **Geopolitics is absent entirely.** The strongest real-world driver of RISC-V adoption is sanction-resistance and supply-chain sovereignty, particularly in China. That demand is indifferent to interrupt latency and encoding elegance; it is buying **the absence of a license that can be revoked**. A purely technical merit analysis systematically understates adoption where the buyer's objective function is political.
	- **"Wins the cheap embedded market" is not a small concession.** Unit volumes there are enormous, and the piece treats the segment as unglamorous rather than sizing it. The correct framing for [[$ARM]] is that this pressures **royalty volume in the Cortex-M tier** while leaving the Cortex-A/compute royalty pool — where per-unit economics are far better — defended by exactly the arguments made here.
	- **Fragmentation cuts both ways over time.** RVA23 stranding today's boards is real, but it is also the mechanism by which a credible baseline eventually forms. The bear case is strongest on **the transition cost** and weakest as a permanent ceiling claim.
	- **Nothing here is a claim about who captures value.** Even where RISC-V wins on the author's own terms, the profit likely accrues to the accelerator or SoC vendor that stops paying a license — not to a RISC-V-branded entity. RISC-V share gains and any single company's earnings are largely decoupled, which is the trap in trading this theme directly.
