- ## Progress So Far
	- **Physical construction is well underway**: the site is described as a 5-mile-by-1-mile dirt pit with trucks, cranes, cement work, dust, worker camps, and RV parks.
	- **Land assembly has expanded materially**: the project has grown from the original state mega-site to almost 4,000 acres dedicated to Hyperion, including more than 1,700 acres sold by the Franklin family since the groundbreaking.
	- **Power plan is being built around dedicated gas generation**: Entergy is planning 10 gas-fired plants, with 6 in the parish and regulators expected to expedite remaining approvals.
	- **Local real estate already repricing**: speculative land asks, temporary housing, restaurants, food trucks, hotels, and new addresses show that economic impact arrives well before data-center operations.
	- **Labor markets are already being disrupted**: police officers and nurses are leaving jobs for higher-paying construction-site roles, creating second-order local staffing pressure.
	- **Institutional financing is already structured**: Blue Owl / Pimco-backed financing shifts much of real-estate / construction ownership off Meta's balance sheet while Meta retains operating control and owns the chip stack.

- ## Data Center Buildout Insights
	- **Power is the gating variable before land or labor**: Meta's site search prioritized electricity availability because 5 GW of compute capacity is not normally available from existing grids.
	- **AI data centers invert the classic rural-development problem**: Richland Parish failed for decades to attract factories because it could not support 5,000 permanent jobs. AI data centers require enormous land, power, and capital but relatively few permanent workers, making previously disadvantaged rural sites newly viable.
	- **State and utility alignment can be as important as site economics**: Entergy, the governor's economic-development team, state legislators, and federal lawmakers were all involved before public announcement.
	- **Secrecy speeds execution but creates legitimacy risk**: NDAs, shell-company land rights, bill repurposing, and post-facto public disclosure reduced friction up front but seeded resident distrust and local-government information gaps.
	- **Tax incentives are increasingly tied to chips, not buildings**: Meta's required sales-tax exemption covered chips, explicitly the most expensive component. This reflects the data-center value stack shifting from real estate to accelerators.
	- **Off-balance-sheet financing helps preserve flexibility**: Meta holds only 20% of the campus JV, with Blue Owl owning the rest and \$27B of debt backing the structure. Meta still owns the chips, but the campus financing lowers direct balance-sheet burden.
	- **Chip replacement cadence is an underappreciated risk**: if GPUs need replacement every few years, a data center is not a one-time capex asset. The facility becomes a recurring hardware refresh platform.
	- **Lease optionality cuts both ways**: four-year increments give Meta flexibility if AI returns disappoint, but local stakeholders bear path-dependency risk from infrastructure, housing, and land-use changes.
	- **Local impact starts before revenue**: construction wages, road use, housing inflation, dust, worker inflows, school relocation questions, and emergency-service strain appear before any AI-compute monetization.
	- **Hyperscaler substitution is becoming part of local underwriting**: local officials hope another hyperscaler could replace Meta if Meta leaves, implying communities increasingly view data-center-ready infrastructure as a reusable asset class.

- ## Sentiment Synthesis
	- **Local support is strongest among officials and landowners who see a once-in-decades development opportunity**: the parish had endured industrial rejection, agricultural decline, poverty, and outmigration; Hyperion offers visible activity and political validation.
	- **Resident sentiment is less ideological than practical**: concerns center on rent, traffic, dust, accidents, school proximity, water use, land-price speculation, and whether local people will actually receive durable benefits.
	- **Process risk is the deepest source of distrust**: local residents and some civic figures learned details after decisions were effectively complete, creating a perception of a "done deal."
	- **The jobs-to-capex ratio is politically fragile**: permanent employment commitments of 300 by 2031 and 500 by 2035 are small relative to more than \$200B of alleged spend, 5 GW of compute capacity, 10 gas-fired plants, and large tax breaks.
	- **Support often sounds like resignation**: several locals express a view that change is already here and the rational response is to benefit from it rather than resist it.

- ## Stock-Relevant Insights
	- **Meta is pursuing AI infrastructure at national-infrastructure scale**: Hyperion's stated 5 GW compute target and more than \$200B spend figure imply AI capacity planning has moved beyond ordinary data-center expansion into utility, politics, finance, and regional-development execution.
	- **Capex flexibility is engineered into the structure**: off-balance-sheet campus ownership, four-year lease increments, and Meta-owned chips allow Meta to pursue scale while preserving exit / refresh optionality.
	- **AI capex is becoming a private-credit asset class**: Blue Owl's majority JV ownership and \$27B debt package show that non-bank capital is now part of hyperscaler AI buildout funding.
	- **Data-center permitting advantage may shift toward rural, pro-energy jurisdictions**: abundant land, oil/gas infrastructure, lighter regulation, and communities seeking economic lifelines can outweigh traditional proximity-to-talent considerations.
	- **Power and water externalities may become the binding constraint**: the project requires 10 gas plants and up to 1B gallons of annual water, making utility politics and community acceptance key execution risks.
	- **Permanent-job scarcity will remain a backlash vector**: data centers can transform local infrastructure and land markets while producing relatively few full-time roles, making tax incentives harder to defend over time.
	- **Meta's AI strategy is increasingly dependent on political execution**: securing tax exemptions, land-law changes, utility commitments, and federal/state political support are now core to AI capacity buildout.
	- **GPU procurement remains the core economic exposure**: Meta still owns chips that cost tens of billions and may need replacement every few years; this keeps Meta directly exposed to accelerator price, performance, and obsolescence cycles.

- ## Open Questions / What to Monitor
	- Whether Hyperion actually reaches 5 GW compute capacity and on what timeline.
	- Whether the "more than \$200B" spend figure is confirmed by Meta or reflected in future capex disclosures.
	- Whether the Blue Owl JV structure becomes a repeatable template for other hyperscaler AI campuses.
	- Whether Entergy's 10 gas-fired plants face delays, legal challenges, environmental pushback, or cost overruns.
	- Whether promised customer savings of \$2B over 20 years materialize or become politically contested.
	- Whether local opposition grows as construction impacts accumulate, especially around water, dust, accidents, housing inflation, and school relocation.
	- Whether Meta meets 2031 / 2035 job targets and whether local hiring becomes a future political demand.
	- Whether GPU refresh economics make four-year lease optionality strategically valuable or raise questions about infrastructure stranded-asset risk.
