- ## Beren Millidge: scaling laws belong to the data, not the model
	- **Source**: [The Scaling Laws Are In Our Stars, Not Ourselves](https://www.beren.io/2025-03-01-The-Scaling-Laws-Are-In-Our-Stars-Not-Ourselves/), Beren Millidge, March 1, 2025. **The earliest and most theoretical of the three related posts** — the mechanism underneath [[2026-08-15-beren-scaling-is-subtler-than-it-seems]] (which constraints must hold) and [[2026-08-23-beren-architecture-research-as-scaling-bottlenecks]] (how architecture relaxes them). **Author's epistemic status: "Pretty uncertain… does have some support, but is not completely rigorous."** Treat as a working model, not a result.
	- **Thesis**: *"The scaling laws… are primarily a property of our datasets rather than that of our models."* Given a sufficiently expressive architecture, the **shape** of the curve comes from the power-law decay of the dataset's covariance spectrum; the architecture only sets the **coefficients**. The investable inversion: **compute buys movement along a curve whose shape you do not control, and only data changes the curve.**
- ## The mechanism, in three steps
	- **1. Naturalistic data is spectrally fractal.** Text, images, and video have "almost infinite levels of detail," with most variance explained by a few causal factors and progressively more fine structure below. Stated precisely, **the covariance matrix of the dataset has a power-law eigenvalue decay** — and he notes this is directly checkable by computing the empirical covariance spectrum of real dataset subsets.
	- **2. Networks learn the spectrum in eigenvalue order.** Training learns eigenvectors of the data covariance **in parallel, at a speed proportional to their eigenvalue** — so the highest-variance features are acquired first. This is [mathematically established for linear networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6120) (Saxe et al.); the hypothesis is that nonlinear networks behave essentially the same way, bounded by dataset size and capacity.
	- **3. The power law falls out.** Learning speed also rises monotonically with model size. For a given model and token budget, only so many eigenvectors can be learned in the "time available." Scale the model → learn more eigenvectors → move further down the spectral slope → **power law in loss versus parameters**. Train on more unique data (1 epoch assumed) → same effect at fixed parameters → **power law in loss versus dataset size**. The key assumption is that a feature's eigenvalue is proportional to the loss drop from learning it, which is what makes the two curves line up.
- ## The most useful implication: the returns are linear, the *value* is what decays
	- Because the observed relationship is a power law, the implied fact about capacity is that **feature acquisition scales linearly**: *"a 10x bigger model can learn 10x more underlying features, it is just that these features contribute progressively less to the loss because the more important features were learnt first."*
	- **This is the reframe worth keeping.** There is no model-side saturation in this account — a bigger model is not learning less efficiently. **The diminishing returns live in the information structure of the world**, because each successive feature explains less variance than the one before it. Compute converts into features at a constant rate; features convert into loss reduction at a decaying rate.
	- **Consequence for evaluating architecture claims**: expect a better architecture to shift the constant, not to break the exponent. He says as much — MoEs and hybrid SSMs show "superior" scaling laws, but the improvement is in coefficients. Conversely an insufficiently expressive model (LSTMs, plain MLPs) can be **worse than power-law** entirely, because it cannot represent the structure at all. This is the same conclusion the 2026 posts reach from the bottleneck side.
	- The telescope metaphor that recurs in the later posts **originates here**: model size is aperture, training length is exposure time, and "these linear effects get transmuted to power-law decreases in the loss" by the structure of the data itself.
- ## The falsifiable core — and why it matters for synthetic data
	- The claim is not merely interpretive. **Change the covariance structure and you change the scaling law**: on random Gaussian data, models converge as a **square root**, as the central limit theorem implies — not a power law. Synthetic datasets can be constructed to produce other scaling behaviours on demand.
	- **This is the sharpest practical read-through in the post, and he does not draw it out.** If the power law is a property of the data's spectrum, then a synthetic corpus whose spectrum is truncated or non-power-law does not just yield slightly worse data — it yields **a worse scaling exponent**. Given how central synthetic pipelines have become to covering rare capabilities, the relevant quality metric for generated data is not fluency or eval lift but **whether it preserves fractal spectral structure**. Pairs with the caution in [[2026-08-15-beren-scaling-is-subtler-than-it-seems]] that synthetic data can be "incredibly repetitive and thus prone to overfitting if done poorly."
	- Related and practical: **datasets differ in intrinsic difficulty**. Code has more regular structure and therefore lower inherent entropy than natural language, so a corpus heavy in code produces **lower loss at equal scale, all else equal**. Cross-corpus loss comparisons between models are therefore close to meaningless without knowing the mix — a useful filter when a lab reports a loss or perplexity number.
- ## Where the power law ends
	- On a **fixed** dataset, the power law terminates at the point of overparametrization, once the model has memorized all the structure present. Assuming full rank, the spectrum's limit is the number of datapoints, so scaling should hold **only for as long as the network remains underparametrized**. With an infinite stream from the same generative process, it would in principle continue indefinitely.
	- **Read-through**: the durability of pretraining scaling is a function of the continued supply of *unique* data, not of model engineering. That routes directly into the expert-data and RL-environment spend identified in the later post — the industry is buying continuation of the curve.
- ## Loss scaling is not capability scaling — written in March 2025
	- Scaling laws relate **training loss** to scale. The relationship to downstream task performance is "not necessarily guaranteed to be linear or powerlaw," remains "somewhat hard to predict," and is only mostly monotonic — decreasing loss helps, by an unpredictable amount.
	- **The line worth noting for calibration**, published eighteen months before the follow-up post: *"the recent performance gains of the last two years have primarily been due to improved datasets… rather than pure pretraining scaling on arbitrary web text."* The 2026 sequel's blunter version — that nobody does Chinchilla-style web-corpus pretraining anymore — was already visible here. The author has been consistent and early on this, which is worth weighting when reading his less rigorous claims.
- ## The open question he leaves unanswered
	- **Why should large natural datasets have power-law covariance spectra at all?** He has no answer, only pointers to [maximum-entropy derivations of power laws](https://academic.oup.com/jeb/article-abstract/22/8/1563/7408072) and work relating [power-law dynamics to chaos and supersymmetric stochastic dynamics](https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/18/4/108).
	- **Why this gap matters commercially**: the entire AI capex case rests on a smooth, predictable return curve. On this account that predictability is inherited from an empirical regularity of the world that nobody can currently derive — so there is no theory guaranteeing it holds into regions we have not yet measured, particularly for curated and synthetic corpora that are increasingly *engineered* rather than sampled. The curve is an observation, not a law.
