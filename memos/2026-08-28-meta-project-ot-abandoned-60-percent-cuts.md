- ## Meta's abandoned "Project OT" — a 60% team-reduction plan that got called off
	- **Source**: The Pragmatic Engineer (Gergely Orosz), summarizing **Reuters** reporting based on *"one internal planning document reviewed by Reuters and three people familiar with the project."* Orosz supplies his own prior reporting and inference on top. **Two bias flags**: he writes for an engineering audience and has been openly critical of Meta's approach, and **the "30–40% company-wide layoff" figure is explicitly his suspicion, not a reported fact** — the reported number is 60% *per affected team*, which is not the same thing.
	- **Thesis**: This is the closest thing available to a **controlled experiment on AI labor substitution run by the best-positioned firm in the world** — maximum AI capability, maximum incentive, full internal information — and **it was aborted before the second wave.** For anyone modelling AI-driven opex reduction at any company, the most informative fact of 2026 may be that Meta planned it, partially executed it, and then stopped.
- ## What was planned, and what actually happened
	- | Item | Detail |
	  |------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|
	  | Origin | **January 2026** leadership retreat at Zuckerberg's Hawaii compound |
	  | Code name | **Project OT** — Organization Transformation |
	  | Vision | An **"AI native"** Meta where AI performs much of the daily work of thousands of employees, with virtual workers overseen by smaller **"talent-dense"** human cadres |
	  | Target | **60% reduction in affected team sizes**, via layoffs plus reallocation |
	  | Core assumption | AI would let the smaller teams **operate as well as before** |
	  | Scale expected by HR | **Larger than the 2022–23 layoffs**, when ~**25% of staff** were cut |
	  | Structure | **Two waves — May and November 2026** |
	  | What happened | On the night of **May 19**, hours before wave one, Zuckerberg **"blinked."** Meta cut **10%** on May 20 and **cancelled November planning** |
	  | Stated reason | Employees were **"in open revolt,"** convinced the AI initiatives were aimed at replacing them |
	- **The gap between plan and execution is the whole story**: a program sized to exceed a 25% company-wide reduction was cut down to roughly 10%, and the second wave was killed **the night before the first one landed**. That is not a phased rollout — it is an abandonment under pressure at the last possible moment.
- ## Why the abort reason matters, and where the source over-reads
	- **Reuters' stated cause is organizational — employee revolt — not technical.** Orosz's framing partly collapses the two, and they carry opposite implications:
		- **If organizational**: the AI substitution thesis may be technically sound but **politically unexecutable at speed**. The constraint is workforce reaction and reputational cost, which softens over time and with normalization. Bearish for near-term opex relief, not for the long-run thesis.
		- **If capability**: the partial execution revealed the assumption was false, and no amount of resolve fixes it. Structurally bearish for the substitution thesis itself.
	- **The evidence Orosz supplies points to the second reading**, which is the more consequential one:
		- Teams that took **30–40% cuts struggled to cope with their workloads** — i.e. even well short of 60%, output did not hold.
		- **A string of outages followed**, including an Instagram **"zero auth password reset"** failure in which an account could be taken over simply by asking the AI bot to change the associated email — reportedly demonstrated against a former US president's account. That is a severe authentication failure, and it is the concrete cost side of the experiment.
		- **Domain knowledge was lost**: engineers with critical context were among those reassigned, and that capability did not transfer.
	- **The honest read is that both are true and the sourcing cannot separate them.** Employee revolt is the proximate cause; degraded output is the evidence that the revolt was correct. **Treat "Meta aborted because AI wasn't ready" as the plausible inference it is, not as established fact** — Reuters does not say that.
- ## The hidden headcount effect that financial disclosure misses
	- Alongside the layoffs, **20–30% of engineers in infra and product teams were reassigned to data-labeling work for AI training.** This never appears as a headcount reduction.
	- **This is a measurement problem for anyone modelling Meta's engineering output.** [[META-2026-Q2]] reports headcount **>75,000, down 3% QoQ**, with ~8,000 affected employees mostly out of the count by end of Q3 and a **\$1.2B severance charge**. That understates the effective reduction in product and infrastructure capacity, because a large additional slice was moved to internal AI training work rather than removed. **Headcount down 3% and effective product engineering capacity down far more are compatible**, and the disclosure cannot distinguish them.
	- It also reframes a line already on file: R&D reaching **36% of revenue**, attributed to AI-talent compensation and infrastructure depreciation. Part of that spend is **existing engineers redirected into data work**, which is an AI cost carried inside a product line rather than a genuine capability addition.
- ## The financial counterfactual, which is the investable part
	- **Meta considered and rejected a path to structurally lower operating expense** at exactly the moment it needed one. Per [[2026-08-03-stratechery-meta-timing-problems-financial-tail]], expenses grew **55% against 28% revenue growth**, and [[META-2026-Q2]] shows free cash flow collapsing to **\$784M** as capex consumed operating cash flow.
	- **Project OT was the offset.** A reduction on the scale contemplated would have been the opex counterweight to the capex build. **Cancelling the November wave means the capex burden is not being offset by headcount reduction** — which converts the expense/revenue divergence from a transitional problem into a structural one, at least through the planning horizon that was cancelled.
	- **The optionality is not gone, and that is the forward-looking point.** The plan exists, the modelling was done, and the only thing that stopped it was timing and morale. **A revived Project OT is a live scenario** — most plausibly framed as an efficiency initiative once AI coding tooling has visibly matured, or after a quarter in which investors punish the expense line hard enough to make the reputational cost acceptable. Zuckerberg has run this play before under the "year of efficiency" label.
- ## Read-through beyond Meta
	- **This is the strongest available counter-evidence to the aggressive AI-labor-substitution thesis**, precisely because of who ran the test. Meta has frontier models, unlimited compute, direct financial motive, and complete visibility into its own workflows. If 60% was not achievable here in 2026, discount claims of comparable reductions elsewhere — and discount vendor claims that AI tooling delivers step-change engineering productivity today.
	- It sits against the demand-side arguments in [[2026-07-27-vibe-coding-demand-destruction-vs-saas-operating-cost]] and [[2026-07-25-ai-agents-indie-saas-seo-demand-compression]]: **if the largest, most capable buyer cannot cut its own engineering organization by 60%, the case that AI agents rapidly compress software seats and headcount economy-wide gets materially weaker.**
	- **The cautionary operational lesson generalizes**: the failure mode was not a straight capability shortfall but **the loss of specific people holding specific context**, plus reliability regressions that surfaced as security incidents. Those costs are back-loaded and do not show up in the quarter the savings are booked — which is precisely why a plan can look sound in January and be cancelled in May.
