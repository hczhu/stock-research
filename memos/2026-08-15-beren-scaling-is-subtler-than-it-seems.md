- ## Beren Millidge: scaling is a regime, not an operation
	- **Source**: [Scaling is subtler than it seems](https://www.beren.io/2026-08-15-Scaling-is-Subtler-than-it-Seems/), Beren Millidge, August 15, 2026. The **parent post** to [[2026-08-23-beren-architecture-research-as-scaling-bottlenecks]] — read together, this one enumerates the constraints and that one explains architecture research as the work of relaxing them. **Epistemic caveat from the author**: explicitly "vibes" and tacit practitioner knowledge, "oversimplified, but hopefully still useful," and possibly wrong in details.
	- **Thesis**: *"Scaling is not an operation but a regime. If you try scaling in most cases you will fail."* Five constraints must be satisfied simultaneously — objective, data, architecture, optimizer, infrastructure — and must **stay** satisfied across orders of magnitude as each grows more demanding. The investable consequences: **the binding constraint has already migrated from research to systems engineering**, "scaling laws" are being invoked far outside where they are actually proven, and the real remaining bottleneck is **data acquisition**, which is a cash cost rather than an idea.
- ## The frame: models as amortized approximators, and the telescope
	- A pretrained model is an [amortized approximator](https://www.beren.io/2022-09-25-Deconfusing-direct-vs-amortized-optimization/) of the data distribution — training compresses the distribution into weights, and the model learns "a shadow" of it. Fidelity is set by four things: **model capacity, numerical capacity for signal propagation, the SNR with which the optimizer sculpts data into weights, and the optimizability of the loss landscape**.
	- Training proceeds by learning progressively **deeper and rarer features**, and stops when one of three things happens: capacity runs out, **gradient SNR falls until the rate of learning equals the rate of forgetting**, or new data runs out.
	- **The telescope metaphor is the reusable part**: model capacity is **aperture** (more photons, finer features resolved); training length / unique data is **exposure time** (averaging out noise). Kaplan and Chinchilla describe how the two trade off, because both draw on the same scarce resource — compute. But the metaphor extends to the failure modes: there must actually be an image out there, enough photons arriving, low enough internal noise, and a working mechanism to inscribe it.
- ## The five constraints — and where the binding one now sits
	- | # | Constraint | What it requires |
	  |---|---------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------|
	  | 1 | **Objective** | Unbounded in potential complexity; must not diverge from modelling the data when minimized |
	  | 2 | **Data distribution** | "Almost infinite, fractal-style, but ultimately learnable complexity" — not so simple it is exhausted, not so complex there is no signal |
	  | 3 | **Architecture** | Expressive enough for the data's generative process while numerically stable, optimizable, and propagating gradient into most parameters |
	  | 4 | **Optimizer / schedule** | Stable, keeps reducing loss to the limits set by data and architecture; naive SGD+momentum fails — *"we only really became capable of scaling when we reached the Adam era"* |
	  | 5 | **Systems and infrastructure** | Large-scale data, parallelism, training infra, hyperparameter selection, numerics and instability tracking |
	- **The single most investable sentence in the piece** is the coda to that list: *"Now that most of the research on points 1–4 has already been done, this is often the largest challenge in practice."* The frontier bottleneck is **infrastructure and engineering execution**, not ideas. That is a direct argument for why capability leadership tracks cluster reliability, tooling, and numerics discipline — and why the gap between labs with equivalent research talent comes down to whether their training runs survive.
	- Supporting this: the author's list of ways to fail is dominated by engineering, not theory. **Subtle bugs get their own category** — things that "only slightly degrade the model at small scales but blow up to be major issues at large scale." The named pernicious class is **causal leaks that destroy autoregression even if only a tiny number of bits leak**; harmless at small scale because the model lacks capacity to exploit them, then producing "baffling collapses at large scale." The cited classic location is normalization — e.g. top-k across time in naive [expert-choice](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09368) MoE implementations. His conclusion: *"Empirical ML is an engineering discipline requiring intense attention to subtle detail."*
- ## "Scaling laws" are doing equivocation work in the industry narrative
	- Since GPT-3 the narrative has been committed to scaling, **but the referent keeps moving** — parameters, then compute, then data and data "quality," then RL and reasoning training, chain-of-thought length, and test-time compute. Genuine scaling laws exist only for the relationship between **model parameters, a fixed pretraining data distribution of varying size, and validation loss on that corpus**. Millidge's warning is that "people apply the scientific notion of proven scaling laws much more broadly than is technically warranted."
	- **Two footnotes sharpen this into something usable for reading capex arguments**:
		- *"Despite the industry continually touting scaling, the actual Kaplan/Chinchilla style scaling has played a relatively small part in the dramatic progress in AI. **Nobody does Chinchilla-style LLM pretraining on web-corpora anymore.**"*
		- Even after reasoning models succeeded, **there are still no publicly accepted empirical scaling laws for RL** comparable to Chinchilla — only [early attempts](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786v1) and presumably private lab studies.
	- **How to use it**: when a compute-demand thesis cites "scaling laws" to justify RL or test-time-compute spend, it is borrowing credibility from a result that was established somewhere else and does not transfer. RL does improve with scale — but the *predictability* that makes pretraining capex underwritable has not been publicly demonstrated for it. That is a real difference in the quality of the forecast, not a quibble.
	- A related caveat the author repeats: scaling laws describe **validation loss on a particular dataset**. Whether that improves the downstream capability you care about is a separate question entirely.
- ## Data is the real bottleneck — and it converts into a recurring cash cost
	- **The tails-come-apart problem**: the model learns *the dataset*, not what you want. Labs started on Common Crawl because it was available; nobody wanted a random internet-text predictor. At low capability, improving on Common Crawl improves math and code too. **As scale increases those objectives decorrelate** — still positively correlated, but "the slope of the improvement will become extremely low."
	- **The feature-spectrum framing is the practical version**: on raw web text, the capabilities you want sit far down the spectrum — the model learns forum styles, gossip, and news long before nontrivial math or AI research, and reaching those features demands enormous capacity and data. **Curate the corpus toward the target and those features move close to the surface**, reachable by far smaller models with far less data.
	- **Hence the deflation claim**: a large share of apparent "AI progress" can be [simulated by training on better data](https://www.beren.io/2025-08-02-Most-Algorithmic-Progress-is-Data-Progress/). It is why [tiny models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05365) today outperform much larger models from a few years ago — the data distribution now sits much closer to the skills we measure "and, cynically, the evals." **Read-through: model-cost deflation is substantially a data-engineering achievement, which means the cost did not vanish, it relocated** — out of flops and into corpus construction.
	- **The named industry consequence, which is the clearest investment content in the post**: because the capabilities now being targeted — proving novel theorems, zero-day cyberattacks, whole codebases, novel AI research bets — are *"extremely rare in naturalistic datasets,"* progress *"requires a huge industry of data providers that pay humans to undertake these tasks and provide the outputs and thinking traces to AI labs, or produce a synthetic data pipeline to bootstrap examples of otherwise incredibly rare capabilities."* He frames it as [distillation from humans](https://www.beren.io/2026-06-12-Distillation-Aint-What-It-Used-To-Be/).
		- **And it shifts again under RL**: there, "much of the challenge is constructing environments and verifiers rather than just sourcing data." **Expert-data vendors and RL-environment builders are a structurally growing line item, and the composition is moving from annotation toward environment and verifier engineering.**
		- Caveat he flags: synthetic data can be "incredibly repetitive and thus prone to overfitting if done poorly" — so synthetic pipelines are not a free substitute for the human bill.
	- **Three requirements for good data**: (1) align with the downstream capabilities wanted; (2) high but learnable intrinsic complexity — "fractal manifolds"; (3) large and diverse enough that rare features repeat in many guises, yet convergent enough that real abstract structure exists. **(2) and (3) come "for free" at internet scale. The whole game is (1)** — and (1) is getting harder as targets get rarer.
	- **Against curriculum learning, with a Moravec twist**: transformers appear "almost universally plastic at initialization" and will learn any distribution thrown at them. Counterintuitively, **hard math and code often have *lower* perplexity than random internet text** for good models — intellectually demanding content is highly predictable once you grasp its structure and has low intrinsic randomness, while an idiosyncratic blogger is genuinely unpredictable. Curricula generally *hurt*, by introducing nonstationary data that causes plasticity and forgetting problems against an i.i.d. objective; labs use staged training only because high-quality data is scarce and placing it last weights it more heavily.
	- Scale note on overfitting: the MNIST era ran ~100 epochs; LLMs now do [fewer than 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.16264), and only on selected high-quality subsets.
- ## Why next-token prediction endures, and why alternatives are harder
	- NTP literally matches the empirical data distribution — the autoregressive process is the conditional factorization of the joint density. Two properties make it unusually good: **it cannot be gamed** (no way to reduce loss without calibrated prediction of the actual next token), and its **bit-rate is extremely high**, up to log-vocab-size bits per token, with logit distillation as the main way to exceed it.
	- The comparison set explains why nothing has displaced it: **classification** objectives can be solved using a small subset of the information; **diffusion** preserves information in theory but noise adds variance and signal is hard to maintain across the schedule (the high-noise end is impossible, the low-noise end trivial, and the useful middle **moves during training**); **GANs** are theoretically convergent but unstable under the minimax formulation; **contrastive** objectives need care to be unhackable and often do not require full information preservation.
	- **Worth pairing with the companion memo**: the sequel frames diffusion as an attractive *hardware* trade (bandwidth for compute), while this post explains why it is a hard *optimization* problem. Both are true, and the tension is the honest state of the diffusion-LLM question.
- ## Architecture, the bitter lesson, and two contrarian data points
	- **The bitter lesson, restated mechanically**: hand-coded inductive bias helps at small scale by supplying structure the model lacks capacity to learn, but no real distribution matches a hand-coded prior, so with enough compute and data it is better to encode **the most minimal prior that covers the hypothesis space**. Hence convergence on maximally general primitives — MLPs as arbitrary nonlinear transforms, gating and norms, and attention as "the most general second-order interaction possible." Convolutions survive only where the bias genuinely matches the generative physics (vision, audio), and are rare in language, which "has no innate convolutional smoothing."
	- **The transformer read as minimalism**: residual stream for stable propagation through depth; MLPs are two-layer because that is the fewest that retains a nonlinearity, deliberately routing complex circuits through the residual stream; attention as the simplest fully general input-dependent quadratic sequence mapping. Neat reframing for anyone modelling inference: with a frozen KV cache, **attention at decode looks like a two-layer MLP across time**, with the key cache as input weights and the value cache as output weights.
	- **The prediction**: the transformer is *"perhaps the first architecture in a general universality class of sufficiently expressive and parallelizably trainable general sequence mixers, as well as one of the simplest members of this class."* Expect elaboration within the class, not replacement of it — consistent with the state-size/expressivity Pareto framing in [[2026-08-23-beren-architecture-research-as-scaling-bottlenecks]].
	- **Contrarian data point one — models are getting shallower, not deeper.** Llama3-405B has **126 layers**; DeepSeek-V4 has **61**; Kimi-K3 has **93**. Despite the scaling rhetoric, depth is not what is being scaled — width, sparsity, and context are. This is the concrete evidence behind the depth-bottleneck discussion in the companion post.
	- **Contrarian data point two — transformers [lose plasticity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24752) over long training runs**, and scaling parameter count "delays but does not appear to prevent this transition." Training pushes models out of a favourable initialization region into "numerical traps of uncertain origin." **This is a genuine open risk to the assumption that ever-longer training converts compute into capability indefinitely**, and it is unresolved.
	- Also worth noting: attention itself carries almost no inductive bias, but **position encoding carries a great deal**. That RoPE-style schemes work implies token-token correlations in natural language really do decay roughly as a power law in distance — a rare case where an empirical architectural win reveals something about the data.
- ## The closing reframe, which is the bridge to the sequel
	- The bitter lesson gets restated in bottleneck terms: because compute grows exponentially, **the methods that matter most are those which remove the bottlenecks to reaching and staying in the smooth scaling regime**, since only those let you deploy exponentially increasing compute at consistently positive returns.
	- **This is the whole investment frame in one line**: compute growth is the given; the scarce, value-creating input is whatever currently prevents that compute from converting into capability. On this account that is, in order — infrastructure and engineering execution first, target-aligned data second, architecture third.
