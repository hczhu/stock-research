- ## Intel and AMD: integrated optimization versus modular flexibility
	- **Source**: Ben Thompson, [Intel Earnings, AMD Earnings, Intel and AMD's Divergence](https://stratechery.com/2026/intel-earnings-amd-earnings-intel-and-amds-divergence/), November 11, 2026; user-supplied eight-page PDF, substantive analysis on pp. 1–6. Financial cross-checks: [Intel Q3 release](https://www.intc.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1786/intel-reports-third-quarter-2026-financial-results) and [AMD Q3 release](https://ir.amd.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1303/amd-reports-third-quarter-2026-financial-results). Management statements below are attributed as quoted in the article unless otherwise noted. Unrelated opening commentary and the sponsor advertisement are excluded.
	- **Thesis**: AMD's modular architecture creates flexibility when demand shifts between markets; Intel's integrated design/manufacturing model offers tighter optimization but makes production execution and factory utilization central to returns. The divergent outlooks illustrate different abilities to fulfill demand—not simply different exposure to an AI boom. Thompson's counterintuitive argument is that Intel may need to restore a functioning, performance-focused IDM rather than attempt to become another AMD or TSMC.

- ## Two kinds of modularity—and why the distinction matters
	- | Dimension | Intel's inherited approach | AMD's approach in the article |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | Company structure | Designs chips and operates fabs; now also sources some chips from TSMC and seeks outside foundry customers | Fabless leading-edge design business relying on external manufacturing |
	  | Historical source of advantage | Co-optimize product design with superior in-house process technology | Reuse strong designs and obtain manufacturing capability from specialized suppliers |
	  | Product architecture | Historically monolithic; now uses chiplets, but client and server compute dies remain distinct in the examples discussed | Compatible desktop/server products can share compute chiplets, with differentiation in chiplet count and I/O |
	  | Response to PC weakness | Change fabrication capacity allocation and process mix to produce more server silicon | Reallocate suitable unassembled compute chiplets toward server packages |
	  | Where risk concentrates | Yields, cycle times, packaging, product mix, and utilization of owned manufacturing assets | Foundry allocation, supplier economics, packaging capacity, and product execution |
	- **Fabless and modular are different choices**: Outsourcing manufacturing does not inherently require chiplets; an IDM can also build highly modular products. Thompson's argument is about the design habits and constraints each company developed, not an unavoidable law of corporate structure.
	- **The architectural turning point**: The article uses 2019 Zen 2 desktop processors and EPYC Rome to explain AMD's separation of leading-edge compute from I/O. Different I/O dies provide memory-controller and PCIe capabilities appropriate to each market, while common compute building blocks spread design investment across products. [AMD's 2019 product announcement](https://www.amd.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2019-5-26-amd-announces-next-generation-leadership-products.html) corroborates the chiplet strategy across Ryzen and EPYC.
	- **Investment inference**: Shared building blocks can pool inventory risk and let AMD allocate scarce silicon toward stronger-demand products. This flexibility is economically valuable before considering any CPU benchmark advantage.
	- **Boundary on that flexibility**: It is not conversion of finished PCs into servers, and not every AMD laptop, desktop, server, or GPU die is interchangeable. Generation compatibility, qualification, performance/power binning, I/O dies, substrates, assembly, and customer acceptance still constrain what can ship. The article supplies no volume or margin estimate for actual chiplet reallocations.
- ## Intel's bottleneck is getting the right silicon through the entire chain
	- Tan identifies three distinct constraints: Intel 3 yield/cycle-time improvement still needed; shortages of advanced organic substrates used with EMIB; and an accident-related Israel factory shutdown affecting Q4 10-nanometer server shipments. These are supply constraints, separate from weaker PC demand.
	- **The capacity-switch example**: Zinsner says Intel is taking 18A wafer capacity intended generally for Panther Lake offline to add Intel 3 capacity in Oregon for data-center production. This is a manufacturing reallocation, not a relabeling of existing inventory; tooling, process changes, qualification, and ramp timing matter. The quoted answer does not disclose cost, duration, or require assuming an entire fab rebuild.
	- **Packaging amplifies the difference**: Thompson contrasts Intel's dense EMIB silicon-bridge connections with the more conventional organic-substrate interconnect approach used in the AMD CPUs discussed. His argument is that Intel accepts more manufacturing complexity to pursue tighter integration, while AMD benefits from a simpler, scalable assembly approach.
	- **Do not turn this into a blanket performance verdict**: Chiplet links entail power/latency tradeoffs, but the article gives no controlled benchmark proving Intel is universally faster or more efficient. Its comparison is CPU- and generation-specific, not a claim that all AMD products avoid advanced packaging or all future Intel CPUs retain the same layout.
	- **Demand is not enough**: More server orders do not become revenue until wafer output, usable die yield, substrates, packaging, and final qualification all support delivery. Fixing one stage can merely expose the next constraint.
- ## Why Thompson thinks Intel may need to be more Intel
	- **Author's argument**: Separating Intel's design and manufacturing businesses assumes each can compete independently. Thompson questions whether building an external foundry is distracting from the immediate task of producing sufficient competitive Intel server chips. He proposes renewed integration and performance focus as a possible alternative—not a management-announced strategic reversal.
	- **Steelman for integration**: Product/process/package co-design can create differentiated performance, and internal demand can anchor fab utilization. Intel does not have to match AMD's inventory flexibility if its product advantages and production execution generate superior customer economics.
	- **Why it is not automatically a cure**: Internal demand alone may not provide enough manufacturing scale, utilization, or returns to fund process development. External foundry customers could improve that equation. The article does not calculate whether an internally focused IDM or a broader foundry strategy has the better return on invested capital.
	- **Strategic change is slower than a reorganization**: Product architectures, fabrication processes, and supplier ecosystems were developed over years. A reporting-segment split or new leadership does not immediately make incompatible dies interchangeable or cure a packaging shortage.
- ## Different investment questions for INTC and AMD
	- **INTC: production recovery and capital productivity**. The relevant evidence is saleable server output, cycle time, yield, substrate availability, and whether factory changes improve margins and cash generation. Strong orders or a process roadmap alone are insufficient. The article also quotes Nova Lake design wins expected at twice Panther Lake's launch level—a forward-looking customer-design indicator, not guaranteed shipment volume.
	- **AMD: adaptability and profitable demand capture**. Shared compute designs can support server share gains and better product allocation, but outsourced manufacturing is not costless or unconstrained. Separate EPYC CPU progress from Instinct GPU revenue, and test whether stronger mix outweighs the PC downturn and supply-chain costs.
	- **TSMC: the supplier behind AMD's flexibility**. AMD avoids owning leading-edge fabs, but the capital still has to be deployed somewhere. Foundry dependence transfers manufacturing execution to a specialist while leaving AMD exposed to capacity allocation and supplier pricing; it does not eliminate industry capital intensity.
	- **Cross-company interpretation**: Intel's weak outlook does not disprove agent-driven CPU demand, and AMD's strong outlook does not prove modularity alone caused the difference. Product competitiveness, accelerator revenue, customer mix, and supply execution all contribute. This source supports differentiated business analysis, not a valuation-based buy/sell conclusion.
