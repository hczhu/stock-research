- ## Neil Movva (Sail Research) on Invest Like The Best: the throughput bet and the scavenger strategy
	- **Source**: *Invest Like The Best*, Patrick O'Shaughnessy with **Neil Movva**, founder of **Sail Research** — a "token factory" serving open-weight models with the stated goal of the lowest cost per token in the industry, plus "sail boxes," long-running agent VMs. Movva previously worked on GPU kernels at NVIDIA.
	- **Bias flag, which applies to every claim below**: he is a founder describing his own book. **Each contrarian position he takes is also the position that makes his company valuable** — background agents over chatbots, throughput over latency, non-NVIDIA silicon, small distributed data centers, cheap intermittent power. The technical reasoning is unusually specific and checkable; the market forecasts are self-serving by construction.
	- **Thesis**: One prediction drives everything — that inference shifts from **~50/50 background-versus-realtime today to 90/10 in favour of background**. If that holds, the entire optimization target flips from latency to throughput, and a cascade follows: **NVLink stops being mandatory, non-NVIDIA silicon becomes viable, gigawatt campuses stop being necessary for inference, and 95%-uptime intermittent power becomes a buyable asset instead of a disqualifying one.**
- ## The latency-throughput trade-off, and why it is structural
	- **The GPU is a throughput machine** — happiest given a large batch to chew through at peak compute utilization. But AI usage has been chatbot-shaped, so the whole serving industry optimized for **latency**, pulled that direction by one important customer (Cursor is named).
	- **The trade-off cannot be engineered away**: batching more users together raises aggregate throughput but means any individual request spends longer on the GPU. Movva's analogy is the bus versus the private car — the bus serves more people by taking a slower path with stops.
	- **The concrete cost of latency optimization**: sharding one matrix multiply across 8 GPUs via NVLink gives **sublinear scaling — roughly 4–5× the speed for 8× the hardware**, because of communication overhead and less efficient work per smaller tile. His conclusion: **"NVLink is mandatory for low-latency inference"** — and therefore optional for him.
	- **That is the whole arbitrage.** If you do not need NVLink, you can buy chips that are strong on raw compute and weak on interconnect, and use **expert or pipeline parallelism** instead of tensor parallelism. His stated objective function is **flops per dollar per hour**, not interconnect quality. *"There are no bad chips, there's no bad pricing. I will make any chip work at the right price."*
	- Supporting evidence for the shift: test-time compute scaling was theorized ~2 years ago but only became bettable with **Opus 4.5**, the first model "at all suitable for longer-horizon tasks." Agents now run **about an hour** at a stretch, not days. **Background workloads are unbounded** because no human attention is in the loop — the constraint on real-time token consumption is the user.
- ## Cerebras and Groq — the sharpest competitive call in the conversation
	- **The memory physics, stated cleanly**: SRAM is built on the logic die and is fast but area-expensive — a full Blackwell-sized 800mm² die of SRAM would hold only single-digit gigabytes, and Blackwell's logic die carries roughly **500 MB**. DRAM is capacitor-based, must refresh every ~50ms, and is far denser — Blackwell pairs with roughly **298 GB of HBM**, about **600× the capacity** at proportionally lower bandwidth. Cerebras WSE-3 reaches **~21 PB/s** against roughly **10 TB/s** for Blackwell HBM — **~2,000× the bandwidth**.
	- **His verdict: they are accelerators, not GPU replacements.** *"You want off-chip memory for capacity, and on-chip memory for speed — we want to hybridize these two things."*
	- **The binding constraint is the KV cache**, and this is the part worth keeping: model weights are static and can be held brilliantly in SRAM, but **the KV cache grows dynamically with users and context length, and you cannot know in advance how much you need**. It frequently exceeds the size of the weights themselves.
	- **"The original sin of Transformers"** — the architecture juxtaposes a fundamentally **memory-bound** layer (attention) directly against a **compute-bound** layer (the MLP), and no single chip is good at both. **The implied optimal split is MLP on the SRAM part, attention on the GPU**, which he believes is what the NVIDIA–Groq arrangement is doing.
	- **This is a sharper, more mechanical version of the conclusion in [[2026-08-09-semianalysis-tilert-inferencex-gpu-vs-dataflow-asic]]** — that software can approach but not raise the HBM roofline, and that dataflow silicon's advantage is real but bounded. Here the bound is named specifically: **KV cache capacity**.
- ## The scavenger strategy — the genuinely original investment idea
	- **The premise is a supply observation**: data centers were built for **training**, and training is a superset workload over inference — *"you can make any training cluster work for inference, but maybe not vice versa."* There is a **dis-economy of scale**: concentrating 100,000 GPUs in one site is far harder than 10,000, which is harder than 1,000.
	- **His scarcity ladder for US sites**: a gigawatt data center is *"basically impossible"* to build easily; **100 MW is increasingly hard; 10 MW is on the edge of possible; 1 MW is plentiful.** The consequence: *"there's this incredible floor on the market where you can find lots of aggregate power, but it will not be concentrated."*
	- **Liquid cooling makes the small unit workable** — **1 MW of compute now fits in roughly 8 racks**, each about the size of a refrigerator, versus a warehouse-scale data hall previously.
	- **He then buys the assets nobody else wants**, and this is the part that is a real arbitrage rather than a preference:
		- **No power redundancy, no backup diesel, single power source, one fiber trench, no failover SLAs.** He expects some sites to run at **95% uptime** and says *"I'm that first buyer."*
		- **His utility function is linear across 95/98/99% uptime**, because a robust control plane moves workloads and a long-running agent tolerates one turn taking an extra 1–10 minutes. The customer proposition is explicit: *"our average throughput is going to be very competitive, but our P99 tail latency is not going to be controlled. In return, I'll give you unbeatable economics."*
		- **He would take 80% uptime at the right price** — which makes **solar and wind viable**, since intermittency stops being disqualifying if you can model the weather and shift workloads. *"My chips are cheap enough, I don't mind the capital cost of having idle chips."*
	- **The stated objective is to avoid competing at all**: *"I do not want to be bidding against Anthropic or OpenAI… I want to use the supply that they don't find legible today. I'm never going to get concentrated supply. I will always get aggregate supply."* The framing is **mini-mills versus monolithic steel plants.**
	- **Why this matters beyond one company**: it identifies a **bid for stranded small power and low-bin silicon that did not previously exist**, and it is the direct counterpoint to the gigawatt-campus model. It also sits directly against the world in [[2026-08-29-dylan-patel-dwarkesh-revenue-per-megawatt-compute-centralization]] — Patel argues anyone can make money at \$10–15M/MW and that the labs will outbid everyone to \$25–50M/MW. **Movva is the "anyone," and his answer to being outbid is to buy assets the labs cannot use.**
- ## The contrarian hardware claims — the ones most worth checking
	- **Performance per watt has stalled at the logic level.** *"If you compare Hopper to Blackwell to Rubin like-for-like, the performance per watt of a BF16 multiply hasn't improved all that much"* — and the same for **TSMC 5nm → 4 → 3 → 2**, where *"performance per watt doesn't change a dramatic amount."*
		- **If true, two consequences follow.** First, **generational value is coming from memory, interconnect, and packaging rather than logic scaling** — which reframes where the margin sits in the supply chain. Second, his explicit geopolitical inference: losing TSMC *"wouldn't be that bad"* — a supply shock, but **Intel is "at worst maybe 2× worse performance per watt," so "the gap is far smaller than the chip-war dialogue makes out."** That is a materially non-consensus position and the single claim in the episode most worth independently verifying.
	- **Achieved utilization is power-limited, not software-limited.** Large matmuls run at **70–80% of peak**, and he attributes the shortfall to **power throttling and thermals** — *"the way NVIDIA quotes peak flops is optimistic."*
	- **The biggest inefficiency is orchestration, not silicon.** He stack-ranks: compute scaling is already efficient (frontier MoE models activate close to **1%** of experts); **attention and KV-cache memory use is bad** — *"we're storing many kilobytes per token… probably off by an order of magnitude or two,"* with DeepSeek making order-of-magnitude compression progress annually. But the largest waste is that **"we don't marshal our compute effectively at all"** — of roughly **5 million Blackwell chips shipping this year**, a great many *"sit in warehouses or in private pools allocated to a specific customer"* and go unused. He notes xAI is mocked for cluster utilization while *"the reality for the rest of the world is far worse."*
		- **Read-through**: if utilization across the installed base is genuinely poor, **effective compute supply is materially larger than installed capacity implies**, and orchestration is a real business rather than a feature.
	- **The fab insight worth keeping** is about **process corners**: TSMC works hard to tighten the spread between the worst and best chip off the line, which costs time and money. Movva argues **he would happily take the worst bin at a discount** — *"maybe I'm willing to take a lot more rejects"* — and that his real goal is to expand cheap power enough to give low-bin silicon a home.
- ## HBM as the bottleneck, and the consumer read-through
	- **His advice to chip entrepreneurs is a clean four-bottleneck framework**: **TSMC wafer capacity, HBM capacity, advanced packaging, and power.** *"It's all arbitrage at the end of the day… NVIDIA isn't perfect, they're just really well balanced. You want to be spiky."*
	- **The bottleneck he would attack is HBM**, because *"there's no easy way to bring on a lot more fabs of memory… boards don't love huge capex for cyclical. They've been burned many times."*
	- **The consumer consequence he states plainly**: the system will route around the shortage by making everything else more expensive — **"iPhones will cut their memory. iPhones are going to go up in price. We're just going to deal with it."** That is an independent, first-principles arrival at the same conclusion documented in [[AAPL-2026-Q2]] and the second round of price increases in [[2026-08-28-stratechery-mac-refresh-memory-pricing-and-jalapeno]].
	- **His own R&D bet follows from it**: custom silicon that **sidesteps HBM by offloading the KV cache to flash**, with the open question being *"what do we have to change about the model architecture to make offloading KV cache to flash work at a much greater level?"* — designed around serving at **1–10 tokens per second**. This is the same **memory-tiering** direction as the Engram/PLE work in [[2026-08-26-ainews-nvidia-q2-hf-confirmed-glm53-flash-economics]], applied to the dynamic rather than static half of memory.
- ## Why he thinks this is not the dot-com bubble
	- The setup was the observation that **semiconductors have gone from 2–4% of S&P 500 market cap historically to ~19–21%**, and that investors expect reversion.
	- **His rebuttal is a distinction worth adopting regardless of whether you share his conclusion**: the late-1990s networking buildout was **speculative** — capacity built against anticipated demand that never arrived. Token consumption is not. *"You don't hoard tokens, you use them immediately."*
	- **He extends it to a distinction within the AI cycle itself**: the 2023–24 Hopper crunch was **training-oriented spend, and training is inherently speculative**. *"I do think inference spend monotonically increases. There's no speculation on inference."*
	- **How to use it**: this is the cleanest available framing for separating durable from speculative AI demand — **track the training/inference mix of incremental capex**, because the two have different reversion risk. It also cuts against Patel's expectation that labs will shift compute *away* from inference toward training, which would move spend back toward the speculative category.
- ## Models, data, and the erosion of the frontier premium
	- **On the labs**: *"the labs pay an immense premium to be three to six months ahead… probably still worth it."* But he doubts its durability — **enterprise deployments do not move at three-to-six-month speed**, with many still on older model versions. That matches the slow version-migration evidence in the Ramp AI Index data already on file.
	- **On distillation, he pushes back on the theft framing** with an argument I have not seen elsewhere: an increasingly large share of artifacts on the internet is already AI-generated. *"What percentage of GitHub repos created in the last year do we think were created by Claude Code?"* He would not be surprised if a frontier-class model could be trained **only on good open-source code on GitHub**. Hence **"latent distillation"** is unavoidable: *"I don't think it's fundamentally possible to prevent the diffusion of information or model capabilities. It will happen. The question is just how fast."*
	- **On data**: *"the internet was a one-time subsidy"* — roughly **30 trillion tokens** of high-quality text, **300 trillion** on a looser definition, and models have seen it repeatedly. **Random human preference data is now worthless** because the median served model exceeds the median human rater; **expert preference** is what has value. The next phase is **RL environment gyms**, and *"frontier labs don't spend that much on data now, they spend a lot more on environments."* Consistent with [[2026-08-15-beren-scaling-is-subtler-than-it-seems]].
	- **His AGI recipe is a one-liner worth recording**: keep **stacking specialized intelligences until there are no gaps left**, with the sole requirement that each task be **verifiable with a self-grading system**. Non-verifiable domains — taste, writing quality, art — he explicitly cedes.
	- **On his own moat**: candidly, the software edge erodes. *"The rising tide of Mythos or GPT-5.6 lifts all boats… we're all downstream of the frontier."* Kernels are now designed on a whiteboard and implemented by models. **He is telling you the software layer is not the durable advantage — the supply-side scavenging is.**
- ## Two structural observations about NVIDIA
	- **Why NVIDIA rations rather than clears the market on price**: it sees that letting the deepest pockets buy everything *"maybe hurts them in the long term if the customer ends up accruing a lot of power."* **Compute is power**, so allocation is strategic, and relationships and financial backing gate access more than price does.
	- **Why NVIDIA does not sell tokens or run its own neocloud**: *"Jensen is really good at making his friends billionaires."* Having made CoreWeave a many-billion-dollar company, he wants **a diverse community of neoclouds and inference providers all jockeying to create demand**, so that if any one defects to AMD or vertically integrates, three more are waiting. *"It's great to have competition amongst his buyers."* **The fragmentation of the buyer base is deliberate policy, not market structure.**
	- On AMD: good chips that people cannot program well — *"that's music to my ears"* — though he concedes Meta and OpenAI have bought heavily and supply is increasingly allocated. The long tail (SambaNova, d-Matrix and others) is gated by **TSMC wafer allocation**, not design.
- ## The demand arithmetic, and what to distrust
	- **The cost target, made concrete**: a trillion tokens at frontier pricing costs roughly **\$5 million** today. To make a trillion tokens per person per day plausible requires **three to six orders of magnitude** of cost reduction — three gets you to **~\$5,000**, six to **~\$5**. He claims that for some model sizes, jobs measured in the **\$10,000s** are already reachable, and that a definitive answer to a scientific question is heading from millions toward *"hundreds or even tens of dollars."*
	- **What I would treat as unproven**: the **90/10 background split** is the load-bearing forecast and is not falsifiable near-term; the **95%-uptime customer tolerance** is asserted, not demonstrated at scale; and the **perf-per-watt stagnation** claim, while the most interesting item here, is stated without data and contradicts the marketing of every vendor involved.
	- **What survives the discount**: the throughput/latency trade-off and its NVLink consequence, the KV-cache constraint on SRAM architectures, the concentration ladder for US power sites, the four supply-chain bottlenecks, the training-versus-inference speculation distinction, and the observation that **a large share of installed GPUs sit idle** — each is checkable, and each holds independently of whether Sail succeeds.
