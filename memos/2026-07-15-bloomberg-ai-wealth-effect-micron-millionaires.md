- ## Bloomberg: the AI wealth effect and the Micron millionaires
	- **Source**: Bloomberg Businessweek, "AI Has Supercharged the Wealth Effect. Just Ask the Micron Millionaires," Stacey Vanek Smith, July 15, 2026 (August 1, 2026 issue). Economist commentary from Oxford Economics (Bernard Yaros), Harvard (Karen Dynan), and Bloomberg Economics (Jamie Rush). The article carries a published correction to the S&P 500 market-cap figure.
	- **Thesis**: The AI equity rally has become a **macro transmission channel**, not just a market event — roughly a third of post-Covid consumer-spending growth is attributed to the wealth effect, with US tech gains alone worth ~\$250B of household spending in one year. The investable asymmetry is that the same channel runs in reverse with **loss aversion amplifying it**: Bloomberg Economics models a 20% S&P drawdown erasing **\$1.6T of global GDP** and pushing the US "to the edge of recession."

- ## The quantified wealth-effect chain
	- | Link in the chain | Figure |
	  |----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
	  | Marginal propensity to consume from stock wealth | **~5¢ of spending per \$1 of portfolio gain** |
	  | S&P 500 market value added since July 2025 | **>\$12 trillion** (figure carries a published correction) |
	  | Share of post-Covid consumer spending growth from wealth effect | **~one-third** (Oxford Economics) |
	  | Additional household spending from US tech gains, 12 months to Aug 31, 2025 | **~\$250 billion** |
	  | Modeled global GDP loss on a 20% S&P decline | **\$1.6 trillion**, US "to the edge of recession" |
	- The 20% drawdown scenario is explicitly benchmarked as **"approximately half the size of the correction in the dot-com crash"** — i.e. this is not a tail case, it is a moderate one.

- ## Why the channel is structurally wider than in past rallies
	- **Stock ownership has broadened**: **58% of US adults own stock** (Gallup, April 2026), up from **52% a decade ago**.
	- **Equities are a record share of household balance sheets**: ~**46% of US households' financial assets**, the **highest proportion ever recorded in Federal Reserve data**. This is the single most monitorable structural datapoint in the piece — it sets the gearing between index moves and consumption.
	- **Demographics compound it**: an aging population means more retirees living off portfolios, which Yaros argues makes consumer spending **permanently more market-sensitive** going forward. Boomers also benefit from housing — **owners' equity in real estate up ~83% since the start of the pandemic**.
	- **Foreign ownership is at a record**: foreigners hold **18% of the US market, the highest share ever measured**, which is the mechanism by which a US AI drawdown transmits globally rather than staying domestic.
	- Dynan's framing is the key macro point: *"The stock market gains are a major driver of the surprising momentum we are seeing in economic activity. The other traditional drivers of spending, like real incomes and consumer sentiment, are at fairly unimpressive levels."* Consumption strength is **not** being carried by wages.

- ## The asymmetry that makes this a risk rather than a cushion
	- **Loss aversion**: Rush — *"When they lose money, they feel it harder than when they get a windfall. There's lots of evidence on that."* The downside coefficient is larger than the 5¢ upside rule of thumb.
	- **Narrow base, wide effect**: "In an economy where a narrower segment of the population has been driving a bigger share of spending, the effects of a consumer retrenchment would be large." Broadening ownership raises the *number* of affected households while concentration of *spending* raises the severity.
	- **Paper vs. realized**: the bulk of the gains sit in 401(k)s and retirement accounts that are "cumbersome or expensive to cash out," and windfalls "could vanish overnight." Yaros' concession is that this is largely a **psychological** channel — which cuts both ways and is why sentiment can reverse faster than balance sheets.
	- Yaros' summary formulation is worth keeping: *"the stock market is not the economy. But it's also not **not** the economy"* — and that second "not" is getting bigger.

- ## The Micron case study — how it shows up on the ground
	- **Micron stock up ~700% over the past year.** Boise-headquartered, one of Idaho's largest private employers, with **employee share discounts and stock options** — so the gain propagates directly into local household balance sheets rather than staying with institutional holders.
	- Reported local spending pattern is **discretionary and durable-goods-weighted**: \$80,000–100,000 trucks, cabins and second homes, boats, ski passes, charitable donations to local colleges, private jet and helicopter bookings (Silverhawk Aviation Academy reports strong demand, including a \$1,450 "James Bond" helicopter date night).
	- One client hit his **retirement savings goal decades ahead of schedule** — the labor-supply implication (earlier retirement) is a second-order effect the article gestures at without quantifying.
	- **Why this matters beyond color**: it is a clean, observable transmission case of AI-driven equity gains converting into regional real-economy demand through an employee-shareholder base. Useful as a template for other single-employer metros with concentrated AI-linked equity exposure.

- ## What to watch
	- The **equity share of household financial assets** (Fed data) — currently at a record ~46%; this is the gearing ratio for the whole channel.
	- Whether **real income and consumer sentiment** recover enough to carry spending independently, which would reduce the market dependency Dynan describes.
	- **Foreign ownership share** of the US market (record 18%) as the global-transmission gauge.
	- Discretionary and big-ticket categories in AI-employer metros (Boise for [[$MU]]) as a leading regional indicator of the effect running in either direction.
