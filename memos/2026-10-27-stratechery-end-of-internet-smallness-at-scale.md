- ## The End of the Internet: Smallness at Scale
	- **Source**: Ben Thompson, “The End of the Internet,” Stratechery, October 26, 2026; user-provided article text. This memo summarizes the author's reasoning, not independently verified security reports or market forecasts.
	- **Thesis**: Openness creates abundance, but abundance undermines producer economics, community trust, and security. Thompson argues that the next opportunity is not another mass-audience destination: AI makes software for individuals and small trusted groups economically feasible, potentially expanding total demand while fragmenting it into smaller markets.
	- **Meaning of the title**: The claimed ending concerns the open, independently hosted, public-facing Internet as a practical default—not the disappearance of Internet connectivity or online business.

- ## Five Reasoning Paths
	- | Domain | Causal chain | Author's conclusion |
	  | --- | --- | --- |
	  | Publishing | Free distribution → abundant content → discovery becomes scarce → Google captures attention and valuable ad inventory → undifferentiated publishers face intense competition | Distinctive voices can monetize a smaller loyal audience through subscriptions |
	  | Social media | Easy participation → overwhelming content and public exposure → algorithmic ranking and defensive behavior → engagement-driven entertainment replaces community | Genuine social interaction migrates to small, high-trust group chats |
	  | Security | Human-written software contains latent flaws → AI expands attackers' testing and exploitation capacity → exposed services need continuous professional defense | Casual self-hosting becomes less viable; managed infrastructure becomes more important |
	  | Software creation | High upfront development cost once required large audiences → AI reduces that cost → previously uneconomic personal and team tools become feasible | Software's market expands beyond standardized products sold to many customers |
	  | Demand formation | Less effort spent on routine cognitive work → people discover previously unaffordable or unarticulated needs → many small groups build tools around those needs | Cheaper intelligence may create new demand, not merely replace existing labor |

- ## Scarcity Moves from Distribution to Differentiation and Trust
	- **Publishing's escape route is low substitutability.** Thompson's subscription argument is not that any blogger can charge; it is that readers will pay for a voice or expertise they cannot easily replace. Smaller reach is the trade-off for higher value per committed reader.
	- **Social platforms and social relationships diverge.** Public feeds can remain large entertainment businesses even as meaningful interaction moves elsewhere. The article does not establish that migration to group chats causes a decline in platform revenue.
	- **AI could weaken the publisher-discovery bargain further.** Thompson argues that AI consumes content and attention without returning the referral traffic that search historically supplied. The pressure is therefore on both content monetization and customer acquisition; no traffic-loss estimate is provided.

- ## Security Pushes Hosting Toward Specialists
	- **Concrete example:** Thompson says Pressable, Automattic's managed WordPress provider, patched Stratechery before disclosure of a critical PHP vulnerability. His inference is that operating public software safely increasingly requires capabilities beyond those of an individual publisher.
	- **The same technology helps both sides.** He expects an initial escalation in discovered vulnerabilities, followed by better software and continuous agent-based defense. That improvement is a prediction, not evidence that AI-generated code is inherently secure.
	- **Analytical boundary:** A smaller audience is not itself a security control. Private tools still need authentication, permissions, patching, and secure handling of data; “anyone running a server is doomed” is stronger than the evidence presented.

- ## AI Changes the Minimum Economic Audience for Software
	- **The key change is upfront cost, not copying cost.** Software already had low marginal distribution cost. Reducing the effort to create it allows projects to justify themselves with one user or one team's benefit instead of a large paying customer base.
	- **First-hand examples:** Stratechery built private tools for podcast production, task tracking, and calendar management. Thompson also built personal tools for his home and sports viewing. These support the existence of previously unmet use cases, but do not quantify savings, reliability, or adoption beyond his experience.
	- **More software does not necessarily mean more packaged-software revenue.** As an inference, custom tools could replace some narrow SaaS purchases while increasing consumption of AI development tools, managed execution, and security services. The article does not establish who captures that spending or at what margin.
	- **Two layers may move in opposite directions — inference:** Applications and workflows can become more fragmented and personal while the infrastructure needed to build and secure them concentrates in specialist platforms. Local customization and infrastructure scale are not mutually exclusive.

- ## From Labor Substitution to New Demand
	- Drawing on Venkatesh Rao's “Cognitive Mercantilism,” Thompson contrasts a fixed-demand view of AI with a demand-expansion view: freeing people from routine cognitive work may reveal new wants, just as productivity gains in agriculture enabled new occupations and activities.
	- The article's agricultural analogy is a possibility, not a measured forecast. Its illustrative percentages about future cognitive activity should not be treated as market-sizing data.
	- **The final reasoning step:** Small groups have needs that standardized global products often overlook → cheaper creation makes those needs addressable → many individually small markets can collectively support substantial activity. Whether that activity becomes monetizable revenue, rather than unpaid personal utility, remains unresolved.
