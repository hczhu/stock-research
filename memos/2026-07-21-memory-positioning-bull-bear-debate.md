		- [Micron's fiscal Q3 2026 earnings call](https://investors.micron.com/static-files/631b1a32-5537-46ae-8f40-82e42fc79dfe) said DRAM and NAND demand significantly exceeds supply and tight conditions should persist beyond calendar 2027.
		- Data-center revenue exceeded $25B in the quarter, which management described as an annualized run rate above $100B.
		- Data-center SSD revenue exceeded $5B and more than doubled sequentially.
		- Micron had signed **16 strategic customer agreements**, evidence that multi-year contracting is becoming material to its business model.
	- **Anthropic linkage**
		- [Micron and Anthropic announced a strategic agreement](https://investors.micron.com/news-releases/news-release-details/micron-and-anthropic-announce-strategic-agreement-scale-next) spanning memory and storage architecture, supply and demand, enterprise Claude adoption inside Micron, and a Micron investment in Anthropic.
		- The agreement supports the screenshot's claim that frontier-model demand is reaching memory suppliers through direct strategic relationships rather than only anonymous spot purchases.
		- It does not disclose committed volumes, prices, minimum purchases, or cancellation rights, so its financial value cannot yet be modeled precisely.
	- **SK hynix supply response**
		- SK hynix announced **KRW 100T** of investment in the Cheongju region, including the M17 NAND fab and an advanced-packaging facility expected by the end of 2027.
		- This supports both sides: today's shortage is severe enough to justify extraordinary investment, but capacity additions eventually create supply-response risk.
	- **Industry duration**
		- Gartner has described DRAM and NAND shortages extending into the second half of 2027, while IDC sees supply-demand imbalance persisting beyond 2027 in key segments.
		- These forecasts support “higher for longer” but remain forecasts; customer elasticity and accelerated supply can change the path.

- ## Kimi K3 as a Memory and Systems Signal
	- [Kimi K3's public technical blog](https://www.kimi.com/en/blog/kimi-k3) describes:
		- **2.8T total parameters**.
		- A mixture-of-experts design activating **16 of 896 experts**.
		- A **1M-token context window**.
		- Recommended deployment on supernodes with **64 or more accelerators**.
		- MXFP4 weights and MXFP8 activations, quantization-aware training, expert parallelism, and prefix-cache changes.
	- These features pull on multiple infrastructure layers:
		- HBM and accelerator-local memory for active weights and inference state.
		- DRAM for CPU-side serving, orchestration, and large context workloads.
		- High-bandwidth networking for expert parallelism across large accelerator domains.
		- NAND and SSD capacity for model checkpoints, training data, cache, and deployment artifacts.
		- Advanced packaging, substrates, power delivery, and cooling.
	- Demand reportedly overwhelmed Kimi's service capacity shortly after launch, causing a temporary suspension of new subscriptions. This is evidence of constrained deployed compute, not proof that every semiconductor input is individually scarce.
	- **Important correction**: the screenshot refers to a Kimi K3 technical report, but the launch blog said fuller technical details would accompany a forthcoming report. The public evidence available at the screenshot date was therefore incomplete.

- ## Long-Term Agreements: Structural Change or Cycle Management?
	- LTAs can reduce memory volatility by specifying:
		- Minimum purchase volumes.
		- Capacity reservation or take-or-pay commitments.
		- Price floors, bands, indexation, or periodic renegotiation.
		- Product-generation roadmaps and qualification schedules.
		- Prepayments or customer funding for capacity.
	- They do not automatically eliminate cyclicality:
		- Customers can negotiate lower prices when supply loosens.
		- Volume commitments may contain flexibility bands or cancellation clauses.
		- Customer concentration can replace spot-price risk with counterparty risk.
		- Fixed prices can hurt suppliers if costs rise or product mix shifts unexpectedly.
		- Long contracts signed at peak assumptions can lead to disputes, inventory accumulation, or delayed deliveries.
	- The correct test is the **downcycle margin floor**:
		- Do realized prices remain above cash cost and depreciation?
		- Do customers take committed volumes when end demand weakens?
		- Are contract renewals durable after scarcity disappears?
		- Do suppliers maintain discipline rather than adding excess wafer capacity?

- ## The 2027–2028 Capex Debate
	- **Bull view**: 2027 budgets underestimate agentic workloads, larger models, enterprise verticalization, and memory content per accelerator or server.
	- **Bear view**: 2027 spending is already consensus, while 2028 depends on realized AI revenue, power delivery, utilization, and the ability of model labs to finance commitments.
	- Budget announcements and memory shipments are separated by a physical conversion funnel:
		- Capital authorization.
		- Land, power, and permitting.
		- Data-center shell and electrical equipment.
		- Accelerator, memory, and networking allocation.
		- System integration and energization.
		- Customer workload deployment and utilization.
	- Power constraints can be bullish for long-term scarcity but bearish for near-term shipments: demand exists, yet servers cannot be installed until sites energize.

- ## Crowding, TRS Restrictions, and China Flow Risk
	- China's restrictions on offshore total-return swaps matter because TRS had allowed mainland investors to obtain foreign-equity exposure without a direct cross-border asset purchase.
	- When access tightens, funds may:
		- Sell broader domestic semiconductor baskets to release collateral.
		- Concentrate in locally accessible DRAM or memory-themed ETFs.
		- Reduce gross exposure and leverage.
		- Express overseas views through less precise domestic proxies.
	- This can produce strong ETF inflows without a change in global memory demand.
	- Crowded leveraged positions create nonlinear downside: falling prices trigger margin calls, forced ETF selling, widening discounts, and contagion to unrelated semiconductor holdings.
	- **Investor test**: separate memory-equity performance driven by earnings revisions from performance driven by ETF creation, margin rules, and cross-border access changes.

- ## Key Tests for the Thesis
	- Contract DRAM and NAND prices versus spot prices through the fourth quarter and into 2027.
	- Realized gross-margin floors at Micron, SK hynix, and Samsung as new capacity starts production.
	- Terms, committed volumes, pricing mechanisms, and renewal rates for strategic customer agreements and LTAs.
	- 2028 capex guidance from hyperscalers and model labs, not only 2027 budgets.
	- Funded, energized, and deployed AI capacity versus announced spending.
	- Codex and Claude Code paid usage, revenue retention, and expansion beyond software development.
	- Memory content per accelerator, CPU, AI server, and inference token after quantization and architectural efficiency gains.
	- China DRAM expansion, government intervention, and changes in leveraged ETF or TRS positioning.
	- Buyback and dividend capacity after funding fabs, packaging, cleanrooms, and working capital.

- ## Key Takeaways
	- The screenshot captures a market with strong fundamentals but no agreement on whether the cycle has become structural.
	- Shanghai bullishness appears partly driven by TRS and margin mechanics, making it a weaker fundamental signal than supplier contracts or end-customer deployments.
	- Micron's official guidance and strategic agreements support tightness beyond 2027, while Kimi K3 demonstrates how model scale pulls memory, networking, storage, packaging, power, and cooling together.
	- Bears ask the correct valuation question: long-term agreements signed during scarcity do not prove a durable earnings floor until the next downturn.
	- The most useful framework is to track three separate clocks—AI demand, physical data-center deployment, and memory capacity additions—because stocks can peak when those clocks diverge even if the long-term AI thesis remains intact.
