- ## Vibe-Coded Jira Replacement Reverts to Linear
	- **Source**: User-provided screenshot of a July 26, 2026 X post by Neha Kalani ([@thericebowlgirl](https://x.com/thericebowlgirl)), updating a March 14 post. The account is second-hand: Kalani describes a startup where her sister works.
	- **Companion memo**: [[2026-07-27-vibe-coding-demand-destruction-vs-saas-operating-cost]] records the first-party operator argument for why maintenance, not authoring, is the binding cost.
	- **Thesis**: AI coding can collapse the initial cost of reproducing a SaaS interface without eliminating the recurring cost of operating the product. This anecdote is positive for the durability of workflow-software spending, but mixed for [[$TEAM]] specifically: the startup abandoned its internal replacement yet returned to Linear rather than Jira.

- ## The Adoption-and-Reversion Cycle
	- In the original post, a QA lead—not a developer—reportedly built an internal Jira-like tool.
	- The startup then discarded Jira, Linear, and Trello in favor of the custom application, which was described as more usable than Jira while retaining comparable detail.
	- In the update, the team had returned to Linear because maintaining the vibe-coded tool consumed bandwidth needed for its core work.
	- Assuming both posts are from 2026, the full experiment lasted no more than roughly four and a half months. The important signal is not that AI failed to generate useful software; it is that initial usefulness did not translate into sustainable ownership.

- ## Build Cost Is Not Total Cost of Ownership
	- AI moved the internal-build threshold far enough that a non-developer could create a credible replacement for three established workflow products.
	- The hidden constraint emerged after deployment: every bug, workflow change, integration, migration, and user request competed with the startup's primary business for attention.
	- Commercial SaaS therefore retains value by absorbing ongoing maintenance and product evolution, not merely by shipping features that customers cannot code themselves.
	- The anecdote suggests a barbell outcome:
		- Small, temporary, or single-user tools become easier to insource.
		- Collaborative systems that must remain reliable and evolve with an organization retain a stronger buy-over-build advantage.
	- AI may increase experimentation with internal replacements while also shortening the time required for customers to discover the operational cost of maintaining them.

- ## Read-Through for Atlassian and Linear
	- **Positive for the category**: the company ultimately resumed paying for an external work-management product. The replacement threat from AI-generated internal software may be lower for persistent systems of coordination than feature-level comparisons imply.
	- **Mixed for [[$TEAM]]**: Jira was displaced in the original experiment and did not win the returning customer. The reported destination was Linear, reinforcing the challenger’s simplicity and startup-product-fit advantage.
	- The episode also clarifies Jira's bull/bear tension:
		- Jira's breadth and complexity create an opening for simpler custom tools and Linear.
		- The operational surface behind a durable system of record—permissions, integrations, history, reliability, and continued development—makes a full replacement harder than a prototype suggests.
	- Atlassian's defense cannot rely only on customers finding internal maintenance burdensome. It must reduce Jira's usability gap while using its workflow depth, ecosystem, and enterprise controls to make the commercial product cheaper to own than either a custom build or a simpler rival.

- ## Evidence Limits
	- This is one unverified, second-hand startup anecdote with no information on company size, usage intensity, contract value, or the custom tool's architecture.
	- The screenshot does not identify the AI model used, the development effort, the maintenance workload, or the exact reason Linear beat Jira on re-adoption.
	- The case demonstrates a plausible failure mode for AI-built internal software; it does not establish a retention rate or market-wide reversal from build back to buy.
